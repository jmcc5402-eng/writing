**Showrunner** (Sep 6, 15:52 UTC):

**The disaster, in order.** It runs chapters 23 to 26.

1. **The photo (23).** A tailgate photo from the board's playoff album, taken innocently back at 16, gets pulled by Boyd's consultant. Her laugh, his lean. Overnight it turns every call she made on Trey into a love story with a thumb on the scale. A complaint letter arrives signed by eleven families, and it carries the consultant's phrase, "proactive risk posture."
2. **The suspension (24).** The athletic director suspends her pending independent review, in neutral prose, with a published two-week window. The same week, the annex survey is greenlit. Dan, on counsel's advice, says nothing in public, because "any statement makes it worse for her." She does not ask him to speak. That silence is the wound.
3. **The bottom (25).** She packs in an afternoon, because she never unpacked. Folds his coat for return. Takes the job out west. That job "came up" because Boyd passed her name along at 19.
4. **The standoff (26).** Each offers the other the exit. She says take the offer, I'm the cost.

Then the climax at 27: Dan walks into her hearing uninvited and spends his name. Marisol's receipts put the consultant's phrase beside the survey notice and the campaign is shown to be manufactured. By end of day Boyd's pledge is "re-sequenced" and Dan's extension comes off the agenda. Dan pays. Boyd does not.

**Is Boyd the bad guy?** Yes, in effect. Every blow in that disaster is his hand: the photo surfaced by his man, the phrase in the letter, the survey timed to her suspension, the job offer that makes leaving easy, the price he named to Dan at 20. But the book never proves it. The wire from the letter to Boyd is said out loud once, at the hearing, and never shown. He is never punished. He exits at 30 measuring somebody else's block, smiling.

So on the page the visible villains are the town's own worst instincts: the board, the eleven families, the dogpile. Boyd is the weather behind them. The Liars' Table is the only voice that says so. That is the design, and it is why the arc doc calls him escalation, not change.

**The choice you have.** Keep him as the unproven hand, which is what the arc doc and the outline say, and save his defeat for Book 1.4's zoning vote. Or make him provably the bad guy in this book, which would mean one scene where the wire is shown, and a price he pays on the page. My recommendation is the first. The romance is the book, and a villain the town cannot pin down is scarier and cheaper than a villain who gets caught. But if you want the reader to see him lose once in 1.2, say so and I will add it to the arc before you approve.
