**Showrunner** (Sep 7, 19:42 UTC):

Here's how a chapter gets made right now, in the order it happens.

**Before any chapter: the canon.** Each book has a bible, a chapter-by-chapter outline, and arc docs for both leads, the romance, and the antagonist. Every decision you make in chat or on a PR gets written into those docs the same day, with an ID, so nothing lives in anyone's memory. The romance has its own ladder, and a script checks it: the inside of each lead can only run one stage ahead of what's on the page, and no kiss or claim can spend what hasn't been earned.

**One chapter at a time, seven steps:**

1. **The card.** Before a word is drafted, I write "Where we are": the plot, each lead's arc, the romance, Boyd, the town, and the clock. As of today the plot paragraph also prices the clock. The card goes to you first. Drafting doesn't wait on your reply, but you see the map before the chapter.
2. **The brief.** The outline entry verbatim, the romance move named by stage, the scenes, one romance beat per scene, who loses what, the ending's register (UP or DOWN, alternating), the full naming list, and the bans and budgets.
3. **The blocking audit.** The continuity keeper reads the brief against every accepted page and every decision. It comes back PASS or BLOCK with an addendum that overrides the brief. Chapter 12's audit caught a Saturday practice that canon says can't exist, and the chapter got better for it.
4. **One drafter.** A single drafting agent writes the chapter from the brief, the addendum, the card, your taste sheet, and the accepted pages. It draws a variance card, a small emphasis like "plain sentences" or "one minor character's competence," so chapters don't come out of the same mold. It reports every new fact it put on the page.
5. **The panel.** A simulated romance reader judges it scene by scene: is she in every scene of his, does the want land as a sum, where does the reader skim, where does she put it down. PASS, PASS WITH FIXES, or FAIL. Fixes are exact line asks and I apply them.
6. **The lint.** A script checks the dialogue floor, line width, banned words and scaffolds, the furniture blacklist (no supper, no casseroles), the beverage rules, lead names, and stray placeholders.
7. **The PR.** One PR per chapter, the card on top, the strikeable defaults listed so you can strike by comment. You get the listening file. You merge or comment. Your comments get applied in a small follow-up PR and go into the taste sheet.

**After the merge: the fold.** The thread map gets the chapter's entry, the furniture registry gets every new prop and who calls whom what, the ladder gets its row, the changelog gets the edit log, and the state file points at the next chapter.

**The separate editorial passes** (developmental, line, continuity) exist as agents and run in that order when a wave of chapters is done, because each pass invalidates the one before. Right now, at one chapter a time, the audit and panel do most of that work up front.

**Decisions bigger than a chapter**, like the provider partner or today's stakes, go on their own decision PR so a chapter never quietly changes canon.

What I'd flag as the weak spots: the line-level pass is thin at the chapter cadence (the panel catches the worst tics, not all of them), and nothing yet re-reads chapters 1 through 12 as one run to check that the stakes and the romance actually accumulate. Both are worth a pass before the midpoint.

It's still on the books, and honestly, we drifted from it.

**What the rule says.** The drafting protocol makes a blind competition the default for any new chapter: three drafters, same brief, different variance cards, none of them seeing each other's work or the neighboring chapters. The panel and the editorial checks judge blind, you read one winner, and the runners-up's best beats get flagged for grafting. The pipeline's single-chapter rule kept that for new chapters and cut re-cuts to one drafter.

**What we've actually done.** Chapters 9 through 12 each had one drafter. That started when you sent chapter 9 back and I moved to one drafter, one fix, one PR to keep the correction loop tight. It's been working because the audit and the panel now catch most problems before and after the draft. But it's a deviation, and I should have said so.

**What the competition buys and costs.**
- Buys: choice at the moments that matter. Chapter 9's first draft would probably have lost to a rival on the "nothing woven in" problem before you ever saw it. Three drafts also spread the tics out. A tic can't copy between drafts that never read each other.
- Costs: three times the drafting, a judging step, and a risk you've seen before, where grafting the best beats from three drafts blurs the voice into a committee's. Your reading time doesn't change. You still get one file.

**My recommendation.** Bring it back selectively rather than for every chapter:

1. **Competition** for the set pieces where a wrong draft costs the most: 13 (the first heat scene), 15 (the kiss), 16 (the game), 20 (Boyd's price), 27 (the hearing), and the first chapter of any new register.
2. **One drafter** for the connecting chapters, where the brief and the audit already decide most of what the page does.
3. **No grafting by default.** A winner ships as written. A runner-up's beat gets in only as a named fix, so the voice stays one person's.
4. **The panel judges blind** and I tell you which draft won and in one line why, but you only read the winner.

If you want that, say so and I'll write it into the protocol and run chapter 13 as the first competition, since it's the film-room heat scene and worth three tries.
