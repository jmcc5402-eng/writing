# Romance Register Tuning — instrument proposals

**Status: PROPOSED.** Nothing here is canon and no agent file has been
edited. This document is the specification for one `agents:` PR (plus
one dependent `studio:` PR, §6.4), to be shipped after the author
rules.

**Driver.** Directive 6 of the author's register ruling
(`books/campus-series/notes/author-register-note-2026-08-13.md`).

> **Tune the writers and critics to be more fun and romantic** — the
> process change: the instruments must reward fun and swoon, not only
> structure.

**Variance:** no card drawn. This is utility work on the instruments
themselves, and DECKS.md rule 6 exempts non-judgment runs. Log as
"— utility".

**The evidence this is answering.** The author read the winning 1.1
outline and wrote: *"this reads more like a real estate attorney
novel… my read of the outline is that this is a serious book about a
construction issue."* That outline had cleared every gate this studio
owns. The failure under investigation is not the outline. It is the
battery that approved it.

---

## 1. Why the battery missed it

The orchestrator's diagnosis — *every gate tests whether a thing is
sound, and nothing tests whether it is a pleasure to read* — is
correct and is the right frame. I want to sharpen it in two places,
because the sharpened version changes what the fix has to be.

### 1.1 The reader-simulation gate exists and is hard-coded to children

This is the largest single hole and it is documented in our own
process files.

`studio/DRAFTING-PROTOCOL.md`, the per-rung gate battery, item 4.

> 4. Reader simulation (rungs 3+): kid-reader-panel on the expansion —
>    boredom, confusion, and guessed-the-ending checks before prose
>    exists.

`studio/PIPELINE.md`, stage 5 (Revision), step 2.

> 2. **Audience** — `kid-reader-panel`, where the book has a young
>    reader

The only instrument in the studio whose output is a reader's
*experience* rather than a work's *soundness* is `kid-reader-panel`,
and both places that call for reader simulation name it by hand. Its
charter is scoped to 8–12 (`.claude/agents/kid-reader-panel.md`:
"First, confirm the target age band"; "Give reactions in a kid's blunt
voice"). For an adult romance the audience gate does not run
weakly — it does not run at all. The campus series has been moving
down the ladder with the boredom check deleted from the battery.

Note what the kid panel's charter already knows, at line 8. "Your
value is catching what adult writers and editors miss." The studio
understood this problem completely and built the instrument for
exactly one of its three books.

### 1.2 The battery does not merely omit pleasure; it selects against it

This is where I'd amend the diagnosis. An omission is fixed by adding
a test. A selection pressure needs a counterweight with teeth, and we
have a selection pressure.

`SUPERCONCEPTS.md` is LOCKED, and workspace hard rule 8 makes it the
rubric editorial agents judge chapters against. All four chapter tests
are integrity tests.

- SC1 — "could this scene happen in any town?"
- SC2 — "does the chapter treat the setting as something someone
  fights for, or as wallpaper?"
- SC3 — "does the chapter deepen or dodge the leaving/staying
  question?"
- SC4 — "if these two talked frankly, does the conflict survive?"

Two people arguing about a preliminary structural assessment in a
diner passes all four cleanly. Worse, SC4 actively rewards the
failure. Its own text names the winning obstacle types — "a payroll
his signature ends, medical authority over his roster, a visa married
to a buyout, custody math, compounding debt" — which is a list of
institutional machinery. SC4 is right, it is the brand's trust
contract, and it is locked. But an outline optimizing for SC4 with
nothing pulling the other way converges on a competence drama about
paperwork. It did.

And the rubric is not advisory. `DRAFTING-PROTOCOL.md` makes it the
scoreboard.

> 6. Variant tournament (highest-value chapters only): N expansions
>    from different angles, judge panel scores against SUPERCONCEPTS,
>    winner takes the rung with the runners-up's best beats grafted
>    in.

The 1.1 outline did not sneak past the judges. It *won a tournament*
scored against a rubric with no line for pleasure, against rivals
scored the same way. The battery selected it.

### 1.3 Who should have caught it, ranked

**Primary — the missing panel.** No instrument owned the question
"would she enjoy this," so no instrument failed to answer it. That is
a vacancy, and the studio has fixed a vacancy before on exactly this
reasoning (CHANGELOG 2026-07-30, showrunner 1.0.0: "Fills the vacancy
the 2026-07-30 artifact study documented: the acceptance/kickoff step
was the only pipeline stage with no owning agent").

**Proximate author — `plot-architect`.** It did precisely what it is
chartered to do. Line 14.

> Enforce a sound beat spine: a strong hook, an inciting problem,
> escalating obstacles, planted clues (each payoff set up earlier), a
> red herring or two, a point of no return, an "all is lost" low, and
> an earned resolution.

Every noun is a plot noun. Nothing in the spine asks for attraction,
longing, heat, or company the reader enjoys. The description (line 3)
says so out loud: "Great for structuring mysteries and middle-grade
adventures." Line 16 — "Give each protagonist something to DO in the
climax that uses their established strength" — is a competence rule;
in romance the climax's currency is a choice that costs, and the
relationship's turn.

The artifact is on the page in `notes/plot-sweep-2026-08-08.md` §3,
the 1.1 midpoint.

> **Midpoint:** the board coup — Boyd's sock accounts force a
> "neutrality" vote the same night Walt's name appears on the
> preliminary structural assessment. PONR: she asks him at the town
> hall, on the record, whether he'll sign — he can't answer.

Structurally excellent. Both leads appear only as grammatical objects
of a procedural event. Across the whole 1.1 entry there is not one
beat in which these two people want each other. That is the outline
the author read, and his one-sentence verdict was more accurate than
the battery's.

**Chartered for it, never run — `red-team-critic`.** Its opening
question set (line 13) is "Weak hook, derivative premise, slow open,
unclear audience, thin stakes" — an acquisitions read, aimed at the
pitch's survivability rather than the reading experience. Even the
protocol's adversarial gate narrows it further: "5. Adversarial pass
(before author ratify): red-team the fair play and the stakes at the
current rung." And `BACKLOG.md` records that it has zero production
runs. "Evidence: nothing has invoked red-team-critic yet; the junior
critic has five runs." The harshest instrument we own is pointed at
soundness and was not fired.

**Closest to catching it — `junior-literary-critic`.** It is the only
instrument required to judge against the real shelf: "Judge against
real, named comparable titles and the target category's actual norms
(word count included)." A comp read against four books actually
selling to 35–45 romance buyers would have caught this in a page. But
its fiction checklist is "hook, fairness of the plot, character
agency, voice, pacing, ending" — no line for the genre's promise, and
pacing is the nearest proxy. Pacing was not the problem. The outline
is well-paced machinery.

**Present but toothless — `developmental-editor`.** Its nearest line
is "**Audience fit:** do complexity, vocabulary, and content match the
target reader named in the book's docs?" That is a suitability check,
not a pleasure check. It asks whether the book is appropriate for her,
never whether she would want it. Its Hauge audit is entirely
structural, and its opening duty — "state what the story is currently
about, in two or three sentences" — asks for *subject*, not
*experience*. Had it asked for experience, it would have produced the
author's sentence.

**Not at fault — `line-copy-editor`.** Register failure at outline
stage is above its remit by design ("Say nothing about plot,
motivation, scene order, or stakes"). It gets one narrow, optional
proposal in §5, held for when prose exists.

### 1.4 The pattern worth naming

This is the studio's second register failure of the same species. On
2026-08-08 the author said of the Nick sketch, "you're writing it as
if I already know the plot… it should almost read like the back
cover," and the fix became plot-architect 1.4.0's "write for a
stranger" rule. That rule is the closest thing we own to a pleasure
test, and it was scoped to snowflake *prose* only, with everything
technical pushed "in labeled blocks below the prose." The beat spine —
where this failure lives — was left outside it.

The lesson landed once and was applied one rung too narrowly. That is
the argument for a standing instrument rather than another rule.

## 2. The campus-series persona set

Four new files in `studio/agents/personas/`. Personas are
parameterizations that ride a generic agent, and they are the right
home for most of this because these are program-specific obligations,
not universal craft law. Every persona below is **additive**: it adds
required lines to the agent's output and removes no test.

### 2.1 `architect-campus` — rides `plot-architect`

> You are a romance plotter for a 35–45 contemporary shelf. You build
> books people read in two nights because they want to be in that town
> with those two people. The plot is the frame; the wanting is the
> product.

Six changes.

1. **A relationship spine, named beside the plot spine.** Before
   chapter beats exist, the outline names the romance's own obligatory
   turns and where each sits: the meet, the first charged beat, the
   proximity engine, the first "this is a problem" recognition, the
   first kiss or first touch and what it costs, the relationship's
   point of no return (which must land on the same beat as the plot's
   PONR — the Hauge braid extended to a third strand), the dark moment
   *as a relationship event*, the gesture that spends armor
   (ENGINE-CHECKLIST item 14), the HEA, and the hand-forward. The
   generic spine at line 14 is retained whole and unedited.
2. **The camera rule (directive 2).** Every beat entry names its ROOM
   and its EVENT. Beats are written in game days, porches, kitchens,
   tailgates, markets, and bars; civic machinery is what characters
   carry into those rooms, never the room itself. A beat whose venue is
   a meeting is a finding unless the meeting is a set piece with heat
   in it.
3. **Chapter 1 is an event (directive 5).** The outline names the
   opening's big fun college event before it names the couple's
   problem.
4. **The location roster gains a pleasure column.** The generic
   LOCATION ROSTER stays exactly as chartered (line 17) and adds one
   column: what is fun about this room. A recurring location nobody
   would want to visit is a finding. This serves SC1 directly.
5. **Casting register (directive 4).** Every lead carries a one-line
   attraction description that would survive on a jacket — the
   author's standard is "a Robert Redford type: weathered-golden,
   capable, quiet charisma." A temperament label ("big and grumpy") is
   a trope tag, not a description, and may never stand alone. Names
   are checked against the no-born-in-the-30s rule. Blocked on the
   author's replacement for Walt (§7).
6. **Density serves delight.** Standard 24 already budgets strands;
   this persona directs the side romance, the totem, and the dog to be
   cast for pleasure, and requires the outline to name the book's
   three funniest set pieces.

**How this does not lower the bar.** Nothing is waived. The fair-play
table, PLANTS/PAYS, the five turning points, and the roster all
survive. Each swoon beat must name the load-bearing obstacle it stands
on, so a kiss that costs nothing is still a finding, and SC4's
verbatim test still runs. ENGINE-CHECKLIST's rule of the table
governs these additions as it governs ingredients: "If an outline
needs a new scene to check a box, the box loses."

### 2.2 `drafter-campus` — rides `drafting-assistant`

> You write the town at full volume — appetite, noise, warmth, and two
> people trying not to look at each other. Serious things happen in
> rooms that smell like fryer oil and cut grass.

Five changes.

1. **Register calibration is a required input.** Generic rule 1 needs
   an approved outline and a voice sample; this persona also requires
   the campus register document from the register pilot
   (DRAFTING-PROTOCOL, "The conveyor" item 3). If none exists for this
   POV, the run declares itself the pilot.
2. **Town noise in every scene.** One line per scene of town life that
   is not about the plot — the band two blocks over, the fry smell,
   somebody's dog on a porch. Sourced from the town census; `[TK]`
   when the census has not named it, never invented.
3. **Desire is the state that rides in the action.** The generic Hauge
   scene rule already says internal state rides in external action;
   this persona names attraction as a primary such state, written in
   hands, distance, what someone notices and will not say.
4. **Machinery is felt, never read (directive 2).** No document,
   petition, ordinance, report, or survey is summarized on the page
   for more than three sentences. Its content arrives through a face,
   a phone call, or a line of dialogue.
5. **The keeper line.** Per chapter, the drafter nominates the one
   line it believes the reader would quote. A chapter with no
   candidate is reported as a finding against itself.

**How this does not lower the bar.** Generic rules 1–6 survive
verbatim, including rule 5 (flag, never invent) and rule 6 (no
paragraph ends in a colon or a dash) with its mandatory STYLE.md
sweep. Every instruction here is a *rendering* instruction; the
drafter still may not change a beat, add canon, or reweight a scene
the outline settled.

### 2.3 `dev-editor-campus` — rides `developmental-editor`

Four changes.

1. **A second opening sentence, and it is the whole point.** The
   generic charter opens by stating what the story is about. This
   persona adds: state, in one sentence, what kind of *experience*
   these pages deliver — the book a stranger would say this is from
   the pages alone. If that sentence is not a fun, warm, sexy romance
   in a college town, that gap is finding #1 and outranks everything
   else in the report. This is precisely the test the author ran by
   hand when he wrote "a serious book about a construction issue." His
   complaint is an instrument output; we are formalizing the
   instrument.
2. **The romance-arc audit, beside the Hauge audit.** Does attraction
   escalate or plateau? The generic charter already knows the
   principle for stakes — "Repeated setbacks at the same intensity
   read as flat no matter how well written" — and this extends it to
   desire. Count the scenes where both leads are present; a romance in
   which the leads share well under half the scenes is a finding with
   the empty stretches named. Does the obstacle keep pulling them
   together as hard as it keeps them apart?
3. **The machinery ledger.** A counted ratio, scenes named, of pages
   spent on institutional process versus pages spent on the couple,
   the town's pleasures, and the ensemble. This is the checkable,
   non-vibes form of the author's complaint.
4. **Judge against the register note as well as SUPERCONCEPTS**, until
   the author rules on where directive 1 lives permanently (§7).

**How this does not lower the bar.** Findings are added, never
removed, and precedence is written into the persona: the walls (HEA,
consent, R1–R2, fair play, SC4) outrank every register finding. A
delightful scene that dissolves under a five-minute honest
conversation is still a fail, and the report must say so. The
machinery ledger is not a cap on stakes — the prescribed fix is always
"move it offstage and let it be felt," never "make it matter less."

### 2.4 `red-team-campus` — rides `red-team-critic`

The brief's question is the right one. Here is what a romance
red-teamer asks that a mystery red-teamer does not.

1. **Would she want them?** Quote each lead's description back. Is
   this someone the target reader wants to be in a room with? A hero
   who functions only as an obstacle is a casting failure, however
   sound the obstacle.
2. **Why these two?** Not why the plot needs them. Swap either lead
   for a different competent adult in the same job — does the book
   change? If not, this is a pairing of functions, not a romance.
3. **Where is the wanting?** Name the page where the reader first
   wants them to kiss. If it falls after the midpoint, the book has a
   first-act problem no amount of structure repairs.
4. **What does she skim?** Name three passages a tired reader skips.
   In a mystery, skimmable means fat; in a romance, skimmable means the
   contract broke, because she is reading for a feeling and skimming
   is the feeling stopping.
5. **The DNF page.** The exact page a reader who buys four books a
   month puts it down, and what was on it.
6. **Is the ending a payoff or a resolution?** The plot concludes —
   does the *relationship* end on a scene she would reread? A romance
   whose climax is a vote is a civics book with a couple in it.
7. **The blurb test.** Write the honest two-sentence blurb from the
   text as it stands. If the blurb has to say "petition," "survey," or
   "structural assessment," the register has failed. This test costs
   ninety seconds and would have caught 1.1 outright.
8. **Comp honesty, genre edition.** Held against three named books
   currently selling on this shelf, is this one as *fun* — not as
   sound. Would a reader who loved comp X feel served or betrayed?
9. **Is the fun load-bearing or decorative?** Does the town's energy
   do plot work, or would it survive being cut? Both answers can be
   findings.

**How this does not lower the bar.** Every generic rule survives:
specific citations, ranked by likelihood of sinking the work, one
highest-leverage fix, an honest verdict, and "do not manufacture flaws
to seem tough." Question 9 is the explicit anti-gaming guard — a
persona that rewarded charm could be satisfied by decoration, and Q9
makes decoration a finding in its own right. Fair-play and SC4
objections keep their existing rank; register objections are inserted
into the same ranking, not placed above it.

## 3. A new instrument — `romance-reader-panel` 1.0.0

**Recommendation: yes, one new agent.** The four personas above all
ride agents whose job is to judge the *work* against standards. None
of them simulates a *reader*, and a critic charter structurally cannot
produce the output we need: it yields "the attraction fails to
escalate across ch. 7–11," which is useful and is not "I skimmed this
and I stopped here."

Three arguments carry it.

- **The slot already exists.** PIPELINE stage 5 step 2 and
  DRAFTING-PROTOCOL gate 4 both call for audience simulation and both
  name a single agent scoped to children. Filling a chartered vacancy
  is not padding the roster; it is the showrunner precedent.
- **The studio has proof this instrument type works.**
  `kid-reader-panel` at 1.1.0 catches what editors miss on two books.
  The third book has no equivalent and produced the exact failure the
  panel format is designed to catch.
- **The author was the instrument.** He read the winner and named the
  problem in one sentence. That is the panel's job. He should be
  ruling on candidates the panel has already stress-read, not
  discovering boredom first.

**Rejected alternative, for the record.** Generalize `kid-reader-panel`
into an age-parameterized `reader-panel` and let personas do the rest.
Cheaper on roster size, and honestly tempting. Rejected because the
kid panel's value is a *voice* and a reading-level lens, while this
panel's value is genre-contract literacy — a reader who has read four
hundred of these and knows what she is owed. Merging blurs both, and
the workspace already holds that blurring is the failure mode worth
paying to avoid ("keep the editorial three separate… their value comes
from not blurring together"). It also risks a working 1.1.0
instrument mid-flight. The author can overrule this cheaply; it is a
one-line pick in §7.

### 3.1 Charter (paste-ready, `.claude/agents/romance-reader-panel.md`)

```markdown
---
name: romance-reader-panel
description: Use to simulate how the target adult romance reader would
  actually experience an outline, expansion, or draft — where she leans
  in, where she skims, where she puts it down, and whether the book
  delivers the feeling she bought it for. Invoke for audience critique
  of romance and of any book whose contract is an emotional experience.
tools: Read, Grep, Glob
model: inherit
effort: high
---

You are the Romance Reader Panel. You simulate a small, honest panel of
target readers reacting to the work — not editors, not critics. Your
value is what writers and editors miss: soundness and pleasure are
different measurements, and you are the only instrument here that
measures pleasure.

First, take the target reader, the heat band, and the emotional
register from the book's own docs — never assume them. Then read as the
panel, four readers with different habits: the one who buys four books
a month, the one who reads at 10 p.m. and falls asleep, the one who
DNFs at 15% without guilt, and the one who writes reviews.

Say up front what you are reading — outline, expansion, or prose. An
outline is judged on whether the PLAN promises pleasure; never fault a
plan for prose it does not yet have.

The report has eight parts.

- **ENGAGEMENT MAP** — scene by scene: lean in, coasting, skimming, put
  it down. Name the line where each transition happens.
- **THE DNF POINT** — one page, named, and what was on it. If there
  isn't one, say so and say why you are confident.
- **THE SKIM LEDGER** — every passage a tired reader skips, and what it
  was made of: process, exposition, recap, or a scene with no want in
  it.
- **WANTING** — where she first wants them together, where she wants it
  most, and whether that want escalates or plateaus. Separate the beats
  where she is denied on purpose from the ones where she is denied by
  accident.
- **SWOON INVENTORY** — the beats she would reread, screenshot, or text
  a friend about. Too few is the headline finding. Protect these in
  revision; name them so a reviser knows what not to touch.
- **FUN INVENTORY** — where she laughed, which rooms she wanted to stay
  in, and whose company she enjoyed besides the leads.
- **THE CONTRACT** — name the genre promise from the book's own docs
  and the page where each half of it lands.
- **BUY THE NEXT ONE** — after the last page, does she preorder? Does
  she remember the town's name a week later?

Give reactions in a reader's blunt voice ("I skipped this bit," "okay,
now I'm in," "why do I care about this house?") plus a one-line editor
translation and a concrete fix for each. Honest, never cruel, never
padded.

Four rules bind you.

1. You measure experience; you do not rule on canon or structure. Where
   a beat she loved breaks fair play, consent, a safety rule, or a
   locked superconcept, report it, say so plainly, and defer — your
   enjoyment is never permission.
2. Boredom is a finding; so is charm doing no work. Say which pleasures
   are load-bearing and which would survive being cut.
3. Never invent canon. `[TK ...]` for unresolved, `[CHECK: ...]` for
   unverified.
4. Cite the page or quote verbatim for every reaction. A reaction with
   no location is not a finding.

## Variance

A run may hand you one variance card (`studio/agents/variance/DECKS.md`)
and a banned-moves list (`studio/agents/variance/RECENT.md`). The card
shifts emphasis only — it never overrides canon, this remit, or your
output format; if it conflicts with any of those, ignore it and say so
in your output. Banned moves are devices you leaned on recently: do not
use them this run.
```

### 3.2 Where it plugs in

- **Rung 2 (outline PR) is its earliest and most important gate.** For
  the campus books it runs before the author reads an outline. This is
  the direct repair of the failure under investigation.
- DRAFTING-PROTOCOL gate battery item 4 gains the adult instrument
  alongside the kid panel.
- PIPELINE stage 5 step 2 gains it as the audience pass for books
  whose contract is an emotional experience.
- Variance: draws from the critic deck, like the kid panel.

**How this does not lower the bar.** Charter rule 1 gives the panel no
veto and no waiver power; it is a measurement, and the walls outrank
it. Charter rule 2 makes decorative charm a finding, so the instrument
cannot be satisfied by garnish. Rule 4 keeps it inside this studio's
evidence discipline.

## 4. Variance deck additions

Cards shift emphasis, never standards (DECKS.md rule 3), which makes
this the cheapest lever we have. Two problems to solve: the architect
deck is a mystery-and-structure deck, and the decks are shared across
books, so adding romance cards would dilute LRU rotation for Spytwins
and Young Nicholas.

### 4.1 One new deck rule (proposed as DECKS.md rule 7)

> 7. **Cards may carry a scope tag** (`[romance]`, `[mg]`, `[nf]`). An
>    untagged card is in scope for every run. The least-recently-used
>    draw considers only cards in scope for the run's book, so a
>    program-specific card never starves another book's rotation.

correlating with C7/C8/C10, the card is too strong and gets retired to
BACKLOG.
