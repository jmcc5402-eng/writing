## The pitch (author's words, lightly compressed)

Semi-fan-fiction romance series where each book is based on a well-known
song — country first, but any/all songs eligible, including One Direction
and modern picks. The move isn't retelling the song; it's *answering* it:

- Garth Brooks, "Unanswered Prayers" — but the high-school sweethearts
  actually DO get together after the football game. What then?
- Megan Moroney, "Tennessee Orange" — Georgia Bulldog family, she falls
  for a Tennessee man; the song is the meet-cute, the book is the war
  of the tailgates.
- One Direction slot open; more modern picks needed.

Needs a theme to tie the series together (see candidates below).

## Prior-art research (2026-08-19)

**The exact thing does not appear to exist.** No branded romance series
was found where each installment adapts one well-known song. What exists
around the edges:

- **Songfic** is an established *fanfiction* genre (stories built on a
  song, historically quoting lyrics — which is exactly the part that's
  legally radioactive commercially).
- **Song-adjacent romance** is plentiful: Goodreads keeps lists like
  "Books With Country Love Song Names" (54 books) and "Opry: Romance
  Novels with Country Music Artists" (108 books) — country-music
  *settings* and song-title *titles*, not song-plot adaptations.
- **Authors who soundtrack**: e.g. LJ Evans titles every chapter with a
  song and publishes playlists — vibe pairing, not adaptation.
- **Song-to-screen is a proven lane**: "Unanswered Prayers" itself became
  a 2010 Lifetime movie **executive-produced by Garth Brooks**; Kenny
  Rogers filmed "Coward of the County" (1981); NewSong's "The Christmas
  Shoes" became a Donna VanLiere novel, then a 2002 TV movie. The market
  loves this move — it just hasn't been productized as a Kindle romance
  series.
- **Adjacent packaging proof**: Taylor-Swift-inspired romance is its own
  informal subgenre; a Disney retelling series titles its books with
  Disney songs. Readers already buy "the book of the song I love."

So the lane is validated by TV and one-offs, and open at series scale.

## The legal line (research summary, not counsel)

- **Song titles are not copyrightable.** A book may share a title with a
  song. (Very famous titles can raise trademark / unfair-competition
  questions when used to imply endorsement.)
- **Plots and premises are not copyrightable.** "Man meets old flame at
  a hometown football game and thanks God for the wife he has" is a
  situation, not protected expression. Writing our own story in that
  situation is legitimate.
- **Lyrics are copyrighted — quoting even one line requires a license**,
  and music publishers charge for it. House rule for this series if it
  ever runs: **zero quoted lyrics, ever.**
- **Marketing is the real tripwire**: covers/blurbs must not use the
  artist's name or imply affiliation ("the Garth Brooks book") — that's
  right-of-publicity / trademark territory, not copyright. Position as
  "for readers who love the story inside [song]" energy without naming
  names in packaging; let reviewers and TikTok make the connection.
- Precedents note the safe version: the Lifetime movie had Brooks
  *aboard*. Unlicensed, we adapt the situation, never the expression or
  the endorsement.

## Series-glue candidates (the "theme to tie it together")

The author asked for a unifying theme. Candidates to evaluate later:

1. **The Answer Series** — every book answers the question its song
   leaves open (what if the prayer WAS answered; what happens after the
   truck drives off). The hook is the counterfactual, and it names the
   brand without naming any song.
2. **One jukebox, one town** — a single bar/honky-tonk whose jukebox
   holds every song; each book opens with someone punching in "their"
   number. Shared-setting glue, campus-series-style bench economics.
3. **The radio DJ matchmaker** — a late-night country station's
   request-line host threads the books; each book is one caller's story.
   (Delilah energy; strong wine-night-adjacent frame device.)
4. **The playlist inheritance** — a mother leaves her daughter a
   19-track playlist and a note: "every song on here happened to
   somebody I know." Each book is one track. Finite, sequenced,
   binge-shaped.

Frame devices 2–4 could carry a calling-card ritual like the campus
series' wine night. Note the studio pattern: our engine (checklist,
register wave, bench rules) ports to any container.
