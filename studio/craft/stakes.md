# Stakes — everyone in the room has something to lose, and some of it is dark

The SPEC (studio/MECHANISM.md): book-agnostic. Each book's instance is
`books/<book>/canon/STAKES.md`; the brief gate demands a STAKES ON THE
PAGE section per chapter; the panel's STANCE test asks, for every named
character on the page, what the reader knows they stand to lose.

## The author's principle (2026-09-20)

> "My favorite books are James Clavell's Asian saga, and what makes
> them so good is that every character has incredibly high stakes —
> they're all in some complete disaster or debacle, usually caused by
> themselves, and the reader doesn't always hear how that specific
> stake resolves, and that's fine. But in the moment when that
> character is on the scene, the reader always knows that the stakes
> are high for that character."

> "I'd actually like some of the character stakes to be a bit darker.
> In Clavell there is always gambling, cheating, drug abuse. I don't
> want to go all-in on that… One of the overall problems with the book
> is that it's a bit cartoonish. Let's start making this book, and the
> next ones, more dark/real-life."

## Why a dark stake is worth more than a sad one

A stake becomes a plot line the moment it touches the story's
pressure point. The example that named the principle: a trainer in
debt to a bookie is a private disaster. A trainer in debt who knows
the quarterback's medical status before anyone is a betting line —
and now every sheet the doctor signs has a price on a phone. Nothing
was added to the plot; a stake was put next to the thing the plot
already turns on, and a conflict appeared that no outline had to
invent. "They create so many complex and exciting plot and conflict
lines" (the author).

So the test for every stake on the sheet is not "is it sad" but:

**STAKE × LEVER.** What does this person stand to lose, and which
thing the story already turns on could it touch? A stake with no
lever is texture. A stake with a lever is a plot line the author can
pull or leave alone — and the reader, who knows the stake, feels the
lever every time the person is in the room, whether it is ever
pulled or not.

## Rules for the page

1. **The reader knows, in the moment.** When a named character is on
   the page, the reader knows what they stand to lose — in a line,
   dropped when they are in the room. Never a speech; never explained
   twice in a book.
2. **Mostly self-inflicted.** A debt they ran up, a lie they told, a
   thing they wanted too much. A victim is a lesser stake than a
   person who did this to themselves.
3. **Never owed.** The reader is not owed the resolution of a minor
   character's stake. Some resolve because the outline needs them to;
   most just stay high. That is realism, not a hole.
4. **Dark and real, not drastic.** The vices are the ones real people
   have — gambling, drinking, a marriage held together by a secret,
   money — and they cost. Not shown being done unless the author
   says; smelled, counted, missed, paid for. A vice is never a joke
   or a costume.
5. **Through the POV.** In a close-third book the drop comes through
   what the POV lead sees, smells, hears or knows, or what the town
   knows — a score checked three times, a breath at two in the
   afternoon, a bill on a counter. The lead does not have to
   understand it; the reader does. (A brief dip into a minor
   character's head is not allowed — the author ruled it, 2026-09-20,
   for 1.2 and the books after.) The author's model drop, the shape to
   copy: *"Aisha waited for the trainer, and saw him close the sports
   betting app before he came into the room."* Seen, named, plain. Not
   "a score he has money on" — that asks the reader to infer; the app
   says it.
6. **Planned at the outline, dropped at the brief.** The stakes sheet
   is written when the cast is (series-kit 15); the outline audit
   asks STAKE × LEVER of every named character; each chapter brief
   names which stakes this page carries and in whose line.

## How much

A line or two per character per appearance, not every appearance.
Three drops of one stake across a book is plenty. A book that explains
a stake twice has told the reader it does not trust them.

## Where it lives

- `studio/series-kit/15-cast-stakes.md` — the template.
- `books/<book>/canon/STAKES.md` — the sheet: locked vs proposed;
  who, the stake, self-inflicted, the lever, the drops so far.
- `canon/FACTS.md` — the locked facts (an amount, a "never shown"),
  so the page cannot drift them.
- The brief — STAKES ON THE PAGE (`brief-gate.py` refuses a chapter
  brief without it).
- The panel — THE STANCE test: want, and what they stand to lose;
  "cartoonish" is a finding.
- The registry — every drop logged, so the count stays small.
