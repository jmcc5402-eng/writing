# Busy Is Not the Same as Good

*Part I — Wake the F\* Up*

Chapter one left a question sitting in your lap:
if the boss is improvising the job,
what exactly are they grading you on?
Here's the second thing nobody says out loud, so I will:

**Activity is the currency of fakers — and looking busy is
counterfeit money.**

You've been getting paid in it your whole career.

Think it through.
Evaluating work requires understanding the work,
and understanding the work is the exact skill
your boss topped out without.
So a faker grades the only thing a faker can see: motion.
Emails at strange hours. A calendar with no white space.
The green dot.
The scoreboard you've been sweating over doesn't measure value.
It measures blur.

## The green dot economy

Somewhere in your company, right now,
a person is wiggling their mouse so their status stays green.
Not working — curating the *evidence* of working.

You know the whole catalog, because it's performed at you daily.
The 9:47 p.m. Slack message that says "quick thoughts on the deck"
and could have been sent at 9:00 the next morning
with no change to the fate of the deck.
The reply scheduled for 6:12 a.m.
so it lands with the sound of a person who rises before the sun.
The thumbs-up emoji deployed across four channels in one minute,
which is not communication —
it's taking attendance of yourself.
And the video-call version, the purest artifact in the museum:
camera on, chin on fist, the slow thoughtful nod —
a nod aimed at a slide,
performed by someone reading a group chat on the other monitor.
The nod isn't for the presenter.
The nod is for the gallery.

That 9:47 message isn't work.
It's a timestamp with feelings.

And here's the thing about counterfeit money: it *spends.*
That's the seduction.
The green dot buys nods, the late email buys "so dedicated,"
the full calendar buys another full calendar.
Counterfeit works great —
right up until somebody checks it against the real ledger.
Hold that thought, because somebody's about to.

I say all this with love, as a former artisan of the form.
I once built a status spreadsheet so beautifully color-coded
it belonged in a gallery.
Nobody ever opened it.
It's still out there somewhere, perfect and unread,
like a shipwreck full of gold.

## Badge swipes and the jacket on the chair

Then the office came back, and the theater got a bigger stage.

Return-to-office politics run on the same counterfeit:
presence as proof.
The badge report is a scoreboard of doors you walked through.
The suit jacket lives permanently on the back of the chair,
a scarecrow for middle management.
People carry laptops through the halls half-open, at 45 degrees —
the universal costume of *en route to something important.*

None of this is a character flaw.
It's a rational response to a broken sensor:
if the scorekeeper can only detect motion,
everyone learns to move.
A badge swipe proves exactly one thing — a door opened.
The company is taking attendance like a substitute teacher
and calling it culture.

And once presence becomes the metric,
you get the meetings that exist to be attended:
a status meeting about a status document
that summarizes the previous status meeting.

## The robot is eating the costume

Now the uncomfortable news for the green-dot economy:
the busywork is being automated out from under the performance.

The status summary, the meeting recap,
the first draft of the 40-slide deck —
the machine does each of them in eleven seconds
and doesn't even want credit.
The pretty Gantt chart? Also eleven seconds.
And a Gantt chart was always a pretty thing, not the project —
if the tasks on it are the wrong tasks,
you've drawn a beautiful map of a road to nowhere,
and now the software draws the map for free.

Which puts a hard question under everyone's chair:
if a robot can do the looking-busy parts of your job instantly,
what was the looking-busy worth?

You already know the answer, and so does the ledger.
So don't compete with the machine for the counterfeit work —
that's a race to the bottom of a shredder.
Wield it.
Feed it the recaps and the formatting and the first drafts,
and spend the hours it hands back
on the problems from chapter one —
the hard, undefined ones nobody's claimed.
The machine can't walk into a vacuum.
You can.

## The catch-up ritual

One more exhibit, because it's the purest one.

Watch what happens when a coworker comes back from a week off.
They announce — with real dread, and a little pride —
that today will be spent "catching up on email."
A full day.
Reading a transcript of meetings
the company already survived without them.
It feels like work. It sweats like work.
It's cardio: effort, motion, zero displacement.

There's a faster version,
and it's embarrassing how well it works.
Walk over to a human and ask:
"Hey Bill, what did I miss this week? Anything exciting?"
Five minutes.
Bill lights up — Bill has been *holding* this material all week
and you have just handed him a stage.
You get what actually broke, what actually shipped,
and who's circling what.
The inbox contains the same information,
minus the truth.
(And yes, sometimes Bill's answer is "honestly? nothing,"
delivered in four seconds flat.
That's not a failed experiment.
That's a full day of catching up, refunded.)

That's the whole chapter in one ritual:
the inbox is activity.
Bill is the ledger.

## The point

Here's the part that should actually bother you,
and it's not the fakery.
It's that everyone kind of knows.
Nobody believes the green dot.
Nobody thinks the jacket is working late.
The counterfeit is an open secret,
and the printing presses run anyway — including yours.
You've sent the 9:47 message.
So have I. Mine had *three* follow-up bullets.

Hold on to how strange that is:
a building full of intelligent adults,
printing money they know is fake,
for a scorekeeper they know can't count it.

They're not performing for the scoreboard.
They're performing because they're hoping.
