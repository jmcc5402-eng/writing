# Your Boss Is Probably Faking It

*Part I — Wake the F\* Up*

There's a rule that runs every office on earth,
and nobody will say it out loud, so I will:

**People get promoted until they reach a job they're bad at — and then
they stop.**
They don't get promoted *out* of the job they're bad at,
because, well, they're bad at it.
So they stay. Forever. Marinating.

This isn't a hot take; it has a name.
A professor called Laurence Peter worked it out decades ago
and boiled it down to one line:
**"You rise to your highest level of incompetence."**
He published it as satire.
Corporate America read it as an instruction manual.
Fifty-odd years later it runs quietly in every building
with a lobby, like HVAC.

Which means the person sitting one level above you —
the one who approves your time off, "gives you feedback,"
and gets visibly stressed by their own calendar —
is, statistically, *at the exact job they are worst at.*
That's not a tragedy.

That's the best news of your entire career.

Take a second with it.
Every locked door in your career has been guarded, this whole time,
by someone who is winging it.

## Let it sink in

You've been walking around assuming the org chart
is a ranking of competence.
Top = smart, bottom = still learning.
That's the story they need you to believe,
because the story is doing a *lot* of unpaid work for them.

Here's the actual situation:
your boss was probably good at a completely different job —
the one they had before this one —
and then someone handed them people to manage
and a problem to solve,
and it turns out those are unrelated skills.
They are not a genius gatekeeper standing between you and success.
They are a person doing an improv scene called "In Charge,"
and they are hoping nobody asks a follow-up question.

The gap between their title and their actual ability?
That gap is not your enemy.

**That gap is the whole opportunity. It's where you're going to live.**

## How to spot a faker (it's not subtle)

Fakers can't solve real problems, so they perform *around* them.
Two tells:

**They love Things.**
A Thing is anything tangible they can point at instead of thinking:
a new dashboard, a reorg, a Slack channel called `#alignment`,
a 40-slide deck, an offsite.
Things feel like progress.
Real problems — "our team doesn't trust each other,"
"nobody knows what we're actually building" — are *intangible*,
and fakers are allergic to intangible.
So they buy a Thing and hope the Thing does the thinking.
Watch how fast a faker reaches for a tool
when the honest move is a hard conversation.

**They love Hope.**
Listen for it.
*"I was hoping this would be further along."*
*"I'm hoping the new process fixes it."*
Hope is what people do instead of managing.
A real boss makes you *more* able to do the job —
clearer, better-equipped, unblocked.
A faker just… hopes at you, from a distance,
and then acts betrayed when hoping doesn't ship the project.

Once you can see Things and Hope, you can't un-see them.
You'll clock it in the first ten minutes of any meeting.
It's a genuinely fun party trick
and it will make you insufferable at brunch.
Worth it.

## The move: walk into the vacuum

Here's the part that separates you from the 90% who saw
exactly what you just saw — and took it back to the group chat,
because standing out feels expensive.
They're not wrong to feel the price.
Everyone feels it: the flush of a raised hand,
the four loud seconds after you ask something in a full room.
They're only wrong about the amount.
It's free — and that's the other secret in this book,
the one that gets a chapter of its own.

Competence, in a room full of fakers, is a **vacuum.**
The problems your boss is performing-around are sitting right there.
Unclaimed.
Nobody in the room *wants* the hard, undefined, intangible one,
because it's scary and it might not work.

**Take it.** Not with a speech. With a question:

> "Quick gut-check before we build anything — what's the actual
> problem we're solving here?"

Watch the room.
The fakers will get uncomfortable, because they don't know,
and now everyone can see they don't know.
And you?
You just did the one thing your boss can't do,
in public, in a way that looks helpful instead of aggressive.
You didn't attack anyone.
You just turned on a light.
That's the entire game,
and it's disguised as being a team player.

Do that once a week and within a year
the higher-ups will start wondering
why *your boss* isn't the one asking those questions.

(A warning from experience: the first time it works,
you will want to do it four more times in the same meeting.
Don't. One light per room.
You're an operator, not a lighthouse.)

## The point (and the vibe)

This isn't a revenge fantasy.
Revenge is for people who are still intimidated —
and you're done being intimidated.
The person above you is a beatable level, not a supervillain.
The bar is on the floor.

So stop looking *up* at them
like they're weather you have to survive.
Look *down* at the problem
like it's a video game you just figured out.
Then go take the wheel.

The final boss has a health bar. Aim for it.

One more thing before you go swaggering into Monday.
If the person above you is improvising the job,
sit with what that does to the rest of the furniture.
The performance reviews they write.
The ratings they hand out —
that five-point scale they fill in about you once a year,
with the confidence of a wine critic who can't taste.
Because the boss isn't only doing the work up there.
The boss is keeping score. Your score.
And if the scorekeeper is improvising —

how much would you bet on the scoreboard?

That's chapter two. Bring a calculator.
