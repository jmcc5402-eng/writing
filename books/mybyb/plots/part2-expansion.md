## Contrarian comp (variance card A4)

**The comp that says this book shouldn't work: *The Dilbert
Principle* (Scott Adams, 1996).** It made this book's acquisition
joke thirty years earlier, sold in the millions, and trained an
entire working culture to treat managerial incompetence as
entertainment — which is the problem. Dilbert proved the diagnosis
sells and simultaneously proved the diagnosis is inert: its reader
laughs, pins the strip to the cubicle wall, and does not move. A
buyer with that book in their memory has a fair objection, and it is
the strongest one on the shelf. "I already know my boss is faking
it. That was a coffee mug in 1998." (Second-order version of the same
objection, from the free end of the shelf: *Ask a Manager* answers
"what do I do about my boss" daily, searchably, for nothing.)

**How Part II dodges it.** Dilbert never hands you a move; it hands
you company. Part II is four consecutive chapters in which the joke
*is* the instruction — every chapter ends with a specific
unauthorized thing you can do at 10:25 on Monday, at zero cost, and
the fourth one costs you something real and says so out loud.
Cynicism that issues a receipt stops being cynicism. That is the
whole difference, and it lives in this Part or nowhere.

## Part II at a glance

**One sentence.** Four moves nobody in your building will make,
each one free, each one performed in public, arranged from the
smallest room to the most dangerous one.

**One paragraph.** There is a problem in your office that has been
sitting unclaimed for a year, and everyone can see it. There is a
meeting drifting toward its own end time with eight people in it and
nobody steering. There is a task on your desk that nobody has
connected to any plan, and a boss upstairs who will describe your
work to their boss using whatever they can remember of it. All four
of those are vacancies, and vacancies are free. Part II walks you
through them in order, from the problem you can take alone to the
person who writes your performance review, and the price goes up at
every stop. By the last chapter the move still costs no money and no
permission, but it costs something you will notice, and you get to
decide whether you want it. Same purchase, four aisles, a rising tag.

**The escalation axis.** One problem, alone → one room, in public →
one direction, upward → one person, permanently. That is the spine
that keeps four applications of a single thesis from reading as the
same chapter four times.

## Chapter 5 — Volunteer for the Grenade

**Claim.** The problems everybody steps around are unowned rather
than above your pay grade, they break into ordinary parts the moment
somebody picks them up, and volunteering to hold one is the cheapest
promotion available to a person with no authority.

Every office has a problem with no name on it. Not a task — a task
has an owner and a date. A problem: the handoff between two teams
that loses something every single time, the process everyone calls "a
known issue" in the tone people reserve for weather. The reader
arrives here having just been shown that acting costs nothing, and
carrying one belief they have never said out loud, which is that the
ugly, undefined messes belong to somebody with a bigger title. This
chapter takes that belief away. Nobody is holding the hard problems.
That isn't a hierarchy. It's a vacancy, and you already know from
chapter four why vacancies stay open.

The chapter's engine is the split, demonstrated live rather than
asserted. Morale is low, so the boss books a happy hour, buys the
first round, and is genuinely confused four weeks later when morale
is still low. A happy hour is a tangible answer to an intangible
problem, and applying one to the other is most of what management
looks like from underneath. Here the book gives the reader the only
tool in Part II that is a tool rather than a nerve: **Tangible vs.
Intangible Problems**, and the rule of thumb that a difficult
intangible problem is roughly eighty percent tangible parts. Then it
does the split on the page, on a real one — the handoff that loses
things becomes nobody owns the checklist, two systems call the same
field two different names, and the person who knew retired in March.
Three tangible problems, all boring, all solvable by an adult with a
calendar. That demonstration is the chapter's receipt, and without it
the eighty-twenty rule is just a number in a nice font.

Then the honest turn, and it is the first one in the book: this move
is not free. The tax was free. Holding a grenade is work, in public,
on something that may not fully resolve, and you cannot put it down
quietly once your name is on it. For a while you will be the person
associated with the ugly thing, which is a real cost and the chapter
should price it rather than wink at it. What you get back is not a
solution — it is that the room's map changes, and problems start
arriving at you before they arrive at your boss. You are not being
asked to defuse it. You are being asked to move it eight feet, which
is eight feet more than it has moved since March.

So the reader leaves believing something new and slightly
inconvenient. The unclaimed problem is claimable, it is made of
ordinary parts, and being the person who holds one is worth more than
being the person who finishes tickets. They also leave knowing the
menu's zero-price tag was true about nerve and not about effort. And
they leave with the next wall in view, because the big splits do not
happen at your desk. They happen in a room with eight people in it,
each holding a different answer, none of them aware the others
brought one.

## Chapter 6 — Hijack the Meeting

**Claim.** A drifting meeting is an unclaimed problem with an
audience, and whoever moves everyone's separate answer into one shape
runs the room without holding the title or asking for it.

The reader walks in able to own a problem alone and still convinced
that meetings are overhead — dead minutes, somebody else's failure,
the part of the day work happens between. This chapter reclassifies
them. A meeting where nobody is steering is exactly the vacancy from
last chapter, except it lasts fifty minutes, has witnesses, and
refills every Tuesday. And the reason it drifts is not that people
are dim. Steering in public is a tax nobody in the room will pay, so
the meeting goes wherever the loudest confidence points it, and
everyone files out believing the same wrong thing about what was
agreed.

The set piece is **The Olympic Rings**, and it debuts here. Picture
everyone's solution as one ring, roughly concentric, barely
overlapping, and the job is not to be right — it is to slide the
rings until they sit on top of each other. The mechanism is nothing
but questions asked in order. You get agreement on the problem, then
you ask each person, by name, what they would do, and you let the
room discover something it finds genuinely surprising: nobody else
walked in with the answer they were assuming everyone had. There is a
whole comedy section in that, because most people appear to believe a
magical scribe is transcribing their inner monologue and emailing it
around. The chapter can also spend the poker table before the rings
arrive, because choosing which meeting to sit in is the same skill
one step earlier, and a table full of pros is a bad place to make
money.

The turn is a trap, and it is where this chapter earns its place
instead of being a technique demo. Steering a room you do not own
looks like a power grab, and it becomes one the instant you steer
toward your own answer. So the price is specific and it stings: you
have to give up being right. The move only works when you actually
want the rings aligned, including on the day they align on somebody
else's ring while yours is the one that gets slid. Second cost, less
glamorous, and the chapter should admit it — this is slow. Sometimes
it takes three meetings, and the first one ends with the room
understanding only that it disagrees, which is progress that feels
exactly like failure.

The reader now believes a room is a workable unit, that alignment
beats being right, and that the person asking the questions ends up
holding the pen whether or not anyone voted. Which sets up the
problem they cannot see yet, and it is the good one. A room can align
perfectly, cheerfully, unanimously, on entirely the wrong thing.

## Chapter 7 — Ask the Question Nobody Wants to Answer

**Claim.** The most expensive unasked question in your building is how
today's work connects to the actual plan, asking it out loud costs
nothing, and it is worth asking even in the case — especially in the
case — where the answer turns out not to exist.

By now the reader can take a problem and steer a room, and still
believes that executing well and steering well is the job. That is
the belief this chapter removes, and it is the last comfortable one
they have. The work you were handed arrives looking like a fact. It
has a name, a deadline, and someone's confidence attached, and none
of that is evidence that it fits into anything. Chapter one told you
the person above you is at the job they are worst at; chapter two
told you the scoreboard counts activity. Put those together and you
get an uncomfortable arithmetic about how much of the work in your
building has been checked against a plan by anybody at all.

So the book hands over its plainest move, **BIU — Bump It Up**, which
gets its only definition here: aligning the task in front of you with
the larger thing it is supposedly part of, out loud, in the room,
usually in one sentence. Once we finish this, what changes. How does
this fit the bigger push. What is the actual question we are trying
to answer. This is chapter one's vacuum question grown from one
meeting to a whole project, and it works for a reason the chapter has
to name, because otherwise it reads like a stunt: nobody else in the
room is sure either. That is why the silence after the question is
not hostility. It is relief, arriving slowly, from four directions.
There is also a version for cowards, which is the version most people
will actually run — bump it up while "explaining things" to the new
person in the room, and let the alignment gap fall out of the
explanation.

The turn is the setback the whole Part has been walking toward. You
bump it up, and sometimes there is nothing up there. The strategy is
three words on a slide, or four people's incompatible ideas of the
same sentence, and now you are the one standing in a room holding
that fact. Chapter five's price comes due again in a bigger room:
find the gap and it is yours. Worse, the same question asked to win
rather than to know reads as a stall or a challenge, and the
difference is audible to everyone. The chapter has to teach the
reader to ask it the way you ask for directions, not the way you ask
for a receipt.

The reader leaves believing alignment is a real, ownable, mostly
unowned problem, that the question is free, and that finding the hole
is better than shipping into it. And they leave with one person still
untouched. Every move in this Part so far has routed around the boss.
The one human being who can make all four of them cheap or expensive
has not been addressed since Part I, when they were mostly a punch
line.

## Chapter 8 — Manage Up or Get Managed

**Claim.** You will either run the communication between you and your
boss or inherit their chaos as your performance review, and running
it is a system you operate rather than a personality you fake.

The reader arrives treating their boss as weather — a condition to be
dressed for, worked around, occasionally complained about, never
addressed. This chapter says the boss is an interface, and interfaces
accept input. Here is the part that reframes the whole decade in one
observation the reader can verify this week: your work does not go
upstairs. A description of your work goes upstairs, delivered from
memory, by someone who was half-listening, to people you have never
met. Whatever your boss can repeat is what you did. Everything else
is a rumor about a spreadsheet.

So the chapter hands over the last three pieces of the book's
working vocabulary, all homed here and nowhere earlier. **BLUF**,
bottom line up front, because a boss who has to read four paragraphs
to find the ask will carry four paragraphs of nothing upstairs.
**CFC**, crystal clear, applied ruthlessly, because clarity is not a
courtesy you extend but a container you build for your own work.
And **NR**, non-reality, the affliction of the boss whose every
passing thought arrives already believing it was assigned — the one
who thinks big, out loud, on a Thursday, from a land of unicorns and
butterflies, and is startled when nobody built it. Naming
non-reality is the move, and naming it kindly, in the room, in the
form of a question about feasibility, is the whole skill.

Then the turn, and it is the Part's low point on purpose, because it
is the objection the reader has been holding since the chapter title.
This looks exactly like the thing the book banned. Where is the line
between managing up and sucking up, and who decides. The test the
chapter offers is the one the book has been building since chapter
four: bootlicking is the tax paid in a different currency, fear
dressed as helpfulness, aimed at making yourself liked. Managing up
is aimed at making the work accurate, and it works identically on a
boss you respect and one you do not. But there is a genuine price
here, the highest in the Part, and the chapter must say it plainly:
you are doing part of your boss's job, and they may well get the
credit. What you are trading is credit for control, and this is the
first offer in the book a reader might turn down.

The reader leaves believing the boss relationship is a system with
inputs they own, and holding a question the Part deliberately cannot
answer. Every one of these four moves works the same whether the
person above you is a faker or the most competent operator you will
ever meet. The tax told you to move. It never once told you who to
move toward, and there is a version of this chapter run on the wrong
boss that wastes two years of your life. That is the next Part.

## Block 2 — The two journeys, translated (Hauge)

- **OUTER goal (visible finish line).** By the end of Part II the
  reader can name and run four specific public moves, and has run at
  least one of them at work. The four Monday-move boxes are the
  finish line, and they are visible from the start because ch. 4's
  menu already listed the aisles.
- **INNER journey.** Identity: *a careful person who is good at the
  job and waiting for that to be noticed.* Essence: *a person who
  acts in public.* The wound underneath is the one the book already
  priced — being seen is expensive — and Part II heals it by
  repetition rather than argument. The reader is seen four times, at
  rising exposure, and nothing happens each time.
- **Turning points, measured across the Part (ch. 4–8).**
  ~10% Opportunity: ch. 4's menu, already on the page.
  ~25% Change of plans: ch. 5's turn, where free stops being free and
  the reader owns something.
  ~50% Point of no return: ch. 6's turn — you have steered a room in
  public, and there is no way back to being furniture.
  ~75% Major setback: ch. 7's turn — you bump it up and there is
  nothing up there, and now the hole is yours.
  ~90% Climax: ch. 8's move, run on the one person who writes your
  review, with the price named and the direction deliberately
  withheld.
- **The clock the reader must SEE early.** The decade. Ch. 5 should
  make it visible once, in one line, in its first page — this is the
  Part where the reader spends the cheapest years they will ever
  have. No statistic unless sourced; see OQ 8.
- **Superior position (what the reader knows first).** In every
  chapter the reader sees the vacancy before the example-room does:
  the unclaimed problem in ch. 5, the misaligned rings in ch. 6, the
  missing plan in ch. 7, the description going upstairs in ch. 8.
  That gap is the Part's comedy engine and it points down at a
  situation, never at a person, which is the guardrail holding.
- **Foreshadow / echo pairs.** Ch. 5 "find it and it's yours" echoes
  in ch. 7's setback at project scale. Ch. 4's "one price, printed on
  every tag: zero" inverts in ch. 8's credit-for-control trade. Ch.
  1's vacuum question is the seed ch. 7 grows to full size.

## Block 7 — Cheap Power slate (names and one-liners only, per rec 4)

Six boxes across Parts II–III. Names checked against the four
existing boxes (The 5:01 Walk, The Front Row, The Donut Move, The
Pre-Break Pee) and against each other for confusability. Copy is not
written here by design.

1. **The Agenda Nobody Asked For** — you send three lines an hour
   before a meeting you do not own, and you are now running it
   without ever having said so.
2. **The U-Turn** — you change your mind out loud, mid-meeting, in
   favor of somebody else's idea, and watch what it does to the room.
3. **The Recap Send** — four lines of "here's what we agreed" after
   any decision-ish conversation, because whoever writes the record
   owns the record.
4. **The Credit Pass** — you name a colleague's specific contribution
   in front of their boss, unprompted, and pay nothing for it.
5. **The First Draft** — you write the bad first version of the
   document nobody wants to start, and quietly set the frame everyone
   else will edit.
6. **The Early Ask** — you ask for the budget, the conference, or the
   project six months before anyone is deciding, when the answer is
   still cheap to give.

**Proposed placement.** After ch. 5, The Agenda Nobody Asked For
(teases ch. 6, the Donut Move precedent). After ch. 6, The U-Turn.
After ch. 7, The Recap Send (teases ch. 8). After ch. 8, The Credit
Pass (opens Part III's register — other people). Banked for Part III:
The First Draft, The Early Ask.

## Block 9 — Voice, density, and rations (Part-level)

Carried from the voice spec, the Part I rulings, and the keep-warm
read's new findings.

- **Word budget.** ~1,200–1,300 body words per chapter, matching Part
  I. Ch. 8 caps at ~1,350 and no higher — it carries three new terms
  and that is the load risk of the Part (see OQ 4).
- **Handoffs capped at two lines**, per ruling 4. Trailer-voice
  endings are a banned move; the handoff states the open question and
  stops.
- **The wait-beat** ("I'll wait" / "Take your time") is rationed to
  **once per Part** and is assigned to **ch. 7**, in the silence after
  the connecting question, where it is the joke rather than a tic.
