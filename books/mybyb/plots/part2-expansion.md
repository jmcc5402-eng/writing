## Part II at a glance

**One sentence.** Four moves nobody in your building will make: no
money, no permission, no title, and each one hands you something
heavier to carry out than the last.

**One paragraph.** There is a problem in your office that has been
sitting in a thread since March with forty-one replies, three
apologies, and no owner. There is a meeting drifting toward its own
end time with five people in it and nobody steering. There is a task
on your desk that nobody has checked against any plan, and a boss
upstairs who will describe your work to their boss from memory, on a
Thursday, having half-listened. Nobody is standing in any of those
four spaces, and the reason is identical every time: standing there
is conspicuous, and conspicuous feels expensive. It isn't. Part II
walks you into all four in order, from the problem you can pick up
alone to the person who writes your review, and the tag reads zero at
every register — no money, no permission, no authority. What changes
is what you walk out holding. By the fourth aisle you are carrying
something you will actually feel, and the book stops and asks whether
you want it, because it is the first offer in here you might turn
down.

**The escalation axis.** One problem, alone → one room, live → one
direction, upward → one person, permanently. Same purchase every
time, a heavier bag each aisle. That is the spine that keeps four
applications of a single idea from reading as the same chapter four
times.

## Chapter 5 — Volunteer for the Grenade

**Claim.** The problems everybody steps around are unowned rather
than above your pay grade, they come apart into ordinary tangible
parts the moment somebody picks one up, and the person holding one is
worth more to the building than the person clearing tickets.

Every office has a problem with no name on it. Not a task — a task
has an owner and a date. A problem: the handoff between two teams
that loses something every single time, sitting in a thread since
March, forty-one replies deep, described by everyone as "a known
issue" in the tone people reserve for potholes. The reader arrives
here having just been told the whole thing costs nothing and having
proved it for about eleven seconds — one question in one meeting, one
hand up first, one walk out of the room at 10:25. This chapter asks
for something longer than eleven seconds. Nobody is holding the hard
problem. That is not a hierarchy. That is the same vacuum from
chapter one, with a calendar attached.

The engine is the split, and it has to be demonstrated rather than
asserted. Morale is low, so the boss books a happy hour, buys the
first round, and is genuinely confused four weeks later when morale
is still low. A happy hour is a tangible answer to an intangible
problem, and applying one to the other is most of what management
looks like from underneath. So the book hands over its diagnostic,
**Tangible vs. Intangible Problems**, and the rule of thumb that a
difficult intangible problem is roughly eighty percent tangible
parts. Then it performs the split on the page, on the thread from
March: nobody owns the checklist, two systems call the same field two
different names, and the person who knew retired. Three tangible
problems, all boring, every one of them solvable by an adult with a
calendar. That demonstration is the chapter's receipt. Without it,
eighty-twenty is a number in a nice font.

Then the turn, and it is the first thing in this book that weighs
anything. The tag still reads zero — no budget, no permission, nobody
to ask. Chapter four promised that the tax comes off. It never
promised the thing you pick up is light. A grenade is work, in
public, on something that may not fully resolve, and you cannot put
it down quietly once your name is on it. For a while you are the
person associated with the ugly thing, and the chapter has to price
that honestly instead of winking at it. What comes back is not a
solution. It is that the room's map changes, and problems start
arriving at you before they arrive at your boss. Nobody is asking you
to defuse it. You are being asked to move it eight feet, which is
eight feet further than it has moved since March.

So the reader leaves holding something new and slightly inconvenient.
The unclaimed problem is claimable, it is made of ordinary parts, and
the person carrying one is worth more than the person finishing
tickets. They also leave knowing that free of tax was never the same
as free of work. And they leave looking at the next room, because the
biggest unclaimed space of all is not at your desk. It happens
Tuesday at ten, with five people in it, and it has been going for
thirty-four minutes.

## Chapter 6 — Hijack the Meeting

**Claim.** A drifting meeting is an unclaimed problem with witnesses,
and whoever moves five separate answers into one shape runs the room
without the title and without asking for it.

The reader walks in able to hold a problem alone and still carrying
one small assumption from Part I: a meeting is a room where you can
make a move. Ask the question, put your hand up first, take the first
donut. A move — not a machine you operate. This chapter hands them
the machine. A meeting where nobody is steering is the same unclaimed
space as last chapter's, except it runs fifty minutes, has witnesses,
and refills every Tuesday. And it drifts for the ordinary reason:
steering in public is a tax nobody in the room will pay, so the
meeting goes wherever the loudest confidence points it, and five
people file out believing five different things about what was
agreed.

There is a decision before the room, and the chapter spends one short
movement on it — Friday afternoon, at your desk, next week's
invitations stacked up like a hand of cards. Choosing which table you
sit at is the same skill one step earlier, and a table full of pros
is a bad place to make money. Then the room, and the set piece the
book has been holding back. **The Olympic Rings.** Picture each
person's solution as a ring, roughly concentric, barely overlapping,
and understand that the job is not to be right. The job is to slide
the rings until they sit on top of each other. The mechanism is
nothing but questions asked in order. Get agreement on the problem.
Then ask each person, by name, what they would do, and let the room
discover the thing it finds genuinely surprising, which is that most
people appear to believe a magical scribe has been transcribing their
inner monologue and emailing it around.

The turn is a trap, and it is where this chapter earns its place
instead of being a technique demo. Steering a room you do not own
looks like a power grab, and it becomes one the second you steer
toward your own answer. So the price is specific and it stings: you
have to give up being right. The move only works if you actually want
the rings aligned, including on the day they align on somebody else's
ring and yours is the one that gets slid. The second cost is less
glamorous, and the chapter should admit it, because this is slow.
Sometimes it takes three meetings, and the first one ends with the
room understanding only that it disagrees, which is progress that
feels exactly like failure. **Improvement over perfection.** And at
the time it will not feel like either.

The reader now believes a room is a workable unit, that alignment
beats being right, and that whoever asks the questions ends up
holding the pen whether or not anybody voted. Which is the moment the
floor gets thin, because there is one question nobody asked while the
rings were sliding into place. Not whether the five of them agree.
What they have all agreed to point at.

## Chapter 7 — Ask the Question Nobody Wants to Answer

**Claim.** The most expensive unasked question in your building is how
today's work connects to the actual plan, asking it out loud costs
nothing, and it is worth asking even in the case — especially in the
case — where the answer turns out not to exist.

By now the reader can hold a problem and steer a room, and they are
running on the last comfortable assumption they own: that somebody
above them has checked. The work arrived looking like a fact. It came
with a name, a deadline, and someone's confidence attached, and none
of that is evidence that it fits into anything. Chapter one said the
person one level up is at the job they are worst at. Chapter four
said the people beside you are not blind, only quiet. Chapter two
already showed what a beautiful chart of the wrong tasks is worth.
Put those together and you get an uncomfortable piece of arithmetic
about how much of the work in your building has ever been checked
against a plan by anybody at all.

So the book hands over its plainest move, and this is its only
definition anywhere. **BIU — Bump It Up.** Aligning the task in front
of you with the larger thing it is supposedly part of, out loud, in
the room, usually in one sentence. Once we finish this, what changes.
How does this fit the bigger push. What is the actual question we are
trying to answer. That last one is chapter one's vacuum question
grown from one meeting to a whole project, and it works for a reason
the chapter has to name or the move reads as a stunt: nobody else in
the room asked either. That is why the silence afterward is not
hostility. It is relief, arriving slowly, from four directions. There
is also a version for cowards, which is the version most people will
actually run — bump it up while explaining things to the new person,
and let the gap fall out of your own explanation.

Then the setback the whole Part has been walking toward, and it is
the Part's low point. You bump it up, and sometimes there is nothing
up there. The strategy is three words on a slide, or four people's
incompatible versions of the same sentence, and now you are the one
standing in a room holding that fact while everybody looks at you.
Chapter five's weight lands again in a bigger room: find the gap and
it is yours. Worse, the same question asked to win rather than to
know reads as a stall or a challenge, and the difference is audible
to every person present. The chapter has to teach the reader to ask
it the way you ask for directions, not the way you ask for a receipt.

The reader leaves believing alignment is a real, ownable, mostly
unowned problem, that the question is free, and that finding the hole
beats shipping into it. And they leave with one person still
untouched. Every move in this Part has routed around the boss — the
thread, the room, the plan, never the person. Part I taught the
reader to diagnose that person and step neatly past them. Nothing in
this book has yet suggested speaking to them on purpose.

## Chapter 8 — Manage Up or Get Managed

**Claim.** You will either run the communication between you and your
boss or inherit their version of it as your performance review, and
running it is a system you operate rather than a personality you
fake.

The reader arrives able to route around their boss with real skill,
having never once aimed anything at them. This chapter says the boss
is an interface, and interfaces accept input. Then it hands over the
observation that reframes the decade, and the reader can verify it
this week: your work does not travel. A description of it travels —
upstairs, from memory, on a Thursday, by somebody who was
half-listening, to people you have never met. Some of you does go up
directly, and the book already sold you those channels: the front-row
seat, the question in the full room, the higher-ups who start
wondering why your boss isn't the one asking. Those carry an
impression of you. The account of what you actually did goes up in
one bag, carried by one person, and whatever they can repeat is what
you did. The rest is a rumor about a spreadsheet.

So the chapter hands over the last three pieces of Part II's working
vocabulary, all homed here and nowhere earlier. **BLUF**, bottom line
up front, because a boss who has to read four paragraphs to find the
ask will carry four paragraphs of nothing up the stairs. **CFC**,
Crystal Fu\*king Clear, applied ruthlessly and out loud, because
clarity is not a courtesy you extend to other people — it is a
container you build for your own work so it survives being handled by
somebody else. And **NR**, non-reality, which is not a diagnosis of a
bad boss but a Thursday condition of every ambitious one: the passing
thought that arrives downstairs already believing it was assigned,
straight from a land of unicorns and butterflies, and is honestly
startled that nobody built it. Naming non-reality is the move. Naming
it kindly, in the room, in the form of a question about feasibility,
is the entire skill.

Then the objection the reader has been holding since the chapter
title, because this looks exactly like the thing the book banned.
Where is the line between managing up and sucking up, and who decides
where it sits. The test is the one chapter three built and never
named at this target: a hopeful move is aimed at being liked and then
waits, and a helpful move is aimed at making the work accurate and
does not care who likes it. Sucking up is the standing-out tax paid
in a different currency — fear, dressed as enthusiasm. Managing up
works identically on a boss you respect and one you do not, and that
is the tell. But there is a genuine price here, the heaviest in the
Part, and the chapter says it plainly: you are doing part of your
boss's job, and there are weeks they will get the credit for it. What
you are buying with those weeks is control of the account of your own
work. It is the first offer in this book a reader might turn down,
and the chapter has to let them.

The reader leaves with the boss relationship reframed as a system
whose inputs they own, and with one question the Part deliberately
refuses to answer. All four of these moves work the same whether the
person above you is faking it or is the best operator you will ever
meet. Chapter four flagged that the tax tells you to move without
telling you which direction, and back then it was a footnote for
later. It is not a footnote now. The reader is finally good enough at
this to spend two years pointed at the wrong person, doing everything
right. That is what the last Part is for.

## Block 4 — Super-concept tests, chapter by chapter

**SC2 — the standing-out tax.** Each chapter must reduce to it, in the
book's own language. Three verdicts are available: *reduces*,
*reduces then hands off to the read* (licensed only by CONCEPT.md's
LOCKED co-thesis, which says the tax does not decide direction), and
*fails*, which is a rung-3 problem to be fixed here rather than in
prose.

- **Ch. 5** — the hard problems are unclaimed because claiming one is
  visible and might fail in front of people. **Reduces.**
- **Ch. 6** — meetings drift because steering in public is taxed.
  **Reduces**, and it is the purest case in the Part.
- **Ch. 7** — the connecting question goes unasked because asking it
  implies nobody knows, in front of the people who should. **Reduces.**
- **Ch. 8** — managing up is taxed twice, by visibility and by the
  fear of looking like a suck-up. **Reduces, then hands off:** the
  move requires reading the person, and the tax is silent on
  direction. This is the designed exit of Part II.

**SC1 — the liberating diagnosis.** The test is whether the diagnosis
stays funny and freeing rather than curdling into grievance. Two live
risk sites. Ch. 7's setback (there is no plan up there) can read as
despair; the fix is that an empty strategy is the best news in the
chapter, because unowned means available. Ch. 8's price (your boss may
take the credit) is the Part's biggest SC1 risk and must be delivered
as a *trade the reader chooses*, in the amused register, never as a
complaint about a thief.

**SC3 — amused confidence, never anger.** Every "the room won't" is
scared, never lazy or stupid — that is ch04:62–74's LOCKED reframe and
it binds the Part. The five people in ch. 6 are not idiots; they are
five adults each holding a different ring in silence. Nothing in the
Part punches at a coworker, a peer, or the reader.

**SC4 — wins the flip test.** Part I's machinery is a box *and* a
pull-quote per chapter — PROPOSED as a convention, since SUPERCONCEPTS
names pull-quotes as SC4 machinery but sets no per-chapter quota and
only ch04's is on the record. Part II matches it: see Block 7, which names
one Cheap Power box and nominates one pull-quote for each of ch. 5–8.

## Block 5 — Venue roster (recurring settings)

Three recurring venues across the Part, no two confusable, each
chapter with one home and at most one cameo.

| Venue | Identity | Position relative to the others |
|---|---|---|
| **The thread** | Where written work and unclaimed problems rot in public — channel, doc, email chain | The only venue with a memory; things sit here for months |
| **The meeting room** | Where the room's real disagreements are visible for fifty minutes | Downstream of the thread; the thread's problems arrive here undefined |

starts). Same room, opposite ends of the clock, and that separation is
the anti-blur device; without it these two chapters read as one. Ch. 8
= the one-on-one.

**The poker table is staged in the calendar, never in the room** (D12).
Ch. 6's opening movement happens on Friday afternoon at your desk,
choosing which invitations to accept. If it drifts into a
before-the-meeting scene it occupies ch. 7's minute-two slot and the
anti-blur device is gone.

**Note for the roster's canon row.** The one-on-one already exists in

## Block 13 — Positioning: the objection this Part answers

*Carried forward from the revision-1 run (variance card A4,
contrarian comp). Fact-checked clean by the continuity gate; only the
pricing sentence is corrected here, to match Block 3.*

**The comp that says this book shouldn't work: *The Dilbert
Principle* (Scott Adams, 1996).** It made this book's acquisition joke
thirty years earlier, sold in the millions, and trained an entire
working culture to treat managerial incompetence as entertainment —
which is the problem. Dilbert proved the diagnosis sells and
simultaneously proved the diagnosis is inert: its reader laughs, pins
the strip to the cubicle wall, and does not move. A buyer with that
book in their memory has a fair objection, and it is the strongest one
on the shelf. "I already know my boss is faking it. That was a coffee
mug in 1998." (Second-order version of the same objection, from the
free end of the shelf: *Ask a Manager* answers "what do I do about my
boss" daily, searchably, for nothing.)

**How Part II answers it.** Dilbert never hands you a move; it hands
you company. Part II is four consecutive chapters in which the joke
*is* the instruction — every chapter ends with a specific
unauthorized thing you can do at 10:25 on Monday, at zero cost, and by
the fourth one the book is honest about what you are carrying out of
the store and asks whether you want it. Cynicism that issues a receipt
stops being cynicism. That is the whole difference, and it lives in
this Part or nowhere.
