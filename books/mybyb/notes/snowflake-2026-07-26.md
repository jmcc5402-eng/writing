## One-page overview

MYBYB is a gift-book-sized, gleefully funny survival guide for your
first decade in the workforce, aimed at the 22–30 corporate/hybrid
reader who is cynical but ambitious. The shock title and cover earn the
gag purchase; the substance — real frameworks under the jokes — earns
the five-star "bought it as a joke, it's actually the realest career
advice I've gotten" review. The tone is amused confidence, never anger:
the reader looks down at a comically beatable obstacle, and the bite
points at incompetence, broken systems, and fear — never at people,
coworkers, or groups.

The argument is one liberating secret told in two halves. Half one, the
acquisition thesis: your boss is probably faking it. The org chart is
not a competence ranking; people rise to the job they're worst at, so
the space above you is empty. Half two, the retention thesis, promoted
from a sidebar to the load-bearing engine: the reason nobody has taken
that empty space is that everyone is terrified of standing out — the
fear of standing out is a tax the average pay every single day, and the
tax is voluntary. Faking-it explains why the space above you is empty;
the tax explains why you can have it.

Part I (Wake the F\* Up) is a closing trap built on a rotating lens —
the boss, the scoreboard, you, the crowd — and each chapter removes one
excuse. Chapter 1: the person above you didn't earn the space above
you. Chapter 2: the system won't notice your effort, because activity
is the currency of fakers. Chapter 3: waiting isn't a strategy — hope
is your own faker tell, and quiet quitting is hope wearing sunglasses.
Chapter 4, the thesis-turn: acting isn't risky — the risk was the tax,
the 90% aren't wrong but scared, and the tax is voluntary.

With every excuse gone, Part II (Take the Wheel) stops being career
tips and becomes the same purchase repeated: five public moves the room
is too scared to make, picked up off the floor — the grenade, the
meeting, the question, the boss relationship itself. Part III (Play the
Decade) adds the maturity beat that keeps the book from being one-note:
not every boss is a loser. Read gas pedal versus ride the wave — beat
the anchor, but strap in behind the rocket, as a cold bet with an exit,
never bootlicking. Then compound: useful skills over impressive ones,
aligned teams over lone heroics, a network that is your exit, and a
pre-decided walk-away point.

The closer is the true thesis: you are not trapped. Beat the boss, ride
the boss, or quietly stop needing one — it has never been cheaper to
build your own boat. A door, not a manual.

Between chapters, boxed Cheap Power interstitials (the Donut Move, the
5:01 Walk) prove the tax thesis in screenshot-sized miniatures — the
bits people text each other, which is the word-of-mouth engine.

## Four-paragraph overview

**Setup.** The book opens by leaking the secret nobody says out loud:
people get promoted until they reach a job they're bad at, and then
they stay. The person above you is statistically at the exact job they
are worst at — not a tragedy, the best news of your career. Part I
teaches the reader to see it (the Things-vs-Hope faker tells), then
widens the indictment: the scoreboard is fake too, because activity is
the currency of fakers, and Slack theater, RTO presence politics, and
"catching up on email" are counterfeit money that AI is quietly
shredding anyway.

**Escalation one — the lens turns inward.** Just as the reader settles
into laughing at the boss, chapter 3 hands them the mirror: hope —
waiting to be noticed, waiting for the promotion cycle, waiting for
fair — is the reader's own faker tell. You are managing your career the
way a bad boss manages a project, and quiet quitting is the average
person's trap. That torches the last comfortable position and forces
the question chapter 4 exists to answer: if hope is out and action is
in, what's actually stopping you? Fear of standing out. Chapter 4
prices that fear — everyone is terrified of standing out, so standing
out is free power — and reframes the Bell Curve on the guardrail's
terms: the 90% aren't wrong, they're scared. Same math, opposite
target, and the only version that supports the argument.

**Escalation two — the argument applied, then complicated.** Part II is
the tax thesis run as a menu: volunteer for the intangible grenade,
hijack the drifting meeting, bump the question up to strategy, manage
up before you get managed — each one a public act the room is too
scared to perform, each one free. Then Part III raises the stakes from
moves to decade: the asterisk chapter admits some bosses are rockets,
and the real skill is reading gas pedal versus ride-the-wave — outshine
the anchor, but backfill the rocket and get towed up the wake, a cold
bet with an exit clause. The long game compounds: learn the useful
thing, stop pulling the wagon alone, build the network that is your
exit, and know exactly when to walk.

**Resolution.** The closer reveals the frame the whole book was inside:
beat the boss and ride the boss are both ways to win someone else's
game, and maybe the problem is the game. Refusing to need a boss is the
ultimate un-paid tax — and it has never, in the entire history of
humans, been cheaper to build your own boat. No startup manual, just
permission and a nudge: build the little boat beside the big one, and
when it holds your weight, let go of the rope — this time onto the
thing you built. The reader leaves with the same amused confidence the
book opened with, now aimed at their whole decade.

## Chapter-by-chapter

Numbering follows the CONCEPT.md spine: Part I = ch. 1–3, Part II =
ch. 4–8, Part III = ch. 9–13, Closer = ch. 14. Thesis draw is tagged
per chapter: (A) = acquisition/faking-it, (R) = retention/the tax,
(G) = gas-vs-wave read.

1. **Your Boss Is Probably Faking It** *(Part I — drafted; A)* — The
   org chart is not a competence ranking: people rise to their highest
   level of incompetence, so the gap between your boss's title and
   ability is opportunity, not oppression. Home of the acquisition
   thesis and the Things-vs-Hope tells, with the Peter Principle open,
   the improv-scene bit, and the vacuum move ("What's the actual
   problem we're solving?") — one repair pending: the 90% group-chat
   line retargets from contempt to fear.

2. **Busy Is Not the Same as Good** *(A → R)* — The scoreboard is fake
   too: activity is the currency of fakers, so effort spent looking
   busy is counterfeit money and value is the only real ledger. Native
   home of the modern threads — Slack performance-theater, RTO presence
   politics, AI quietly eating busywork, the email "catch-up" ritual
   replaced by "Hey Bill, what did I miss this week?" — and it hands
   forward the question of why everyone, the reader included, still
   performs.

3. **Hope Is a Loser's Strategy** *(reader pivot; A → R)* — The lens
   turns on the reader: hope — waiting to be noticed, waiting for the
   cycle, waiting for fair — is your own faker tell, and quiet quitting
   is hope wearing sunglasses. It calls back ch. 1's Hope tell, spends
   "Bosses need to be helpful, not hopeful." verbatim before flipping
   it inward, and ends on the question ch. 4 prices: what stops action
   is fear of standing out.

4. **Do the Exact Opposite of Everyone (the Bell Curve)** *(Part II
   opener, functioning as the thesis-turn; R)* — The retention thesis
   promoted from the sidebar: everyone is terrified of standing out, so
   standing out is free power — the 90% aren't wrong, they're scared,
   and the fear tax is voluntary. The Difference Bell Curve rebuilt on
   fear plus "To change, you must do things differently.", with the
   ch. 1 callback (the vacuum exists *because* of the tax) turning the
   rest of Part II into a priced menu.

5. **Volunteer for the Grenade** *(R, with A underneath)* — Own the
   hard, undefined, intangible problems everyone dodges — that's where
   the money is, and the field is empty because fakers are allergic to
   intangible and peers are paying the tax. Home of Tangible vs.
   Intangible Problems and the 80/20 rule of intangibles, defined here
   and nowhere earlier.

6. **Hijack the Meeting** *(R)* — Meetings drift because steering in
   public is a tax nobody will pay, so the person who moves everyone's
   ring into alignment owns the room without holding the title. Home of
   the Olympic Rings framework; the move works identically under a
   faker or a rocket, which is why the tax, not the faker hook, powers
   it.

7. **Ask the Question Nobody Wants to Answer (Bump It Up)** *(R)* —
   Connect today's task to the actual strategy, out loud, in the room —
   the question everyone is thinking and nobody will spend status on.
   Sole definition site of BIU, escalating ch. 1's vacuum question from
   one meeting to the whole project.

8. **Manage Up or Get Managed** *(R, with A underneath)* — Run the
   communication your boss should be running — bottom line up front,
   crystal clarity, naming non-reality when it drifts in — or inherit
   their chaos as your performance review. Reserved-lexicon home: CFC,
   BLUF, and NR live here and must not be defined earlier.

9. **Learn the Useful Thing, Not the Impressive Thing** *(Part III —
   G)* — The decade view opens: chase the skill that compounds, not the
   one that demos well — smart, not smarts, with AI as the thing you
   wield rather than the thing you fear. First chapter powered by the
   long-game read: what's worth learning depends on where you're going,
   not on what the room applauds.

10. **Don't Pull the Wagon Alone (the Stagecoach)** *(G)* — Everyone
    pulling in a different direction works harder and moves the wagon
    slower, so alignment beats lone heroics — stop being the strongest
    horse on a stuck stagecoach. Home of the Stagecoach framework,
    extending the decade lens from your skills to your team.

11. **Some Bosses You Beat. Some You Wakeboard Behind.** *(drafted;
    G — Part III's engine)* — The maturity beat: not every boss is a
    loser — read gas pedal vs. ride the wave, because riding a rocket
    is a cold, confident bet on a winner with an exit clause, never
    bootlicking. Drafted, but needs voice-spec retrofit — it argues
    almost entirely by antithesis, so add one fresh office prop per
    section and a release valve. [CHECK: draft file is numbered 09;
    CONCEPT.md seats this chapter third in Part III (position 11) —
    confirm the intended slot before final numbering.]

12. **Your Network Is Your Exit** *(G + R)* — The durable asset of the
    decade is the people who've watched you work, not the org that
    happens to employ you — and every tax-free move in Part II was
    also a public audition. Build the network before you need it, so
    the exit is a door you already hold the handle of.

13. **Know Exactly When to Walk** *(G)* — Leaving is a move, not a
    failure: pre-decide the exit conditions — the wave flattened, the
    anchor is sinking, the learning stopped — and walk on your timing,
    not theirs. The gas-vs-wave read matured into a discipline, with
    "let go of the rope" primed for the closer's reversal.

14. **Closer — Build Your Own Damn Boat** *(the true thesis; R's final
    form)* — You are not trapped: beat the boss, ride the boss, or
    quietly stop needing one, because it has never been cheaper to
    build your own boat — the ultimate un-paid tax. Drafted as a door,
    not a manual; pending micro-fixes: cut or name the "Somebody wanted
    you to assume the difference" up-punch, and keep the one-hour
    Monday splash.

### The escalation contract (all four chapters)

Rotating lens: ch. 1 the boss → ch. 2 the scoreboard → ch. 3 you →
ch. 4 the crowd. Each chapter removes exactly one excuse and hands an
explicit open question to the next:

- After ch. 1 the reader can't believe *the person above me earned the
  space above me* — hands off: if the boss fakes, is the scoreboard
  fake too?
- After ch. 2 they can't believe *the system will notice my effort* —
  hands off: why does everyone (you included) still perform? Because
  they're hoping to be noticed.
- After ch. 3 they can't believe *waiting is a strategy* — hands off:
  hope's out, so what stops action? Fear of standing out. Next chapter
  prices it.
- After ch. 4 they can't believe *acting is risky* — the risk was the
  tax, and the tax is voluntary; Part II becomes a priced menu.

Do not re-diagnose the boss after ch. 1; a chapter that aims at the
previous chapter's target is a repeat, not an escalation.

### Voice spec (binding; full 17 rules in the voice analysis)

- Second person, present tense, accomplice one row back — never guru,
  never surprised, never angry. No exclamation points anywhere.
- Rhythm: build 20–40 words, then snap; big beats as one-line
  paragraphs; fragments only as punches, max two, descending length.
- The turn lands on the last 1–8 words of its unit and is never
  explained afterward; setup is 1–3 sentences of verifiably true
  observation.
- Targets: incompetence, rituals, systems, fear — never coworkers,
  groups, identities, or the reader; any "X% of people" is scared,
  never stupid or lazy; no vague "somebody/they" oppressors (never
  up-punch).
- Density floors and caps, per chapter unless noted: ≥1 hyper-specific
  office-prop collision per `##` section; ≥1 self-deflating release
  valve; exactly 1 literalized physical metaphor whose physics are
  obeyed; ≤2 antithesis aphorisms; "just…" ellipsis ≤1; bold = thesis
  only (≤2 per section); italics/scare quotes ≤2 uses each per page.
- Comedy rent: sincerity only after a laugh in the same section.
- Chapter shape: cold-open leaked secret → bold thesis → one-line
  payoff → 2–4 sections → "The point" (half-register drop) → Monday
  move blockquote (concrete, zero-cost, runnable Monday). Vary the
  closing image — "take the wheel" is spent after ch. 1. Suggested
  lanes: ch. 2 a ledger/counterfeit image, ch. 3 the mirror, ch. 4 the
  floor ("pick it up off the floor").
- Cheap Power boxes: one image, one turn, one line out — never a list.
