## What the prose is doing well

The re-label is a real comic instrument here, not a mannerism:
"It's a timestamp with feelings," "a word that sounds like science and
works like weather," "It's cardio: effort, motion, zero displacement."
Each lands a full argument inside a noun phrase, which is exactly what
a flip-test gift book needs.
The confessional release valves do more work than their word count
suggests — "So have I. Mine had *three* follow-up bullets" and "Four
words. I rehearsed them" are what keep SC3's amused confidence from
tipping into lecture, because the narrator keeps putting himself on the
wrong side of his own diagnosis.
And the money metaphor is disciplined: counterfeit → ledger → invoice →
tax → receipt → money on the floor runs four chapters without a single
mixed image.

**Where the line was drawn.** A shape is a DEVICE when its *content*
changes every time and the reader is meant to recognize the move (T1,
T2, T7, T9, T10) — that is the book's spoken-comedy grammar, and
stripping it would flatten the voice.
A shape is a TIC when the *wording* is what repeats, or when a formula
that should vary has hardened into one slot (T3 in the boxes, T4's
narrow variant, T12, T13).
T2 at n=24 looks alarming on a spreadsheet, but it is the argument
itself — the whole book is "you have priced this wrong, here is the
right price" — so every instance stayed.

## The pattern worth watching

This book's risk is not weak sentences. It is **formula hardening in
the slots where formula is supposed to live.** Every tic here was a good
structure that stopped varying: the box closer, the chapter opener, the
smug pause, the chapter-end handoff, the precise-number gag.
The device/tic line in a voice like this is almost never "is this shape
overused" — it is "is the *wording* what repeats, or the *idea*." The
four repeatable structures that carry the book all pass, because their
content changes every time. The five flagged all failed for one reason:
the same clause, redeployed.

Practical form: before writing the next six Cheap Power boxes or the
Part II chapter openers, grep the existing ones for their first and
last lines. Two identical openers is a signature; three is a template;
by four the reader is reading the scaffolding instead of the joke.
