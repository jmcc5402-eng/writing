## 1. THE REVIEW

**Outlet:** *Shelf Awareness* (Pro edition), starred lead review — the
trade sheet booksellers read to decide what gets stocked where. Chosen
because a gift book lives or dies at retail, and this is the register
that tells a buyer "face-out, by the register."

> **[TK: title]** by [TK: author] ([TK: publisher], $[TK: price])
>
> Shelve it in Humor. Stack it by the register for graduation season
> and again in December. And do not let the gag cover fool you into
> the novelty ghetto, because this is the rare gift book that gets
> read past the parking lot.
>
> The pitch fits in one breath, which is exactly what a handsell
> needs: your boss is probably faking it. People get promoted until
> they reach a job they're bad at, and then they stay. The genius is
> what the book does with that diagnosis — no rage, no HR-safe mush.
> The boss here is never a villain, just "a person doing an improv
> scene called 'In Charge,'" and that one swap — dread out, comedy
> in — powers all fourteen chapters.
>
> It passes the flip test from any direction. Open at random and you
> hit a boxed "Cheap Power" move, a pull-quote, or a bit you'll
> photograph for the group chat. ★ I opened to the Donut Move — be
> first to take one, because "you are now the most relaxed,
> highest-status person in the room, and you achieved it with a
> pastry" — and had performed it at our own staff meeting by Friday.
> The chapter on performative busyness is the read-aloud champion:
> the wiggled mouse, the badge-swipe scoreboard, the 9:47 p.m. Slack
> message that "could have been sent at 9:00 the next morning with no
> change to the fate of the deck." ★ I read the verdict on that
> message — "It's a timestamp with feelings" — out loud to three
> coworkers before I'd finished the page, which is the whole
> word-of-mouth engine of this book operating exactly as designed.
>
> But the reason it earns five-star reviews instead of a chuckle is
> the idea underneath the jokes. ★ The book's real thesis follows you
> to work on Monday: "the fear of standing out is a tax the average
> pay every single day, and you can just… not pay it" — and once
> you've heard it, you cannot watch typing dots appear, hesitate, and
> vanish in a meeting chat without seeing somebody pay up. Nobody's
> auditing, the book keeps insisting, and it's right. From there the
> back half becomes a menu of moves the room is too scared to make —
> volunteer for the ugly problem, steer the drifting meeting, ask how
> today's task connects to the strategy *[flagged: Part II ch. 5–8
> undrafted — praised here as spine promise only]* — before the
> wakeboard chapter turns the whole book on its head: some bosses are
> rockets, and "You don't beat the boat. That's not how wakeboarding
> works."
>
> That turn is why the tone never curdles. ★ The bite lands on broken
> systems and fear — the cubicle middle of the bell curve "is not a
> ranking of ability. It's a heat map of fear" — so your actual boss
> can read it over your shoulder and laugh, which makes this the only
> workplace comedy in the store you can gift upward. And the closer
> quietly reveals the book was never anti-boss at all: beat the boss,
> ride the boss, or build your own damn boat. ★ "Every door you
> thought was locked was just closed" is the sentence our twenty-
> something booksellers have been quoting at each other all month —
> and I bought five copies before the staff meeting ended: two for
> the new-grad table, one for a nephew starting in consulting, one
> for our events coordinator, and one, face-out, for me.
>
> Handsell it to: new grads, anyone 22–30 with a lanyard, the
> coworker just promoted into management (yes, really — that's the
> trick of it), and every office Secret Santa under $15.

*Review body ~540 words.*

## 3. FINDINGS

### (a) ★ moments no current super concept covers — candidates

- **CANDIDATE C-A — The Door (the closer's Build-Your-Own-Damn-Boat
  turn).** The fifth ★ — the arc that plants "the final boss has a
  health bar," complicates it with the rocket, then revokes the game
  itself ("maybe the problem is the *game*") — is a named, specific,
  three-beat payoff no SC owns. SC1–SC4 are all page- and
  chapter-level; nothing protects the book-length turn that makes the
  rave quote the *ending*. Concrete test: does this chapter make the
  closer's reveal stronger, weaker, or neither?
- **CANDIDATE C-B — The Signature Move.** The reviewer *performs* the
  Donut Move in-store. SC4 covers boxes-as-flip-bait; it does not
  demand one definitive, named, physically-performable set-piece the
  brand is known by (the way "the marshmallow test" travels). The
  Donut Move is behaving like that asset; nothing in canon says so.

### (b) Super concepts generating no ★ — suspect

- **SC1 (the liberating diagnosis)** produced the review's *pitch*
  paragraph but no ★ sentence. Consistent with THREADS T01 ("the hook
  only powers the front third" — retention passes to T02 by design),
  so not broken — but as written, SC1's chapter test guards tone
  drift, not tingle. Suspect as worded: either accept it as the
  acquisition-only concept or sharpen its test toward the improv-scene
  register that actually got quoted.
