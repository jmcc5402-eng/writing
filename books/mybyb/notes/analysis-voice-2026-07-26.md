## 1. The voice fingerprint

### 1.1 Sentence rhythm: build, then snap

The signature move is a long, syntactically busy build (20–40 words, often
with an em-dash swerve or a parenthetical aside) that gets snapped by a short
punch — frequently a fragment, frequently a one-line paragraph:

> They don't get promoted *out* of the job they're bad at, because, well,
> they're bad at it. So they stay. Forever. Marinating.

and the follow-through, given its own paragraph:

> That's the best news of your entire career.

The rhythm is a staircase: each fragment shorter than the last, the final
step a single word. This happens at every scale — inside sentences (em-dash
pivots), between sentences (long/short alternation), and between paragraphs
(the one-liner payoff paragraph). When the draft is at its best, no two
consecutive sentences have the same length class.

### 1.2 Paragraph and chapter shape

All three chapters share a repeatable architecture:

1. Cold-open claim stated as a secret being leaked ("nobody will say it out
   loud, so I will").
2. Bolded thesis sentence.
3. One-line-paragraph payoff.
4. Two to four `##` sections with verb-forward or image-forward headers
   ("Let it sink in," "The move: walk into the vacuum," "The wakeboard").
5. A "The point" section that drops half a register toward sincerity.
6. Horizontal rule, then a blockquoted **Monday move** — one concrete,
   zero-cost action.

The critique (rec 6) correctly notes this is already a template at n=4: three
of four drafts close punch-paragraph + Monday move, and two land on a "take
the wheel" variant. Verified on the page — ch01 "Then go take the wheel" and
ch09 "Then take the wheel — or grab the rope."

### 1.3 Typography as voice

- **Bold** is reserved for thesis sentences — the sentence you'd screenshot.
- *Italics* carry sarcastic stress and quoted faker-speech (*"I was hoping
  this would be further along."*).
- Scare quotes flag corporate ritual as performance: the boss who
  "gives you feedback."
- The ellipsis-plus-"just" is a recurring deadpan hesitation: "A faker
  just… hopes at you, from a distance"; "just… sitting there"; "*I could
  just… do my own thing*"; "you can just… not pay it."
- No exclamation points anywhere in the reboot prose. The old 2014 master
  used them ("Live in Reality — Please!"); the reboot's deadpan depends on
  their absence. This is one of the clearest old-voice/new-voice tells.

### 1.4 Register

Second person, present tense, conspiratorial. The narrator is not a guru
above the reader but a slightly older accomplice one row back, leaning over:
in on a funny secret, never surprised, never mad. Profanity is implied or
censored in titles ("Wake the F\* Up," "Build Your Own Damn Boat") and absent
from body prose — the edge is in the ideas, not the diction. The master's
register (earnest seminar-speak: "MYBYB is a lifestyle and it is catching
on") is fully gone; nothing in the reboot addresses the reader as a student.

## 2. Joke construction

### 2.1 Setup length and turn placement

The standard joke is **1–3 sentences of verifiably true observation, then a
turn in the final clause** — the laugh lands on the last 1–8 words of the
paragraph and is never explained afterward:

> They are not a genius gatekeeper standing between you and success. They
> are a person doing an improv scene called "In Charge," and they are hoping
> nobody asks a follow-up question.

Setup = flat truth; turn = a hyper-specific concrete image ("improv scene,"
the follow-up question). The book almost never front-loads the joke; the
funny word is the last word. When a paragraph continues past its turn, the
energy dies — the Pre-Break Pee's three numbered points each bury a good
turn under a moral (the critique's "over-narrated" call is confirmed; a
numbered list is structurally incompatible with the snap rhythm, even though
it contains a top-ten line: "Your bladder is not a team player and
pretending otherwise helps no one.").

### 2.2 What makes it laugh-out-loud vs. smile

**LOL-tier** jokes in this draft always have all three of:

1. a *concrete office prop* named with absurd precision — "a Slack channel
   called `#alignment`," "a 40-slide deck," the donut, "logs off at 5:01";
2. a *status reversal* — the small thing beats the big thing (a pastry
   confers command of the room; the boss is an improv performer);
3. *terminal placement* — the prop detonates at the end of the unit.

**Smile-tier** lines are structural rather than concrete: balanced
antitheses and aphorisms — "It was a bet, not a marriage," "a *door, not a
manual*," "Rocket or anchor." These are quotable and useful, but they argue;
they don't collide. Chapter 9 is built almost entirely from them (gas/wave,
rocket/anchor — the vehicles are the framework itself, not fresh props),
which is exactly why it reads as the least funny chapter. Verified against
the critique with one amendment: ch09 is not joke-free — "The correct and
delicious answer is *you.*" is a genuine turn ("delicious" is the
prop-precision word) — but it is the only one, and "Rockets don't launch
without a second stage" is, as the critique says, a poster. The closer has
more comedy than the critique credits: beyond "post a screenshot of your
resignation, and set your savings on fire," both "You can be the founder,
the department, and the intern before lunch" and the "smallest, dumbest,
most embarrassing version of your own boat" carry real laughs.

### 2.3 The release valve

The single most important tonal safety device: a self-directed joke that
deflates the narrator right after a flex, so amused confidence never curdles
into smugness:

> It's a genuinely fun party trick and it will make you insufferable at
> brunch. Worth it.

The critique is right that there is "nowhere near enough" of this — one
clean instance in ch01, arguably zero in ch09 and the closer. The valve
should be a per-chapter requirement (spec rule 12 below).

## 3. Tone-rule audit

CONCEPT.md: "Amused confidence, not anger… the reader looks **down** at a
comically beatable obstacle and laughs, never **up** at an oppressor," and
the guardrail: bite at "*incompetence and broken systems* — never at people
or identities"; banned: "Punching down at people, coworkers, or any group."

## 4. Where the voice thins (verification of the critique)

- "Chapter 9 has almost no jokes" — **confirmed with one amendment** (see
  2.2). Root cause identified: ch09 argues by antithesis instead of by prop
  collision. The fix is mechanical: one fresh office prop per section.
- "The closer runs on sincerity with one real laugh" — **undercounted**;
  three laugh-tier lines (2.2). But the diagnosis stands directionally: the
  last third leans on aphorism density that reads, in the critique's apt
  phrase about ch09, "indistinguishable from LinkedIn" when props run out.
- "Pre-Break Pee is over-narrated" — **confirmed.** The proposed rule ("one
  image, one turn, one line out. Never a list.") is adopted into the spec.
- Endings templating — **confirmed on the page** (1.2).

## 5. VOICE SPEC (numbered — ghostwriter-followable)

1. **Person and stance.** Second person, present tense. The narrator is an
   accomplice leaning over from one row back, in on a funny secret — never a
   guru, never surprised, never angry.
2. **Rhythm.** Build 20–40 words, then snap. Every long sentence is followed
   by a short one. Big beats land as one-line paragraphs.
3. **Fragments** are legal only as punches, max two in a row, descending
   length ("So they stay. Forever. Marinating.").
4. **Turn placement.** The joke detonates on the last 1–8 words of its unit
   and is never explained afterward. If a paragraph continues past its turn,
   cut the continuation or move the turn.
5. **Setup budget.** 1–3 sentences of verifiably true observation before any
   turn. Accuracy buys the laugh; the reader must nod before they snort.
6. **Targets.** Bite lands on incompetence, rituals, systems, and fear —
   never on named groups, coworkers, identities, or the reader. Any
   "X% of people" is *scared*, never stupid or lazy.
7. **Props.** Every LOL-tier joke contains one hyper-specific office prop
   (`#alignment`, 40-slide deck, 5:01 logoff, the donut). Generic nouns
   produce smiles at best. Minimum one fresh prop collision per `##`
   section, including Part III.
8. **Deadpan hygiene.** No exclamation points. Sarcasm carried by italics
   and scare quotes, rationed (≤2 uses each per page). Profanity censored in
   titles, essentially absent in prose — the edge is conceptual.
9. **Typography.** Bold = thesis sentences only (≤2 per section).
   Blockquotes = say-this-out-loud sample lines and the Monday move. Nothing
   else gets decorated.
10. **The "just…" hesitation** (deadpan ellipsis) at most once per chapter.
11. **Metaphors are load-bearing and literalized.** One physical system per
    chapter (health bar; boat/rope/wake); obey its physics ("You don't beat
    the boat. That's not how wakeboarding works."); callbacks reuse the
    object with its meaning changed (the rope: escape hatch in ch09 →
    graduation in the closer).
12. **Release valve — mandatory.** At least one self-directed deflation per
    chapter ("insufferable at brunch. Worth it.") so confidence never reads
    smug.
13. **Aphorism cap.** Antithesis lines ("a bet, not a marriage") are
    closers, not filler — max two per chapter, or the page turns into
    LinkedIn.
14. **Comedy rent.** Sincerity is allowed only after a laugh in the same
    section. Part III floor: no spread without one prop-collision joke.
15. **Chapter shape.** Cold-open leaked secret → bold thesis → one-line
    payoff → 2–4 sections → "The point" (half-register drop) → Monday move
    blockquote (concrete, zero-cost, runnable Monday). Vary the closing
    image; "take the wheel" is spent after ch01.
16. **Never up-punch.** No vague "somebody/they" pulling strings; conspiracy
    flavoring reads as grievance, which CONCEPT bans as low-status.
17. **Interstitials (Cheap Power).** One image, one turn, one line out.
    Never a numbered list. Each proves "standing out is free power" in
    miniature.

## 6. COMEDY DEVICE LIST (one verbatim example each)

   Marinating." (ch01)
2. **One-line-paragraph payoff:** "That's the best news of your entire
   career." (ch01)
3. **Improv-scene reframe** (authority as performance): "They are a person
   doing an improv scene called "In Charge," and they are hoping nobody asks
   a follow-up question." (ch01)
4. **Verbed abstraction / anthropomorphism:** "A faker just… hopes at you,
   from a distance, and then acts betrayed when hoping doesn't ship the
   project." (ch01)
5. **Hyper-specific prop list, absurd escalation:** "a new dashboard, a
   reorg, a Slack channel called `#alignment`, a 40-slide deck, an offsite."
   (ch01)
6. **Release-valve self-mockery:** "It's a genuinely fun party trick and it
   will make you insufferable at brunch. Worth it." (ch01)
7. **Video-game register:** "The final boss has a health bar. Aim for it."
   (ch01)
8. **Literalized extended metaphor:** "You don't beat the boat. That's not
   how wakeboarding works." (ch09)
9. **Prop-precision adjective turn:** "The correct and delicious answer is
   *you.*" (ch09)
10. **Antithesis aphorism (smile-tier closer):** "It was a bet, not a
    marriage." (ch09)
11. **Mock ceremony / deadpan congratulation:** "Congratulations — you are
    now the most relaxed, highest-status person in the room, and you
    achieved it with a pastry." (cheap-power)
12. **Object-as-evidence reframe:** "The donut is just the receipt."
    (cheap-power)
13. **Flat one-word verdicts:** "Revolutionary. Wildly underrated."
    (cheap-power)
14. **Deadpan ellipsis hesitation:** "the fear of standing out is a tax the
    average pay every single day, and you can just… not pay it."
    (cheap-power)
15. **Hyperbole delivered flat:** "It has never, in the entire history of
    humans, been cheaper or faster to build your own boat." (closer)
16. **Compression gag (scale collapse):** "You can be the founder, the
    department, and the intern before lunch." (closer)
17. **Anti-heroics deflation:** "That's not brave — that's just a different
    way to lose." (closer)
18. **Callback with changed meaning:** "let go of the rope" — escape hatch
    in ch09, graduation in the closer ("when your own boat can finally hold
    your weight, *let go of the rope.*").
