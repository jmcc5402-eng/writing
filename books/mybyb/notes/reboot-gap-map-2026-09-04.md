### Structural audit — turning-point positions

Against `studio/craft/hauge.md`'s five positions, mapped onto a
fourteen-chapter argument.

The braid is sound. The outer goal has a visible finish line (you are
not trapped: beat, ride, or stop needing a boss — `99...md:41`), and
the inner journey runs identity → essence as *the average who pay the
tax* → *the operator who doesn't.* The closer is genuinely unwinnable
from inside the identity: a reader still paying the tax cannot build
the boat. That braid is the book's spine and it holds.

The positions:

- **TP1, the opportunity (~10%):** ch. 1's diagnosis. Lands at ~7%. On
  time.
- **TP2, change of plans (~25%):** ch. 4's tax turn — "the risk was the
  tax, and the tax is voluntary." Lands at ~28%. On time, and it is the
  best-built turn in the manuscript.
- **TP3, the point of no return (50%):** **missing.** The midpoint of a
  fourteen-chapter book falls at ch. 7, and nothing in the spine or the
  snowflake designs a commitment there — ch. 7 is currently just the
  third item on ch. 4's menu. This is a real pacing finding, and it is
  why Part II risks reading flat: four chapters described in the book's
  own words as "the same purchase, repeated" (`ch04...md:135`) with no
  beat where the reader crosses a line they cannot uncross. **The fix
  is free and it is in section 5:** give ch. 7 midpoint duty. Asking,
  out loud, what a project is actually for is the one move in Part II
  that cannot be un-asked — after it, the reader is publicly the person
  who asks, which is an identity change, not another purchase.
- **TP4, the major setback (~75%):** ch. 11. Lands at ~78%, and it is
  the right kind — everything the reader learned is wrong for this
  boss. Well placed; protect it.
- **TP5, climax (~90%+):** ch. 13 into the closer. Positioned
  correctly, undrafted at ch. 13.

**Superior position.** Non-fiction's version of dramatic irony is the
book's comedy engine: the reader can see the faker and the faker
cannot, and ch. 1 spends the gap deliberately — "Once you can see
Things and Hope, you can't un-see them" (`ch01...md:84`). Part I spends
it well on every page. **It is squandered in ch. 11,** which contains
no one for the reader to be ahead of: the rocket boss is described, not
observed. That is the root cause of the chapter's comedy failure, and
it is a more useful note than "add jokes."

**Echoes.** Strong and deliberate: the rope (escape hatch in ch. 11 →
graduation in the closer, `THREADS.md:273-275`); 5:01 (the box in ch. 1
→ the happy nine-to-fiver in the closer); the vacuum (opened in ch. 1,
explained in ch. 4, inverted in ch. 11); and the money chain —
counterfeit → ledger → invoice → tax → receipt → money on the floor —
running four chapters unmixed.

**Plants that echo nowhere,** all from `LEXICON.md` and all listed
dormant in `THREADS.md:448-468`: "Improvement over perfection.";
"Mission / Vision / Values = Crap / Crap / Crap"; "A land of unicorns

Ideology. Sections 5 and 6 propose homes for three of them and

### What the difference is, in terms a drafter can act on

**The strong passages put a photographable object in the reader's hand
and detonate on the last words of the unit. The weak ones name a
relationship between two abstractions and then explain it.**

Concretely, three rules a Part II drafter can check against:

1. **One object per section, and it must be specific enough to
   photograph.** "9:47 p.m.", "an improv scene called 'In Charge'",
   "eleven seconds" — all are things a camera could point at. "Rocket,"
   "anchor," "second stage" are categories wearing costumes: the
   vehicle *is* the framework, so the metaphor illustrates the argument
   instead of colliding with it. Nothing collides, so nothing lands.
   The test: could you draw this sentence?
2. **The funniest words are the last words of the unit, and nothing
   follows them.** Passage 1 ends on "feelings"; passage 2 ends on
   "follow-up question"; passage 3 ends on "leadership." The bulleted
   gas/wave definition ends on "get towed" and then keeps going for
   three more paragraphs of the same idea. If a paragraph continues
   past its turn, the turn moves or the continuation goes.
3. **Never state the framework as a list in the body.** The book's own
   rule for interstitials — one image, one turn, one line out, never a
   list (`LEXICON.md:70-76`; `analysis-voice:226-228`) — is a rule about
   rhythm, and body prose obeys the same physics. A bulleted definition
   cannot build-and-snap; it can only enumerate. Ch. 11 proves it:
   `:33-36` is the least funny sixty words in the manuscript and it is
   the only bulleted definition in it.

Fourth, narrower, aimed at the seams: `ch04...md:5-8` and
`ch03...md:154-159` fall out of voice for a different reason — they are
**recap register**, not weak prose. They address a reader who is
reading in order, in a book whose entire acquisition model
(`CONCEPT.md:52`, `SUPERCONCEPTS.md:47-48`) assumes the reader opened
to a random page. A recap is a page that cannot pass the flip test by
construction. Budget: at most one per Part, and never as a chapter's
first eight lines.

## 5. THE NEXT THREE CHAPTERS

**Chapters 5, 6, and 7, in spine order.** Three reasons for that order,
in descending weight.

First, ch. 4's menu (`ch04...md:124-130`) names the four Part II moves
in a fixed sequence — the ugly problem, the meeting, the strategy
question, the boss relationship — and that sequence is a promise the
reader has already read. Drafting out of order means either honoring an
order you drafted against or renegotiating a locked page.

Second, and this is the craft reason: Part II's stated design is "the
same purchase, repeated" (`ch04...md:135`), which is a *repetition
risk*, not an escalation. Something has to rise across four
same-shaped chapters or Part II reads flat. The thing that rises is
**exposure**: ch. 5 is you and a task, ch. 6 is you and a room, ch. 7
is you and the organization's direction, ch. 8 is you and the person
with power over you. That ladder is exactly spine order, and naming it
gives every Part II brief a stake that grows.

Third, ch. 7 is the book's structural midpoint and currently has no
turning point in it (section 3, TP3). Writing 5 → 6 → 7 arrives at the
midpoint with the escalation already built, which is the only way to
install a point of no return that feels earned rather than announced.

Before any of the three: the branch. See section 6, question 1.

### Next 2 — Ch. 6, Hijack the Meeting

**Its argument, in one sentence:** *Meetings drift because steering one
in public costs status that nobody will spend, so the person who moves
everyone toward one shared version of the problem ends up running the
room without ever being given it.*

**Why second.** It is the most universally experienced situation in the

**Its two or three moves:**

1. **The drift autopsy.** One meeting, rendered: five people solving
   five different problems while agreeing with each other. Props are
   the whole job here — the recap doc, "let's take that offline," the
   person who restates the previous person as if it were new. This is
   where the superior position gets spent: the reader sees the
   misalignment before anyone in the room does.
2. **The Olympic Rings, sole debut.** Everyone's ring is their own
   version of the solution; you do not win by arguing your ring is
   bigger, you move rings until they overlap. Obey the physics
   (`analysis-voice:208-211`, rule 11): a ring you shove does not move;
   a ring you overlap does. The framework has to behave like the object
   it is named after or the metaphor is decoration.

   they aligned themselves, which is ch. 1's "disguised as being a team

### Next 3 — Ch. 7, Ask the Question Nobody Wants to Answer (Bump It Up)

**Its argument, in one sentence:** *Asking out loud how today's task

**Why third, and why it carries extra freight.** It is the payoff of

closer's "stop needing a boss" is not the first identity move in the
book.

**Its two or three moves:**

1. **Escalate the vacuum question and say what changed.** Ch. 1 asked
   *what problem are we solving?* Ch. 7 asks *what is this task for?*
   State the difference plainly, in one sentence — per THE SAY-IT TEST
   (`studio/STYLE.md:316-350`), the load-bearing beat of this chapter

   once and got a bad answer and had to live in the room afterward.

**Lexicon to spend:** **BIU / Bump It Up** (`LEXICON.md:13`), sole
definition site (`LEXICON.md:63-69`). **Do not define** CFC, BLUF, or

### What is working structurally and must survive revision

Five things. If a revision breaks any of them, the revision is wrong.

1. **The two-thesis structure** — hook (faking it) for acquisition,
   engine (the tax) for retention, with the gas-vs-wave read explicitly
   *not* decided by the tax (`CONCEPT.md:18-33`, LOCKED). This is the
   single best structural decision in the project. It is what lets the
   book survive its own hook running out after the front third.
2. **The rotating lens across ch. 1–4** — boss, scoreboard, you, the
   crowd, each chapter removing exactly one excuse and handing an open
   question forward (`snowflake:262-279`). It is why Part I reads as an
   argument accumulating rather than four essays.
3. **The two deliberate walk-aways** — ch. 11 complicating the hook and
   the closer dissolving the game (`THREADS.md:239-242`). They look like
   drift and are not; they are what makes the book giftable upward and
   what turns a one-joke premise into an argument.
4. **The money-metaphor chain** — counterfeit → ledger → invoice → tax
   → receipt → money on the floor, four chapters without a mixed image.
   Do not add a fifth currency image to Part II; extend this one.
5. **The guardrail as practiced, not just stated** — the group-chat
   retarget, the "scared, not wrong" Bell Curve, "None of this is a
   character flaw," the pulled waitress line, and the closer's
   protection of the reader who opts out. The book keeps choosing
   recognition over contempt at the exact moments contempt would be
   easier and funnier. That choice is the product.
