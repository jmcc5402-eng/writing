## Why target the Boss?

The boss is the most influential person in your job. In one aspect or another, they control what you do for eight hours every day. This is a lot of responsibility and should not be taken lightly.

Many bosses don't value their position. They don't comprehend the importance of their position. Those bosses should not be bosses. Or at a minimum, it should be crystal clear they need to improve.

There is a famous phrase:

> You rise to your highest level of incompetence.

This means that some jobs are not appropriate for some people. Too often, however, this message does not get communicated to the incompetent person. And if this person is your boss, your life is not fun.

I want you to learn how to identify the traits of incompetent bosses that don't value the responsibility they hold.

I want you to take action to improve the situation.

In many cases, the action will improve your position, improve your boss's position, or improve your co-workers' positions. We are not "out to get anyone." But the injustice of incompetence in positions of authority must be addressed.

This is where you start.

## Chapter 1 — Live in Reality — Please!

*Alignment: Reality*

Has your boss ever been annoyed at you for not completing work he didn't assign? Yes, that sentence is confusing, but it is accurate. How often does your boss casually mention a task and then get mad when it's not completed? If the answer is often, this boss suffers from "Non-Reality" (NR).

This boss thinks that every thought in his head is written down by a magical scribe and sent out to the office in a bulletized action-item list. This boss "thinks big" and doesn't care that everyone is telling him the project is unrealistic.

To be successful, a boss needs to actively fight the siren-sounds of non-reality. But most don't. To be honest, most don't have the skills to get the job done and don't have a clue that they live in a land of unicorns and butterflies.

But there is hope. If you can identify how and when your boss is operating in la-la land, you can effect positive change. This positive change will help your career, help your colleagues' careers, and possibly help your boss's career. And if they don't want the help, you may have to MYBYB.

### Bosses love "things"

It's easy for people to grasp "things." Things are tangible. They have a beginning and an end. Sometimes you can hold them. They are black and white. They either work or they don't.

Unfortunately, most of the problems that we have in the world can't be fixed by "things." Things are tangible and most of our big problems are intangible.

Examples:

- HR Software is a tangible item. It will not be the answer to your office's low-morale problem.
- Presentations and Gantt charts are pretty things. They are not the project. And more importantly, if the tasks on the chart aren't the right tasks, even the pretty chart won't save the project.

"Things" are often tied to solutions. But way too often, the problem isn't clearly identified. Things appear to make problems simple, but if not used correctly, they make life harder.

Often, things are built by other people. Bosses take advantage of this to outsource responsibility. If someone else builds, implements, or writes the "thing," then there are numerous ways to blame them when something goes wrong.

If the use of the "thing" is addressed in a realistic fashion, the complications will be addressed head on. However, if the "thing" is viewed as the answer to all problems, the complications will take a back seat, not be addressed appropriately, and ruin the project.

Look around your office for the "things" you deal with every day. Are they adding to the group's productivity? Are they addressing the actual problem? Are they blamed for everything?

### Bosses love to "hope"

"Things" enable bosses to "hope" a job will get completed correctly.

Hope is what many bosses do in order to complete tasks. Instead of working hard to identify the problem and a corresponding solution, bosses "hope" that the "things" will work themselves out.

Some bosses have turned hope into an action verb. They can proactively hope you are doing things by checking on status, but not actually providing additional guidance. Or the opposite: they hope things work out by providing clear expectations, but don't provide the correct supplies or manpower.

Has your boss ever said, "I had hoped this would be done by now"?

> **Bosses need to be helpful, not hopeful.**

## Chapter 2 — Two Problems

*Alignment: Problem Solving*

> **[TODO: formatting]** Marked `****FORMATTING` in the source. Title needs finalizing ("Two Problems" vs "What's the problem?").

Problem solving is a core issue to MYBYB. It is something that permeates almost every aspect of your life. However, across the country bosses are failing their employees for the simple reason that they don't know how to solve problems. If you can master this skill, you will MYBYB.

From a high-level, simplistic point of view, the goal of most jobs is to solve a problem.

Examples:

- How to teach low-income kids how to read.
- How to put out a fire.
- How to increase revenues on the west side.
- How to better serve the constituents.
- How to provide breakfast in 90 seconds or less.

If you're a supervisor, these are some of your problems:

- How to motivate your employees.
- How to deal with HR issues like harassment and time-card fraud.
- How to expand your group's role in the company.

To make your job harder, many of the problems you face are overlapping, have dependencies, or are contradictory. So where do you start?

Problems can be broken down into two broad categories: **Intangible** and **Tangible**.

- Intangible problems are hard.
- Tangible problems are easy.
- Intangible problems can be broken down into tangible problems… even when it doesn't seem possible.
- Tangible problems are part of a larger, intangible problem.

> **[TODO: art]** `<<INSERT PICTURE OF OVAL>>` — hierarchy-of-problems diagram.

The big bucks are made by solving intangible problems. Master this and the Higher Ups will view you as a high-level, strategic thinker, and most likely they will MYBYB.

I'm going to guess that your boss, like most people, hates intangible problems. He probably has an arsenal of excuses why the intangible problems never get solved, such as "that's the problem with this place," or "that's life working at XXX company."

Your boss is probably ineffective at solving intangible problems, but why? The primary reason people can't solve intangible problems is because they treat them like tangible problems.

If morale is low in your organization, you have an intangible problem. Don't solve it with tangible solutions like scheduling a happy hour. Scheduling a happy hour and buying the first round is a good start — the drinkers will like it — but will it fix the overall problem? Likely not.

Happy Hour is a tangible solution. It may fix the tangible problem of something like "the group doesn't know each other" or "people have been working too hard lately." But it won't fix an overall morale problem.

**Problem-Solving Checklist:**

- Identify problem as intangible or tangible.
- Break intangible problems into tangible problems.
- Devise tangible solutions to tangible problems.

A good boss thrives on intangible problems. A bad boss avoids them.

Why do bosses do this? Bosses tell higher-ups that they are attacking the problems.

Who does this hurt? You and your colleagues. If your boss is applying tangible solutions to intangible problems, the solutions seem ungenuine and weak.

The bosses didn't take the time to understand the root of the problem. They saw a shiny solution and applied it. Many bosses spend their careers (including lots of promotions) applying tangible solutions to intangible problems. It usually takes months or years to realize it didn't work, but by that time everyone has moved on, or the boss will have built up an arsenal of excuses as to why it didn't work. He/she looks good; the company and people like you suffer.

Are you currently working on tangible problems or intangible problems?

> **Rule of thumb:** Difficult intangible problems usually consist of 80% tangible parts. Once the 80% has been broken down, the last 20% seems much easier to tackle. Often, once you break down the first 80%, you find the answer to the last 20%.

## Chapter 5 — Training — All books don't suck

*Alignment: Training*

It's easy to get lost in the ocean of books you see at the library or bookstore (do those even exist anymore?).

When choosing a book to spend your valuable time on, two important criteria should be considered:

- Read the right books.
- Read books the right way.

### Read the right books

There are a million leadership/management books. Many are good, many are not. Worse, many are politicized. When I choose books, I think about a few criteria.

**Types of books.** Generally, when I read a book, I want to get right to the point. Some books are straightforward and get right to the point — *Getting to Yes* comes to mind; you know the exact point of the book the moment you pick it up.

Other books are more storytelling and provide more background. Many of the books about military officers' experiences in the field are examples. These are often excellent, but you have to enjoy the act of reading the exciting adventure and not expect to get clear "how-tos."

Some books are very textbook-like. Others use lots of scenarios. I like scenarios as long as they are believable. When writing this book, I ensured the scenarios were accurate. Some real-life scenarios seemed so fake that I didn't use them (mostly examples of people being VERY stupid).

**Quality of books.** Many non-fiction books lose steam after about three chapters. It's not easy to have more than three "fresh" ideas about a topic. In *Getting to Yes*, the entire strategy of the book is included in the first 3 pages. I remember listening to that book on CD and after 15 minutes I stopped it. It was amazing — I totally understood the new strategy. The rest of the book seemed to be repeating itself. Since I wasn't too interested in mastering all the details of negotiation, I put the book down, but was very satisfied.

Other books just get bad after about 3 chapters. In these cases, the authors had great ideas but tried to add fluff to make the book longer. This is a result of the traditional publishing market where the size of the book matters (insert inappropriate joke).

The "good" chapters are always at the beginning. The beginning chapters can be composed of fluff like "history of bicycles" or "the author's lengthy background." Be wary of fluff chapters.

One thing that is great about Kindle books is that there is less pressure to add fluff. If you believe you are not getting your money's worth out of a book… leave a bad review. Just remember: that book from Barnes & Noble with 17 useless chapters cost $24. The Kindle book you're reading with 4 solid chapters likely cost less than $5.

### Read books the right way

**Don't read cover to cover.** Except for a few outliers, you don't have to read a book cover to cover. Especially books with 17 bad chapters.

- Read the title and decide if this interests you.
- Scan the contents. Do the second-half chapters seem like fluff? Are the opening chapters fluff? Or does the whole book suck (like this one)?
- Scan the content: is this book "step by step" or "general knowledge"? Both can be interesting, but with general-knowledge books you can often just skim the beginning of each chapter for the gist.

> **Note:** This version of MYBYB is "General Knowledge." We are delving into many of the specific details of problem solving, training, and self-leadership. This specific version is a general overview.

**Skim the beginning of each chapter.** I believe most non-fiction books should follow **BLUF: Bottom Line Up Front.** (This is also a technique for almost everything at work, but for the moment let's apply it to non-fiction books.)

With BLUF, the purpose of the section is stated up front. The purpose is then supported in the following parts of the section. This isn't a new concept, but many books do not follow these guidelines… and those books are confusing!

Like many important concepts, BLUF is iterative. The bottom line of the entire book should be made clear and then supported by the chapters. Each chapter should have a clear bottom line that is supported by each paragraph. And further, each paragraph should have a clear topic sentence that is supported by the following sentences.

This methodology won't win any literary awards, but the reader will understand the author's message.

If the author can't get his message across in one or two paragraphs at the beginning of a chapter or section, the message is likely too jumbled or disorganized. The book might not be worth reading.

**Important.** If the book isn't written with BLUF, you need to go into analysis mode. There are a few possibilities:

- The book is written in a "story fashion" where the author builds the argument slowly. This is like *Who Moved My Cheese?* or possibly an autobiography/leadership combo book. These books can be great — just remember, you'll likely have to read the whole thing to capture the point.
- The book is written poorly. If the point of each chapter isn't CFC, or if the subsections don't seem to support the point of the chapter, there is a problem. This book isn't well organized… and probably sucks. Put it down.

> **FLASHPOINT — More on BLUF:** BLUF should be done in an iterative fashion, like a pyramid. The point of the chapter should be crystal clear at the beginning. The point of every supporting argument should be crystal clear at the beginning of each subsection. Even further, the point of every paragraph should be crystal clear in the first sentence.
>
> Good writers do this. Have you ever read *The Economist*? I know, I know — not interesting. But if you ever pick it up, notice how the first sentence of every paragraph tells you exactly the point of that paragraph.
>
> In a previous job, I traveled to Latin America on a regular basis. Before a trip, I always wanted to get a general understanding of the country I was visiting. I would read the first sentence of every paragraph in *The Economist*'s "Americas" section. By the time I had finished my vodka and tonic, I would have an excellent understanding of current affairs in the region. Overall, I probably only read 15 sentences, but each sentence was so crystal clear I understood exactly the message being communicated.
>
> When books/articles are written this way, they are very easy to read quickly and capture the point. If I wanted more detail on a specific paragraph, I could read the supporting sentences. I would just read the topic sentences for paragraphs that were less interesting. More importantly, this gave me extra time to: sleep; read something cool like *Shogun*; study Fantasy Football.

## Chapter 6 — Meetings — Use the Olympic Rings

*Alignment: Problem Solving*

Most people hate meetings. This makes sense because most meetings suck.

For all the downside, meetings do have some benefits:

- Many important decisions are made during meetings — i.e., it can be a high-impact usage of your time.
- Meetings are a time to demonstrate your skill set to other colleagues, bosses, and higher ups.

### Only attend useful meetings

The first rule of meetings is to think like a poker player. Poker players know that the most important decision you make is what table to sit at. If the table is full of pros, avoid it at all costs. But if the table is full of rich tourists, take a seat and wait for the money to roll in.

You must choose which meetings you attend. I have a policy that I only attend meetings where I'm going to speak or contribute. Any other meeting, I can get the information from someone else.

### Problem-solving meetings are high impact

In some meetings, problems are solved. These are my favorite types of meetings. They are also the most difficult to run and attend.

If you can help the team solve the problem, even if you aren't an expert, you'll look like a superstar. People will start requesting you contribute at future meetings. Your boss will be impressed. The higher ups will wonder why your boss isn't leading the meetings. You will MYBYB.

Stated or not, there is always a goal to any problem-solving meeting. There is some problem to solve.

The downside is that many obstacles stand in your way:

- The problem isn't well defined.
- Everyone has a different opinion on the problem.
- Everyone has a different opinion on a solution.
- No one understands the fact that their solution is different than other people's solutions.
- Everyone prioritizes the criteria of the decision differently (consciously or unconsciously).
- There is not a clearly defined strategy to help make the decision. *(NOTE: more advanced — see the future chapter: Mission/Vision/Strategy = Crap/Crap/Crap.)*

OK, enough bellyaching — how do we solve the issue of a problem-solving meeting?

The key is to think about the Olympics, specifically the **Olympic Rings.** Imagine each person's solution in the meeting is one of those rings. The rings are somewhat concentric, but there is not a lot of overlap. The goal is to move each ring so they are aligned on top of each other.

The key to do this is to ask good questions. Here is one scenario:

> **You** (after describing the problem and getting people to agree that that is the problem): So now that we agree on the problem, Bill (from accounting), what solution do you suggest?
>
> **Bill:** Blah Blah Blah.
>
> *(Remember, it doesn't really matter what he says.)*
>
> **You** (after Bill is done): Jim (from marketing), does that solution work for you? What approach would you take?

The goal here is to get everyone in the room to realize that the other people didn't come to the meeting with the exact same solution in their head that they did. Your goal at this stage is *not* to solve the problem. As you'll soon discover, jumping to a solution is rarely the best route to take.

Even after everyone thinks they understand the problem in the same way, it often takes until people's alternative solutions are aired that they understand that their takes on the problem are different.

Many people seem to think that a magical scribe is jotting down each of their thoughts and emailing it to everyone they know. It may come as a surprise that other folks in the room don't see the problem (and the world) the exact way they do.

> **Extra Credit:** If you are a real rock star, you will document the variations in each person's plan. As you go around the room asking for people's solutions, you can ensure they understand some of the main points and requirements the other attendees offered up.

When the meeting (or multiple meetings — sometimes this is slow work) is over, each attendee will understand the same problem and the same solution. They will also understand that other people come from different perspectives and have different priorities. These affect the solution to the problem.

If people come to clear agreement on the problem, the solution will fall into place.

## Chapter 7 — Training — Smart, not Smarts

*Alignment: Training*

Next time you're at work, look around. Who is actively trying to improve? Probably not many people.

Continual improvement is vital to job success.

This is part of the "reality check" section of this book. You MUST be constantly assessing your abilities and seeking to improve them. It is a fact that successful (and happy) people are willing to make change for the better. The first step is to honestly assess your abilities and determine where you need to improve.

### You can improve!

You might be thinking, "there's no way I can compete with these other folks who have masters degrees and lots of experience."

That is untrue. You can compete, especially if you focus on exactly the right things to improve upon. You need to set realistic goals and focus on the right skills to improve.

Part of life is realistic goals. Improving yourself for your own job satisfaction is very realistic.

As I discuss below, masters degree programs are good for about three, very general, concepts. This isn't the time to learn generalized concepts — this is a time to learn the specific things you need to succeed.

It's not about smarts, it's about being smart.

You need to analyze your situation and figure out where you need to improve. What you focus on is vital. Spend a lot of time thinking about that. It will pay off.

### It doesn't take much

In the problem-solving section I discuss one of my favorite phrases: **"Improvement over perfection."** This is key for self-improvement too.

You aren't going to become the subject-matter expert in Eastern European market habits overnight, but you can learn how to correctly pronounce each of the country's names.

Often a few small improvements will start a snowball effect. Many books discuss the 80/20 rule — the idea that focusing on the most important 20% of a problem will improve the entire problem. Self-improvement is the same way. Getting started is key.

### What to improve on

**You must focus on the right things.** I've spoken with scores of employees who went back to school or paid for a masters degree and their work doesn't value it. The reality is (and remember, we must consider reality) jobs value education because it can help improve productivity, profits, impact, etc.

Unfortunately, you don't get a raise just because you studied really hard… sorry. If your education isn't strategically aligned to productivity, profits, or impact, your work has a right not to care. You must learn the right things.

You need to improve the detailed tasks that will make you valuable *today.* Unless you are planning to move to a new job, don't spend your time learning about cost accounting if you work in sales.

**Don't think school, this is the real world.** Higher education is important — don't get me wrong. However, school is more likely to get you a new job rather than improve your current situation. By its nature, formal education generalizes reality in order to teach concepts and processes. But in your company, you already understand the concepts and processes. You need to learn how to improve those specific concepts and processes, or come up with new ones.

As I noted earlier, there are three skills people learn in a masters degree program:

- How to work in teams.
- How to ask questions.
- How to think for themselves.

To improve your work situation you can't rely on high-level generalized concepts. You need to focus on the specific skills that will improve your performance.

## Chapter 8 — Live in Reality — Learn the Compass

*Alignment: Reality*

**Communication equals reality.** Valuing the importance of robust communication is a way to wipe non-reality off the map.

Bosses and higher ups hear news from other bosses and higher ups, not from real people. For them to make reality-based decisions, they must have "boots on the ground" information.

On the other hand, bosses must push important information down to the employees. Employees are the most annoyed when they don't "know what's going on." Assuming that "everyone is talking" and "knows what's going on" is the quickest way to drive the ship into an iceberg.

> **[TODO: reference]** Source note: "(****Ref to First Break All the Rules.)"

Communication goes up and down and left and right. Understanding these flows of information will help you identify when and where non-reality is entering your workplace.

**Up and Down.** Are bosses informed? Are bosses informing employees? If not, you have an up-and-down problem. Are bosses making decisions that don't make any sense to employees? If yes, you have an up-and-down problem — either the bosses made a bad decision and weren't given the correct negative feedback, or they weren't given the correct information in the first place to make a good decision.

**Left and Right.** Are groups making moves without coordinating? Are some groups implementing new strategies that others haven't heard of? Are all workers on the same page about basic goals and strategy, or is everyone living on their own planet?

Communication is usually a cultural thing; it has little to do with the technology used: email, chat, Skype, or phone calls. Cultural issues are intangible problems. They seem daunting, but as we know, any intangible problem can be broken down into tangible parts. The tangible parts can be improved with simple steps.

> **Note:** The first step is identifying that there is a problem.

**What can screw up communication? Lots of things:**

- **Meetings.** Information will not flow if meetings are too frequent, have too many people, include too many higher ups, or don't include anyone of authority.
- **Micromanagers.** Bosses that insist on hearing every detail of everything. We talk a lot about micromanagers, but don't discuss *why* they are bad. One reason: they slow down communication. If each employee must constantly double-check every decision with their boss, work slows down.
- **Microfollowers** *(see self-leadership chapter for definition).* If people feel they need to tell every detail of everything, things fall apart. There isn't enough time in the day to hear or tell all the details. It's not realistic!
- **Lack of common terms, principles, or strategy.** This starts to fall into the later chapter *Mission/Vision/Values = Crap/Crap/Crap*, but it is important. If all parts of the organization have different definitions or perspectives, communication really breaks down.

**Improve communication.** When you have an intangible problem, you need to break it down and apply specific tangible solutions. The solution must fix the problem. You must truly understand the problem first.

*Up and Down (intangible):*

- Hold stand-up meetings every morning (tangible). Everyone gets 30 seconds to say what they are doing that day. Limited discussion. (These are great to expose cry-babies that want to use all their face-time to complain about unrealistic projects they started that aren't working — but it's not their fault.)
- Have standardized status reports (tangible). Most people hate these, but they don't have to be bad — maybe 5 bullets with the highlights and two obstacles of the week. If the bosses have questions they can chat with you about them. Oh, and the bosses have to read the reports too.

*Left and Right (intangible):*

- Start a message board for everyone to discuss tasks/processes (tangible).
- Make a group email list, and tell people that if they don't want to see so much spam, use a filter to put those emails into another folder.
- Have a "worker only" meeting where managers are not invited. At this meeting you can ensure everyone is on the same page, and also agree on major issues that you want to push up the chain (i.e., up and down).

If marketing is only focused on marketing and doesn't give a hoot about IT, it will be difficult for any effective communication to happen. Producing some simple definitions and/or principles provides something to "anchor" a conversation off of. They also give you something to defend yourself with when people are being "non-realistic."

None of these are groundbreaking ideas. The key concept is that you have to *start.* You have to realize that poor communication is an intangible problem that can be addressed by a number of tangible actions.

## Chapter 9 — Problem Solving — Bump it up

*Alignment: Problem Solving*

I bet your boss focuses on random details at random times. He probably complains about specific issues to his boss, but doesn't fix them — or doesn't really understand them. During a discussion, does he jump to detailed or technical questions, possibly to look cool? Or does your boss have no idea what is going on? That's bad. Even worse is when the boss doesn't know what's going on, pretends that he does, and *then* still makes important decisions.

You need to **Bump It Up.**

"Bump it up" (BIU for short) is the act of aligning the current task/question/problem with a larger strategy or project. You can do this by asking questions such as:

- "Once we complete this, what is the impact?"
- "How does this fit into the bigger picture?"
- "Are we sure we are on track? What is the big question we are trying to answer?"

In the Problem Solving section, we discuss how intangible problems break down into more tangible problems, and those into more tangible problems. This concept works well, as long as everyone understands how each of the smaller problems fits into the larger problems.

If you start to think that the smaller problem isn't fitting in, you need to BIU, to get everyone on the same page about the larger project.

Many times decisions are made or problems solved at lower levels, but the higher levels aren't solidified. On rare occasions that can be OK, but in general, any low-level decision that doesn't jive with a higher-level strategy is a waste. Best case, the people working on that task will have wasted their time. Worst case, work is done that can't be fixed, or sets the bigger strategy totally off its axis.

"Bump it up" is not 100% tied to decision making. Like many of the chapters of this book, it is a technique that can be used in many situations. But decision making is one of those. BIU will not only help ensure your decisions are good, it will make you look great. And in many cases, it will make your boss look like… well, you know.

Why bump it up? The reason is simple. Way too often NO ONE in the room really understands what the hell is going on (similar to the earlier chapter on living in reality). Further, BIU comes at a low cost to you, but the benefits can be huge. Worst case, you say something like, "Let's take a step back and ensure these decisions fit into our bigger plan." If the decisions are aligned, you don't look that bad. You may have spent a few minutes, but most good bosses would appreciate that.

> **Action Technique — BIU on the sly:** BIU when there is a new colleague in the room. That way you can disguise your bump-up by "explaining" what is going on to the newbie. This has a few benefits: they see you as a leader, they drink your Kool-Aid (if you have an agenda), you seem nice (filling their bucket), and if there is an alignment problem, you get the credit for uncovering it.

One way to MYBYB is to innocently ask a question they should know the answer to. This works best with their boss in the room, but in any case it's a step in the right direction. The question: "How does this current process/task fit into the bigger scheme of the group, company, organization?"

I would argue that a boss's first priority is to ensure that the work being done aligns with the higher-level goal, and then that the higher-level goal aligns with a higher-level strategy… and on and on.

Help your boss do his job correctly with BIU. If they don't know the higher-level problem or strategy, they look like a fool. But it also gives the team something substantial to work on: defining the higher-level problem and ensuring the current task aligns.

Most bosses are so focused on "getting the work done" and diving right in (see book 3 about the MBTI) that they don't double-check that the work they are doing is the "right" work to do.

Most bosses think busy people is an indication of success and impact. This is probably the case if you are digging holes (and that's not always true either), but if you're doing anything else, people busy on the wrong thing is likely more hurtful than helpful. You need to bump it up to ensure everyone is on track.

## Chapter 10 — Being Different — The Other Option is Sucking

*Alignment: Change*

Your performance in life is a combination of all the decisions you make. Each decision, big and small, is important. Some more than others.

So how do you know what decisions to make? You need an anchor. Something that helps you choose along the way. One anchor I suggest is **being different.**

### The Difference Bell Curve

If you constantly do what everyone else is doing, then 75% of the time you are doing things that crappy and average people do. Nobody wants that.

Where am I getting this 75%? Imagine the people in your office on a bell curve. The lower 25% are crappy performers, the middle 50% are medium performers, and the upper 25% are the high performers. You can apply this scale to almost any group of people.

When choosing every action you take, you must consider: is this a 75%'er action or an upper-25%'er action?

And remember, this is *every* action. Little actions accumulate.

This idea can be broken down further. Remember school? You only received an "A" if you were in the top 10%. Life isn't much different, and rarely grades on a curve.

> 10% — 15% — 50% — 15% — 10%

With this in mind, 90% of the actions you see people taking are likely the WRONG action to take.

**Examples:**

- During a meeting, all the attendees go around the room complaining about how there isn't enough money in the budget for the new "changes." What do most people do? They keep the circle of complaining alive by adding new flames to the fire. As an outside observer, do any of these complainers seem like "A" students? The 10%'er will be the person that moves the conversation toward something constructive.
- Many people have commutes of 30 minutes or longer. What is the normal thing to do? Listen to music, commercials, podcasts. What would the 10%'er do? It depends. If they feel they're lacking in skills, they might get a constructive audiobook or podcast. On the other hand, if they've identified those 30 minutes as time to relax and catch up on music, they'll do that — but they understand it's time to relax, not to drive 90 mph and swerve in and out of traffic pushing to get home first.
- The salesmen on the team are working harder, but they aren't increasing revenues. What do the bottom 75% do? Keep pushing, banging their heads harder against the brick wall. What does the 25% do? Something different! The current sales process is not working. They've identified that CHANGE is needed.

This may sound obvious, but it's not:

> **To change, you must do things differently.**

The 75%'ers keep doing the same thing. They think they are changing because they are working harder, but they aren't. The upper 10–25%'ers consider NEW ways to increase sales — new customers, new marketing, new pricing. They realize the old ways don't work anymore, and if they want to succeed they need to do things differently.

### Being different

Your boss is probably very "the same." Do you want to be like your boss? I didn't think so.

Does your boss stay late at night getting "caught up" on email? If so, does it help? When asked what a typical day consisted of, most people responded "reading email." How often do you actually feel like you've accomplished something productive after "catching up" on emails? Not often. Then why do people do it? Because "everyone else does."

Usually what people really want is to know what's "going on." Reading emails is one way to understand this, but it is slow, possibly inaccurate, and not productive. How can you learn what's going on in a different way?

- **Walk around the office and ask people.** "Hey Bill, what did I miss this week? Anything exciting?" — a good way to start a conversation. You'll probably get all the details, Bill will feel better for spilling the beans, and there's a chance you can discuss solutions to any problems that arose.
- **Go talk to your boss.** "Hey Boss, I just got back, I want to get right back to work — what's going on that I should focus on?" You'll ensure you start on the most pressing issues first (assuming your boss actually has a clue… big assumption). You let your boss know you value their opinion. And you get to gauge whether your boss has a clue by comparing what they say to what your colleagues say.

### Final thoughts

For every decision you need to make, consider the bell curve. If other people are making a decision, consider that 75% of the time it is wrong. This means that 75% of the time you should do the opposite of others in the office.

Have confidence in this calculation. You will need it to MYBYB.
