## One sentence

Nick is stranded past the edge of every map with a broken sleigh, three
caribou, and a date he promised someone he would keep — and the only
workshop that can mend the sleigh belongs to a dead man, whose killer
still comes calling every few days to take away everything a man makes.

## Back cover

Everyone in Bronston, Alaska believes Nick died on the mountain, and he
needs them to go on believing it.
One person knows better.
Grace stood with him in the dark at the top of a cliff and heard the
whole impossible story, and she is the only soul who will ever be told,
and she is leaving.
There is a steamer out of Juno before the ice closes the channel and a
medical school in San Francisco at the far end of it, and the two of
them said the sailing date out loud to each other once, quietly, the
way people say a thing they mean to keep.
Then the glowing stone he dug out of a cave wall carried him north,
past the last of everything, and set him down on ice with a runner
cracked through, three caribou standing on a flat with a few weeks of
thin feed under the snow, and the stone gone cold as any other rock in
his hand.
Above the shelf he finds a seam of light that turns out to be a door,
and behind the door a place where a man worked.
Tools.
Iron.
Timber worth more than gold up here.
And a long wall cut end to end with tally marks.
Nick reads marks in stone for a living.
He counts them, gets a number that means years, feels sorry for
whoever worked out here that long with nobody to tell, and gets to
work, because the sun drops a measured amount against the ridge every
morning and he has done the arithmetic and it will not close.
He can build a runner that will last, or he can build one that will be
finished in time.
He picks the fast one.
Then something comes in the night and takes the wood he shaped and
leaves the meat lying beside it.
Something lifts a latched door off its pin without breaking it.
Something walks around his deadfall and picks its heading up on the far
side.
He calls it a bear for as long as he can stand to.
And when he finally reads that wall the way a surveyor should have read
it the first time — not years of labor but a count of visits, the gaps
tightening, the last group stopping in the middle of itself — he
understands what the dead man learned too late.
The thing is not hunting anybody.
It takes what hands have lately changed, and it comes back sooner the
harder a man works, and up here a man who cannot work does not last the
winter.

## Q1 — The Setup

The stone puts him down on the ice in the last of an afternoon, and the
first thing Nick does is what his father drilled into him for weather
he did not understand, which is count.
Three caribou, still in harness.
One runner cracked through at the curve.
One stone, warm in his palm an hour ago in a cave a thousand miles
south, now dead cold.
No smoke, no track, no sound at all.
He finds the door in the ridge because he goes looking for the seam of
light he glimpsed on the way down, and behind it is shelter, and iron,
and worked timber, and a bench somebody kept the way a careful man
keeps one.
There is more here than a man could hope for and less than he needs.
And there is the long wall, cut end to end with tally marks.
He counts them, gets years, and stands a minute feeling sorry for a
stranger.

Then he goes to work, because he has a date.
Grace said it aloud to him in the dark, the last thing either of them
said, and boats do not wait.
So he takes the only measurement this country offers.
Every morning the sun clears the same notch in the ridge a little
lower, and the difference is small and measurable and he measures it.
He cuts two fresh rows beside the dead man's — days of sun, days to the
sailing — and does the arithmetic in front of the wall, and the
arithmetic tells him no.
A runner that will hold has to be laminated, steamed, and left in the
forms to set, and the setting is days he does not own.
A runner sawn from one stick and scarfed can be under the sleigh in a
week, and it will be short-grained, and he knows exactly what
short-grained means because he spent ten years in the California timber
watching it happen to other men's work.
He writes both numbers on the wall.
He chooses the fast one, in daylight, in his right mind, and that is
the moment the book turns.

That night something comes into the shop.
In the morning the meat he left out is lying where he left it, frozen
and untouched, and every piece he shaped is gone.

A thousand miles south, Grace is at her father's table when the passage
is booked out loud, with a date on it, by a man who has been holding
his breath for twenty-five years.
The stove is sold.
She writes the date down.
She does not mention the other thing she has been counting, or who she
has been counting it for, because there is no version of that sentence
a sane person says in a kitchen.

## Q2 — Build, and the Point of No Return

A man alone can do the work of a shop if the shop is good enough, and
this one is, and for a while Nick is nearly happy.
Steam and clamps, the smell of hot wood, a runner coming true under his
hands.
He is also being taken apart, and not attacked.
Audited.
Shaped wood goes and food stays.
He rehangs the latch and three nights later the latch itself is gone,
lifted off its pin without a mark on it.
He builds a deadfall the way he was taught and finds, in the morning, a
line of tracks that comes to it, stops, walks its whole perimeter, and
picks the same heading up on the far side.
So he stops trying to catch it and tries to shut it out.
He bars the door, drives pins, hangs a second door of sawn plank, and
sleeps four hours, and wakes to find it has come in over the ridge and
down the forge vent — the same seam of light that showed him the way in
on his first afternoon.
The caribou tell him it is coming before he ever hears it.
They go rigid and stop chewing, all three at once, and that is the only
alarm he gets.
He calls it a bear out loud, alone, in an empty workshop, and the word
starts to sound like something he is saying on purpose.

Then he loses a whole night's work and goes to the wall in a temper to
cut another mark in his own row, and sees, finally, what he has been
looking at every day for weeks.
The marks are not evenly spaced.
They cluster.
The clusters repeat at an interval any man who has read six sample tins
at six depths would have caught in an hour, and the gaps between the
late ones tighten and tighten, and then the count stops in the middle
of a group.
The dead man was not tallying his own years.
He was tallying something's visits, and learning its rhythm, and he was
still learning it when it ended.
Worse than that, because Nick can see his own two rows from where he is
standing.
The weeks the man cut the most marks are the weeks he must have been
working hardest, because that is what the shop's own leavings say, and
the visits came fastest in exactly those weeks.
Nick climbs the slope above the door and finds the bed.
Packed snow, a sightline straight onto his own doorway, used, and used
long before he arrived.
The overland walk-out is still barely possible while there is light.
It means leaving the caribou to starve and the sleigh where it lies.
He goes back inside and stays, and that night he does something he has
never done in his life.
He writes.

Far down the coast, on a beach nobody has a name for, a man wakes who
is supposed to be dead.
He does not know where he is either.
The first thing Donald Grin does, when he can talk, is give the people
who pull him out of the surf somebody else's name.

## Q3 — The Major Setback

The fast runner comes off the forms and it looks like a runner.
It even lifts.
The first flight is short and ugly and nearly the last one, because
somewhere over the flats the stick splits along the grain he chose to
cut across and the sleigh yaws and puts him down hard on ice, and he
lies there a while working out that the thing that just tried to kill
him was a decision he made in daylight three weeks ago.
He learns something else that week and does not understand yet that it
is the most valuable thing he will learn all year.
He sets two things out on the same patch of snow, well away from the
shop.
A slab of raw fat, and a cake of tallow he rendered and poured and let
set in a mold with his own hands.
In the morning the cake is gone and the fat is lying there frozen, and
whatever came for the one walked past the other to get it.

Then the stone.
He seats it in the sleigh's socket the way it was seated the night it
brought him here and it lies there like gravel, because the sky he
sighted it against does not exist up here.
The star he locked his course to in Bronston sits nearly overhead in
this country, and an instrument that reads one sky cannot read another
until it is told where it is.
That is survey work, and it is nights of it, and there are no nights.
At this latitude, in this season, the sky never goes properly dark.

He tries the other thing a man tries, which is to sit up.
He watches four nights with a lamp and the caribou for an alarm, and
for four nights nothing comes at all, and on the fifth, when he has to
sleep, it calls.
It is not avoiding him.
It is outlasting him, and the watch has cost him four working days off
a count he cannot afford to spend.

He is out on the flats with a plumb line and a frozen face when it
comes.
It comes out of the blue dusk in the open, on ground where nothing can
hide, and it takes the split runner off the sleigh and carries it away
at a walk, thirty feet from a man who could not reach it in time and
would be no use if he could.
It looks at him once while it does it.
That afternoon the sun clears the notch, goes along the ridge, and sets
for the winter, and does not come back.
Nick stands in a cold shop with a dark stone, no runner, no good timber
left, and two rows of marks on a wall.
The lower row says the steamer sails in the crossing time plus a
handful of days.
He cuts one line into the stone beside them, in the first person, by
sleigh-glow.
*I did the arithmetic right. I had the wrong idea about what was
counting.*

In Bronston, Grace loses something a letter cannot buy back.
She has already been to the dock on a Thursday and asked the boat men,
carefully, the way you ask about weather, whether anybody travels north
of the channel and whether anybody has seen lights out that way, and
got a story, and a laugh, and the fact she was afraid of, which is that
people who go north of there do not come back to be asked about it.
She has already asked the agent to move the passage and been told about
the ice, and the next sailing, and the word he used was spring.
Now the town, which is richer this year than it has been in a decade
and does not know why, votes to set a stone for the men it lost, and
Nick's name is on it, and because she was the last person to see him
alive they ask Grace to choose the words and read them out.
She stands up in front of everyone she has ever known and reads an
epitaph for a living man.
Then Old Rob says the thing half the town has been saying since the
gold came back, which is that maybe the boy's ghost had a hand in it,
and every face turns to her, because she would know.
She says no.
She says he is dead and the gold was somebody's kindness and that is
all it was, and she watches the true story go out of the room like heat
out of a door.
Nobody will ever ask her again.
She goes home and finishes packing a house she grew up in, for a boat
she now has to be on.

## Q4 — Climax and Aftermath

Nick stops trying to beat the animal and starts trying to read it,
which is the only thing that wall was ever good for.
He works the intervals like a set of samples, and what comes out is not
menace and not hunger.
It is a route.
The thing walks a long circuit of an empty country and calls at every
place where hands have changed something, and it takes what has lately
been changed, and it comes back sooner where there is more of it.
Which means the dead man was killing himself with his own diligence,
and so is Nick, and it also means the interval is not a fact.
It is a dial, and Nick's hands are on it.

He knows what the polar dark costs him, because it is written on the
wall in his own hand.
He also knows it is the only thing on earth that can give him a sky.
Four clear nights of stars he could not have had while the sun was up,
and a first fix that is wrong, and he only knows it is wrong because
the stone comes up faintly warm and swings the sleigh onto a heading
that puts a star where no star can be.
He puts it down.
He finds the error, which is his own [TK method — the drafter's
manifest fixes the exact fault], and starts again, and spends nights he
is counting on a thing he cannot hurry.

So the choice at the bottom of it is one he makes with his eyes open,
and the arithmetic is on the wall where he can see it.
He can bolt.
Lash the old cracked runner from the landing, leave the stone dark,
guess a heading off memory, keep every ounce of his own food, and go
now, tonight, and by the wall's own numbers he probably makes the dock.
Or he can do the work.
Doing the work costs the survey nights, and the last good timber in the
place, and the hours the thing will take out of him while he does it,
and the only currency he has left to buy those hours with.
For days he renders and packs and sews and wraps his crossing rations
by lamplight, every ounce of it changed by his hands, which is the one
thing in this country that will draw the animal off a route it has
walked since before he was born.
Then he carries it out into the dark along a line he has only ever seen
tracks on, sets it down bare on open snow at the far end of the
circuit, and backs away.
It comes while he can still see it.
It goes to work on the wrappings the way he went to work on them, and
it does not once look up.
He walks home and spends what he bought.
The last runner comes out of the last good timber in the place, joined
by feel in lamplight, and the stone goes into the socket sighted at
last to the sky it is actually under, and comes up warm.
The caribou go rigid, all three, and he checks his own count and knows
they are right and knows exactly where the thing is standing, which is
the first time all year he has been ahead of it.
Then the harness knots he has tied since he was a boy, and the animals
leaning into it, and the shelf dropping away with no sun anywhere on
the planet and the aurora going over like water.

The steamer casts off on the second night of the crossing.
He is over open ice when it goes, and he knows the hour, and he keeps
flying south, because south is where the letter is and because there is
nothing behind him but a wall.
The crossing is not free.
He flies it in legs, down a coast rather than across open country,
because the animals have to eat and he has nothing to give them, and
the days they spend digging thin ground with their forefeet are days he
cannot spend.
On the third leg the sea fog closes and a stone that reads position
does not read weather, so he puts down blind on shore ice and does the
oldest thing he knows.
Shelter, fire, food in the cold, minus the food.
He gets up a day and a half later and goes on.

Juno has ice in the slips and a hole in the harbor where a steamer
was, and the date on the agent's board has already gone by.
Nick stands on the boards and does the only thing he is any good at,
which is arithmetic.
He works out how late he is.
Then he works out how many hours he spent on the ice at the cache and
in the shop and under the stars, and the second number is larger than
the first, and he stands there holding both of them, and neither one
was luck.
Bronston is a dark house with the stove sold out of it and a new stone
with his name on it in the frozen ground [TK where — the burying ground
is not established in canon].
He goes to the rock at the creek where two children carved their names
and a dead man cut a heart around them, and finds the letter, weighted
under a stone.
It does not say goodbye.
It says how long, and what she means to be qualified to do when she is
done, and where he can find her at the end of it.
Years, in her handwriting.
He puts it inside his coat and turns north.

We watch her leave, because it is the last thing she owes this story.
Grace at the rail of a mail boat with her father beside her, the boards
crowded because a town this size turns out for departures, the channel
bending, and Grace looking north over the stern at a country that has
never once answered her, and staying there looking long after the
sensible thing would have been to go inside.
Far to the south, in a port town where nobody knows him, a thin man
with a new name and ruined hands listens to two sailors telling a story
about an Alaskan mining camp that woke up rich, and about the thing the
local people say came in the night to do it.
He does not laugh.
He asks, very politely, where.

# APPARATUS

## THE ANTAGONIST'S PLAN (variance card A1)

Fixed first; the book is built to intersect it.

**What it wants.** Made things.
Not meat, not men, not territory.

**The rule, stated once and never explained.**
It leaves what the country made and what the country killed.
It takes what hands have lately changed.
The fresher the work, the sooner it comes.

That rule does three jobs at once.
It is why the meat lies untouched beside the missing wood in Q1.
It is why the rendered cake goes and the raw fat stays in Q3.
It is why a shop full of a dead man's old furniture was not stripped
bare decades ago, while the latch Nick rehung last week is gone.

**Its route.** A long circuit of an empty country, walked for longer
than either man has been up here, calling at every place where hands
change things.
The station is one stop on it.
Nick's flats are the open ground the circuit crosses, which is why
everything that happens in the open happens in view.

**Its cycle, and why the gaps shorten.** The interval between calls is
the length of the circuit, shortened by how much there is to collect.
A working shop is a richer stop than a quiet one.
So the gaps tighten when a man works hard and open out when he does
not, and the dead man's wall records exactly that and he never quite
read it.
This is the mechanism, not a mood.
It also means the interval is a dial rather than a fact, and the dial
has a man's hands on it, which is the entire climax.

**Why it has never killed him (F8).** It does not hunt men.
It has no use for him at all.
The dead man did not die of teeth.
He died because a thing that takes hafts, runners, mends, latches and
rendered fat took him down to nothing over a season, and the harder he
worked to replace what was gone the sooner it came back for the
replacement, and up here a man who cannot work does not last the
winter.
The wall's shortening gaps are that spiral, cut in stone, in the
victim's own hand, in front of Nick from chapter two.

**What it never does.** It is never explained, never named by the
narration, never given a motive, and never given a name a reader can
look up (see OQ1).
It is never cruel.
It is never friendly.
At the climax it does not look up.

## THE CLOCK

Two hands, deliberately **offset**, both cut into the same wall by
Nick's own hand and both visible to the reader by chapter two.

1. **The sun.** At this latitude the sun sets for the winter [TK date],
   a measurable number of days **before** the sailing. With the light go
   the working hours and the forage.
2. **The sailing.** The last steamer south out of Juno before the ice,
   on a date Grace said aloud to Nick in Book 1's away-from-town scene
   [TK exact date; see OQ3 — the plant is owed by Book 1, and depends on
   PR #27, still unmerged].

**The offset is the load-bearing number of the book.**
At the last sun the wall must read: the crossing time, plus a handful
of days spare.
That is what makes Q4 a live race rather than an epilogue.
Bolting spends the spare and probably makes the date.
Doing the work spends the spare and more.
He is late by a countable margin and he can compute it standing on the
dock, which is what the last Juno beat is for.

The creature eats the margin, theft by theft, and the reader watches it
happen in cut stone.

Third counted resource, not a third hand.
Days of feed on the flats, thin and wind-bared and running out, which
is why the crossing has to be flown in legs.
It sits under the two hands as a cost, never as a deadline, so the dial
stays legible.

Fix all of these as one arithmetic table at rung 2 (OQ11), never scene
by scene.

## DENSITY DIAL

**MEDIUM.** Declared.
The survival-and-craft problem explains itself scene by scene, and the
one body of knowledge the book genuinely owns — reading a series of
marks as an interval — is the same competence the reader watches Nick
get wrong and then right, which is drama rather than instruction.
The freed pages go to the creature's evidence ladder, Nick's three
failed campaigns, and the Grace braid.

carrying the deep explanatory movement and no two DEEPs adjacent.

**Red herrings, declared.**
(1) Nick's first reading of the wall as years of unthanked work, which
is sentimental, wrong, and the exact mistake he made about his father.
(2) The bear. The word is his, not the book's, and he keeps using it
after it stops fitting.
(3) **Hunger.** Nick's own working theory for why it comes, held for a
quarter and refuted on the page by the fat lying frozen in the snow.
The book never asserts hunger; a character does, and is wrong.
(4) The empty station read as abandonment, with the latch broken from
the outside on the page long before Nick lets it mean anything.

**Nothing resolved by luck.**
The window at the climax is bought with work he cannot spare.
The stone is woken by his own measurement, after his own wrong answer.
The runner is cut from the last timber in the place and the cost is
permanent.
The creature is unharmed, unbefriended and unexplained, and the race is
lost by a margin Nick can compute, because he chose to spend it.

## LOCATION ROSTER

Three recurring locations. No two confusable names.

1. **The Station.** A dead man's workshop cut into a ridge at the top
   of the world, with tools, iron, timber, and a wall of marks nobody
   living can explain. Nick's whole book happens within a hundred yards
   of its door. [TK name — PROPOSAL: never named on the page in any
   volume; "the station" in our docs only.]
2. **The Flats.** The open, wind-bared lichen ground below the station
   door, where the caribou feed and the sleigh must launch. Thin
   feed, counted in weeks, and running out — **the larder that is always
   nearly empty**, which is the corrected version of revision 1's
   contradiction (F5). It is also the ground something else's circuit
   crosses, so everything that happens in the open happens in view.
3. **Bronston.** The Alaskan mining town far south, richer this year
   than it has been in a decade and unable to say why. Grace's quarter
   of the book. Rob's counter, Jim's kitchen and the meeting are rooms
   inside it, not separate locations.

Single-visit, named but uncounted: the slope above the station door
with the watching-bed on it; the far end of the circuit, where the
cache is set; the Juno waterfront; the rock at the creek; the shore ice
where the fog puts him down; the beach where Donald wakes; the southern
port where he hears the story.

## SENSORY INVENTORY

- Cold with nothing in it, no smell at all, no sound but his own pulse,
  and then the door, and behind it tallow, lamp oil, cold iron and the
  dry sweet smell of stored timber.
- The dying light as a countable thing. The same ridge notch every
  morning, the sun a hand lower, then a finger, then a rumor, then blue
  on blue with no horizon in it.
- The animal almost never seen and constantly present. The caribou
  going rigid mid-chew, a latch off its pin, tracks with a stride he
  cannot make sense of, and one long look across thirty feet of open
  snow that the book spends two hundred pages earning and then never
  repeats.
- Worked wood as the book's smell of hope. Steam, hot shavings,
  scorched clamps, the ring of a plane on end grain in a room where
  nothing else makes a sound.
- The stone's whole sensory signature, withheld and returned. Dead rock
  through a mitt for three quarters of the book, a faint wrong warmth
  at the first bad fix, and blood warmth in the palm at the moment it
  is finally told where it is.
- The launch under the aurora. No sun anywhere on the planet, green
  water moving overhead, the harness creaking, and one animal a long
  way off in the dark with its head down, working.

## THE STATION FORK — the author's ruling (F2 + F3)

**Not decided here.** The adversarial pass raised two linked
objections, and they are one ruling.
F2: the station demotes Nick from originator to successor, because a
man already kept a workshop at the top of the world and Nick moved in.
F3: the station is the series' largest coincidence, and its cedar,
iron and forges have no stated origin in a country with no road, no
trees and no harbor.

The three rulings on the table are **(a) a kept workshop**, **(b) a
wreck or depot cache**, and **(c) an empty shelter**.
This revision is written to survive all three by keeping the fixed
facts abstract in the prose.
What is fixed: there is shelter; there is some worked material; a man
was here and is not here now; there is a wall of marks; the material is
finite and Nick spends it to the last piece.
What is variable: how furnished the place is, and where the material
came from.

| Passage | (a) kept workshop | (b) wreck or cache | (c) empty shelter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1, "shelter, and iron, and worked timber, and a bench somebody kept" | keep as written | "a ship's iron, deck timber, and a bench somebody knocked together out of it" | "shelter, a little iron, and nothing else — the timber is the sleigh's own cargo box and what he can salvage" |
| Q4, "the last good timber in the place" | the dead man's own bench | a deck beam | the door Nick hung himself, which makes the cost his own work twice over |
| The wall of marks | the keeper cut it | a survivor cut it | somebody cut it and it is the only thing left of him |
| SERIES LEDGER "LOCKS — THE WORKSHOP" | needs rewording; under (a) the priority problem is real and the lock should read *Nick makes it his* rather than *a shop kept by one man* | clean; the shop is Nick's, built out of a dead expedition's materials | cleanest for SC1; the workshop is Nick's from the first plank |
| The landing's coincidence (F3) | needs a stated reason the stone put him within walking distance [TK] | a compass keyed to worked iron, or to the wreck, makes the landing causal at one line's cost | needs the same [TK]; the shelter is a bare accident of geology |
| The cedar problem | unsolved; needs a supply route, and a supply route collapses the isolation | solved outright; the wreck brought the wood, the iron and the man | solved by having almost none, which makes the book harder and better |
| Book 3's stocked station and Book 4's home (F10) | inherits fully furnished | inherits salvage plus whatever Nick has added | inherits only what Nick has built, which is the strongest series arc and the largest rewrite |

Two notes the ruling should carry.
First, the one defense of the current draft worth preserving under any
ruling is that Nick **surpasses** the man before him — he does what
that man never dared, which is to stop defending his work and offer it.
Second, whatever the ruling, the wood must have a stated origin,
because the climax's cost structure depends on the material being
finite and countable.
