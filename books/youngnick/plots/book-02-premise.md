## One sentence

Stranded by a magic he barely understands at a dead man's workshop
north of every map, with a cracked sleigh, three starving caribou, and
a date he knows by heart because the only living person who believes
he is alive sails for San Francisco on it, Nick has to rebuild the one
thing that can carry him south before the sun goes down for the
winter — while something enormous and patient begins taking his work
apart, one piece at a time, exactly the way it took the man who kept
this place before him.

## Back cover

Everyone in Bronston, Alaska, believes Nick died on the mountain, and
he needs them to go on believing it, because a dead man can put back
what the living stole and never be thanked for it. One person knows
better. Grace stood with him in the dark at the top of a cliff and
heard the whole impossible story, and she is the only soul on earth
who will ever be told, and she is leaving; there is a steamer out of
Juno before the ice closes the channel and a medical school in San
Francisco at the far end of it, and the two of them said the sailing
date out loud to each other once, quietly, the way people say a thing
they intend to keep. Then the glowing stone Nick dug out of a cave
wall carried him north, past the last of everything, and set him down
on ice with a runner cracked through, three caribou standing on ground
with nothing on it to eat, and the stone gone cold as any other rock
in his hand. Above the shelf he finds a seam of light that turns out
to be a door, and behind the door a workshop — cold forges, good
tools, seasoned wood, and a long wall covered end to end in a dead
man's tally marks. Nick reads marks in stone for a living. He counts
them, gets a number that means years, feels sorry for whoever worked
out here that long with nobody to tell, and gets to work, because the
sun drops a measurable amount against the ridge every morning and he
has done the arithmetic and it is going to be close. Then something
comes into the shop in the night and takes the wood he shaped and
leaves the meat lying beside it. Something lifts a latched door off
its pin without breaking it. Something walks around his deadfall
instead of into it, and has been lying up on the slope watching the
door for longer than he has been alive to the fact that he is watched.
He tells himself it is a bear for as long as he can stand to. And when
he finally reads that wall the way a surveyor should have read it the
first time — not years of labor but a count of returns, the gaps
between them getting shorter, the last group stopping in the middle of
itself — he understands two things in the same breath. The man before
him did not wander off. And every hour this animal costs him is an
hour off the only date that matters.

## Q1 — The Setup

The stone puts him down on ice in the last of an afternoon, and the
first thing Nick does is what his father drilled into him for weather
he did not understand, which is count. Three caribou, still in
harness. One runner cracked through at the curve. One stone, warm in
his hand an hour ago in a cave a thousand miles south, now dead cold.
No smoke, no track, no sound at all — a silence so complete he can
hear the blood in his own ears. He finds the door in the ridge because
he goes looking for the seam of light he glimpsed coming down, and
behind it is a shop any craftsman would weep over and no man has stood
in for a long time. Forges cold. A drawknife and a plane hung the way
a careful man hangs them. Wood stacked to season further, by someone
who expected to come back for it. And the long wall cut end to end
with tally marks. He counts them, gets years, and stands there feeling
sorry for a stranger. Then he goes to work, because he has a date.
Grace said it aloud to him in the dark, the last thing either of them
said, and boats do not wait. So he takes the only measurement the
country offers: the sun rising lower each morning against the same
notch in the ridge, the difference small, measurable, and measured. It
tells him the light will be gone before he is ready. He cuts two rows
of his own marks beside the dead man's — days of sun, days to the
boat — and begins steaming wood for a runner. That night something
comes into the shop. In the morning the meat he left out is untouched,
and every piece he shaped is gone.

A thousand miles south, Grace is discovering what it costs to be the
only person who knows. At Rob's counter the story of the gold that
appeared on every porch in Bronston is already being improved by
people who were there, and she stands in it and says nothing. Her
father is selling the stove and talking about San Francisco like a man
who has been holding his breath for twenty-five years. At his table
the passage is booked out loud, with a date on it. She writes the date
down. She does not mention the other thing she has been counting, or
who she has been counting it for, because there is no version of that
sentence a sane person says in a kitchen.

## Q2 — Build, and the Point of No Return

A man alone can do the work of a shop if the shop is good enough, and
this one is, and for a while Nick is almost happy: steam and scarf
joints, the dead man's clamps, the smell of hot wood, a runner coming
true under his hands. He is also being taken apart. Not attacked —
audited. His shaped wood goes and his food stays. A latch lifts off
its pin without a mark on it. He builds a deadfall the way he was
taught and finds, in the morning, a line of tracks that comes to it,
stops, goes around, and picks the same heading up on the other side.
The caribou tell him it is coming before he ever hears it; they go
rigid and stop chewing, all three at once, and that is the only alarm
he gets. He calls it a bear out loud, to himself, in an empty
workshop, and the word starts to sound like something he is saying on
purpose. Then he loses a whole night's work and goes back to the wall
in a temper to add a mark to his own row — and sees, finally, what he
has been looking at every day for weeks. The marks are not evenly
spaced. They cluster. The clusters repeat at an interval that anyone
who has read six sample tins at six depths would have caught in an
hour, and the gaps between the last of them get shorter and shorter,
and then the count stops in the middle of a group. The man who kept
this station was not tallying his own years. He was tallying
something's returns, and learning its rhythm, and he was still
learning it when it ended. Nick climbs the slope above the door and
finds the bed — packed snow, a sightline straight onto his own
doorway, used, and used long before he arrived. The overland walk-out
is still barely possible while there is light. It means leaving the
caribou to starve and the sleigh where it lies. He goes back inside
and stays, and that night he does something he has never done in his
life. He writes.

Far down the coast, on a beach nobody has a name for, a man wakes up
who is supposed to be dead. He does not know where he is either. The
first thing Donald Grin does, when he can talk, is give the people who
pull him out of the surf somebody else's name.

## Q3 — The Major Setback

The runner comes off the forms. It holds. The first flight is short
and ugly and nearly the last one — the sleigh lifts, yaws, and puts
him down hard on the flats — but it lifts, and for one evening the
arithmetic still closes: airborne before the light goes, Juno by the
sailing, with a day to spare and nothing to spend it on. He learns
something else that evening, and does not understand yet that it is
the most valuable thing he will learn all year. The tallow he left out
in the snow, well away from the bench, is gone. The tallow on the
bench, beside his tools, has not been touched. Then the stone. He
seats it in the sleigh's socket the way it was seated the night it
brought him here and it lies there like gravel, because the sky he
sighted it against does not exist up here; the star he locked his
course to in Bronston sits nearly overhead in this country, and an
instrument that reads one sky cannot read another without being told
where it is. That is survey work, and it is days of it, and he does
not have days. He is on the flats with a plumb line and a frozen face
when the animal comes out of the blue dusk in the open, on ground
where nothing can hide, and takes the new runner off the sleigh and
breaks it, unhurried, thirty feet from a man who cannot reach it in
time and would be no use if he could. It looks at him while it does
it. Then the sun sets on the last afternoon of the year, and does not
come back up, and somewhere south of him in the dark a steamer takes
on coal and casts off on the date they both said out loud, and Nick
stands in a cold workshop with a wrecked runner, a dead stone, and a
wall of marks proving he is standing exactly where the last man stood.
He wins nothing that week. He writes one line on the wall by
sleigh-glow, in the first person, for whoever comes next, because
there is no longer anyone else to write to. *I did the arithmetic
right. I had the wrong idea about what was counting.*

In Bronston, Grace tries to buy time and finds out what it costs. She
goes to the dock on a Thursday and asks the boat men, carefully, the
way you ask about weather, whether anyone travels north of the
channel, and whether anyone has seen lights out that way — and gets
back a story, and a laugh, and the fact she was afraid of, which is
that people who go north of there do not come back to be asked about
it. She asks the agent to move the passage. The agent explains the
ice, and the next sailing, and the word he uses is spring. Her father
has sold the stove. The school term does not care. So Grace does the
arithmetic too, and it comes out the same for her as it does for him,
and she starts packing a house she grew up in, for a boat she now has
to be on.

## Q4 — Climax and Aftermath

Nick stops trying to beat the animal and starts trying to read it,
which is the only thing the dead man's wall was ever good for. He
works the intervals like a set of samples: the gaps shorten as the
country empties, which is not menace, it is hunger — the thing is
circling a route that no longer feeds it, and everything alive on that
route is standing on his flats in harness. He can predict the hour it
will be at the far end of its circuit. He cannot predict a fight and
he cannot win one, and traps are worse than useless against something
that reads them. So he does what the keeper never dared: he does not
arrange his work around the visits, he arranges the visit. He walks
out into the polar dark along a route he has only ever seen tracks on,
carrying his own crossing rations — everything he needs to survive
days in the air with nothing under him — and sets them down bare on
open snow, far from any shaped wood, any steel, any smell of a man's
tools, because that is the one rule this animal has taught him about
what it will and will not take. He backs away. It comes while he can
still see it. That is not a friendship and it is not a truce; it is a
starving thing taking food from a man who is too frightened to run,
and it buys him hours, and the hours are all he wanted. Then he
spends them: the last runner cut out of the keeper's own bench,
because there is nothing else left in the world to cut it from, the
joint made by feel and lamplight, the stone finally sighted to the sky
it is actually under and coming up warm in his palm, the harness knots
he has tied since he was a boy, the three caribou leaning into it, and
the shelf dropping away under him with no sun anywhere on earth and
the aurora going over like water. He looks down once. It is at the
cache, feeding, and it lifts its head to watch him go.

He flies south for days on nothing. Juno has ice in the slips and a
harbor with a hole in it where a steamer was, and the date on the
agent's board is a date that has already gone by, and there is nothing
to do at an empty dock but stand on it. Bronston is a dark house with
the stove sold out of it. He goes to the rock at the creek where two
children carved their names and a dead man once cut a heart around
them, and finds the letter she left for a man she had no earthly right
to expect, weighted under a stone, written the way he writes on his
wall now — for a stranger, in the hope that someone comes. It does not
say goodbye. It says how long, and what she means to be qualified to
do when she is done, and where he can find her at the end of it. Years,
in her handwriting. He turns north with it inside his coat.

We watch her leave, because it is the last thing she owes this story.
Grace at the rail of a mail boat with her father beside her, the
town's boards crowded because a town this size turns out for
departures, the channel bending, and Grace looking north over the
stern at a country that has never once answered her — and staying
there looking long after the sensible thing would have been to go
inside. Far to the south, in a port town where nobody knows him, a
thin man with a new name and ruined hands listens to a story two
sailors are telling about an Alaskan mining camp that woke up rich,
and about the thing the local people say came in the night to do it.
He does not laugh. He asks, very politely, where.

# APPARATUS

## HAUGE MAP

**OUTER GOAL.** Reach Grace before the steamer sails from Juno for San
Francisco. The image of it achieved: a young woman on a dock, and a
boat still tied. It is a person and a date, not a place; the polar
sunset is the physical obstacle standing between him and that harbor,
because with the light goes the work, the forage, and the navigation.

**INNER JOURNEY.** Identity → essence, one series-scale stage
(quarter-turn, per `studio/craft/hauge.md` series mode and the §12
four-volume shape).

- Wound (inherited, T09): his father spent his working life at other
  men's benefit and was never marked for it, and Nick called that
  weakness. Book 1 began the answer with one act of restraint. It is
  not finished.
- Identity Nick starts in: *the miracle was an accident on my way
  home.* The sleigh, the giving, the station are all a delay between
  him and the life he came back for.
- Essence he earns: *the work is mine, and it counts even if no one
  ever reads the wall.* The tally wall is the wound made into an
  object — years of unwitnessed work that he misreads at first exactly
  as he misread his father.
- Why the goal is unwinnable from inside the identity: treating the
  station as a delay is what kills him. Only a man who takes the place
  up as his own reads the wall correctly, survives the count, and gets
  airborne — and by then the boat is gone. He wins the fight and loses
  the race, and the essence victory is what costs him the outer goal.

## THE CLOCK

Two hands on one dial, both cut into the same wall, both visible to
the reader by chapter two.

1. **The sailing.** The last steamer south out of Juno before the ice,
   on a date Grace said aloud to Nick in Book 1's away-from-town scene
   and he has not stopped counting since [TK exact date; see OQ3 — the
   plant is owed by Book 1, not by this book].
2. **The sun.** At this latitude the sun sets for the winter [TK
   date], a measurable number of days before the sailing. With the
   light go the work hours, the forage, and the navigation, so the
   practical deadline is the sunset, not the sailing.

Both counts are scratched by Nick himself into the keeper's wall, in
two fresh rows beside a stranger's, and updated on the page. The
creature's structural function is that it eats the margin between
them, theft by theft, and the reader can watch it happen in chalk-cut
stone. At ~75% the sun goes and the sailing passes; the clock does not
stop, it changes meaning — from *how long do I have* to *how long has
it been*.

**Red herrings, declared.** (1) Nick's first reading of the wall —
years of unthanked work — which is sentimental, wrong, and the exact
mistake he made about his father. (2) The bear. The word is his, not
the book's, and he keeps using it after it stops fitting. (3) The
empty station read as abandonment; the keeper is assumed to have
wandered off or gone south, and the latch broken from the outside is
on the page long before Nick lets it mean anything.

**Nothing resolved by luck.** The window at the climax is bought with
food he cannot spare. The stone is woken by his own measurement. The
runner is cut from the last wood in the world and the cost is
permanent. The creature survives, unharmed and unbefriended, and the
race is lost because the antagonist won the hours it was always trying
to win.

## SENSORY INVENTORY

- Cold with nothing in it — no smell at all, no sound but his own
  pulse — and then the shop door, and forty years of cedar shavings,
  tallow, lamp oil, and cold iron waiting behind it.
- The dying light as a countable thing: the same ridge notch every
  morning, the sun a hand lower, then a finger, then a rumor, then
  blue on blue with no horizon in it at all.
- The animal is almost never seen and constantly present — the
  caribou going rigid mid-chew, a latch off its pin, tracks with a
  stride he cannot make sense of, and one long look across thirty feet
  of open snow that the book spends two hundred pages earning.
- Worked wood as the book's smell of hope: steam, hot shavings,
  scorched clamps, the ring of a plane on end grain in a room where
  nothing else makes a sound.
- The stone's whole sensory signature, withheld and returned — dead
  rock through a mitt for three quarters of the book, and faint blood
  warmth in the palm at the moment it is finally told where it is.
- The launch under the aurora: no sun anywhere on the planet, green
  water moving overhead, the harness creaking, and one animal below
  eating in the dark and lifting its head.

## OPEN QUESTIONS

1. **The creature's name.** The book never says yeti or abominable
   snowman and never explains the animal; the author rules the name.
   Options drawn from what the keeper wrote on the wall and in his

2. **Series POV rule for the bible.** Nothing in `STORY_BIBLE.md`
