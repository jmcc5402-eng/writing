# The mute leads — where Nick and Grace can speak (2026-08-11)

**PROPOSED — instrument output; four author rulings at the end.**

_developmental-editor, variance card E6 (weight what's missing over
what's wrong). Answers THREADS T10: Nick and Grace exchange zero words
in any adopted or trunk chapter, and the author's own question — "will
this book have enough dialogue?"_

---

## The reframe

**T10 is not a dialogue problem. It is a superior-position problem
wearing dialogue's clothes.** From the end of ch1 to the climax — 85%
of the book — the reader knows the most important fact in the story
and Grace does not: he is alive. Suspense lives in that gap, but a
knowledge gap can only be SPENT in scenes where both parties are in
the same room. Otherwise it just accrues. This book has the longest
superior-position gap on its shelf and **two scenes in which to spend
it**: one before the gap opens (the ch2 balk) and one at 90% (the
catch, where she is unconscious). The reader is handed a loaded gun on
page 30 and does not see it fired until page 260.

## The constraint that was too strict — a live canon contradiction

The brief for this pass asserted that Grace hearing Nick before the
climax would break the vanish. **That is stricter than §7.9b actually
says.** The lock is *town-scoped*: "The town never learns Nick lived…
Grace alone holds the whole truth… and keeps it." Nothing in it limits
what Grace and Nick say to each other.

And three canon or canon-adjacent documents put a spoken scene at
~60%:

- Bible §4: the dock is "site of the central Nick-and-Grace
  misunderstanding."
- Bible §6 Book 3: "Grace waits for the mail boat; **Nick calls to
  her**; each misreads the other."
- `notes/book-1-copy.md` Q3: "When he tries to reach Grace at the
  dock, **every word goes wrong**."
- Archive sketch: "Its Nick! … She is shocked. Nick says there isn't
  much time…"

Read literally, **the dock misunderstanding IS a conversation and
always has been.** If the stricter reading stands, the book's own
named central scene is dead and the misunderstanding engine has no
on-page carrier. This is the ruling that decides the most (Ruling 1).

## 1. The pre-push window

Exactly two places exist; one is closed by canon.

**Ch2 flashback, the boardwalk (v2 l.510–561) — the only window.**
The entire pre-push romance is eighteen lines:

> She stopped on the walk to settle the basket. / If she had looked up
> she'd have been looking straight at him. / Her name was right there
> in his mouth. / One word. Two steps off the boards and one word. /
> He stood there and held his sack of beans / like a man holding sacks
> of beans was his whole trade, / and said nothing.

**Recommendation: play it as a failed conversation.** Nick's identity
is *the man who cannot say the thing*, and an identity must be tested
and hold before the climax can break it. As drafted he is never
tested: she never looks up, he is unseen, it costs him nothing.
Silence at a distance from a woman who doesn't know you're there is a
near-miss, not a balk. A balk is: she sees him, she is glad, and he
stands in front of her and talks about redwoods.

What it buys: the climax becomes a reversal instead of a first
attempt; Grace's ch4 engine ("Why had he not come to find her?")
becomes *he came, and said nothing that mattered, and I let him go
too* — which gives her a flaw instead of pure victimhood; a plantable
echo line to answer on the cliff; and it puts the ring on the page
(the jacket copy promises "a ring he hasn't found the nerve to
mention"; no ring exists in any manuscript). [TK: is there a physical
ring, and does he still have it on the mountain?]

**Containment:** do NOT extend the flashback — trade inside it. The
Rob exchange runs nine spoken lines to deliver three things and can
carry them in half. Spend the recovered lines on Grace, cap at ~12–20
lines, end on the same image. Chapter length stays flat and ch2's
SC4 survival tension is undiluted.

**The three weeks: closed, leave shut.** Rob's "saw him but the once"
and the stove men's "hadn't hardly been back a month" lock Nick to one
town visit; Grace's ch4 collapse depends on his never having come.

**Ch4 — a free, non-breaking gain.** Rob witnessed the balk (he said
"You all right, son?") and never testifies to it. Four lines at the
counter — telling her *where* he saw the boy and *when*, across the
street from her basket, and he didn't cross — converts Rob from a
plant-payer into the man holding the fact, and gives Grace evidence
instead of inference.

**Ch5 — no window, correctly.** But log the consequence: as briefed,
the entire Donald–Grace relationship exists as one line in a villain's
mouth and then an off-page captivity.

## 2. The post-climax window

Generous, because the vanish is town-scoped. Three dramatic
constraints:

1. **She is unconscious for the entire drafted rescue**, and the trunk
   jumps to her already in town. **The waking is the missing scene**
   — it exists in no prose in this repo. Recommendation: **she wakes
   in the air**, with the caribou in front of them and the country
   underneath, which is the only place where "and then he has to go"
   is a fact she can see rather than a thing he asserts.
2. **Do not re-explain the proposal** — she already has it from
   Donald. This is two people holding the same fact and negotiating
   what to do with it.
3. **It must end on her CHOOSING the silence.** The book's thesis —
   giving without credit — has never been dramatized as a choice;
   it is a condition imposed on Nick by the plot. Grace is the one
   character who *could* give him the credit, standing in front of a
   town that would love him for it, and doesn't. That is the
   thematic climax the book currently lacks.

Budget: one scene, 600–900 words, away from town, mostly not about the
truth; the belt return is its physical business.

**A repair this unlocks:** the trunk's "Maybe it was a dream, but I
remember seeing him" currently contradicts §7.9b. Keep the words,
change who is fooled — she says it to her father *knowing exactly what
she saw*. Same line, now a lie, now the vanish's cost paid in her own
mouth, setting up "Nick who?" as something she personally purchased.

## 3. The wider absence (card E6)

Measured: across four adopted chapters (~8,000 words) there are three
two-person conversations. Across the entire 23,000-word trunk after
the push, Nick speaks to another human **once**. In a complete draft,
the protagonist addresses another person roughly twenty times.

**The addressee the book already invented and dropped:**

> "Some days it goes like that," Nick said to the eagle (v2 l.87)
> "That's not a fire," he said, mostly to hear a voice. (v2 l.748)

Two plants about a man needing to hear a human voice, no payer. Make
it a running device — Nick talks to the animals as he talked to the
eagle, and by the time the caribou arrive he has a team to talk to.
The last thing he says before the warp, he says to them.

**The three most valuable ABSENT conversations:**

**A. Nick and his father, the morning of the earthquake.** The wound
is asserted in narration ("angry at his father… over how his father
took it, quiet, every time"); the scene does not exist. The ruled
climax turns on Nick throwing Donald the line — restraint chosen over
revenge, the father finally answered — and right now that answers a
paraphrase. Placement: the lowest moment (before the caribou), where
it *turns* him. [TK: what the father said back — canon offers only
"the order was the explanation," which is a promising key.]

**B. Donald and Grace, once, on the page.** Bible §3 has him
courting/coercing her; the page has nothing but a rumor. The dock
misunderstanding requires Nick to read her as *waiting for Donald* —
built on hearsay, the reader feels the plot arranging itself. It also
means her Book 3 captivity is the first time she and the antagonist
ever interact. One scene, Grace POV, before the dock: Donald asks for
something and she must refuse a man who can fire her father.

**C. The service for Nick, overheard from the trees.** Promised in the
jacket copy, exists in no manuscript. It is **a whole town talking** —
what a book with three conversations needs most — and it gives a
second, escalated balk: in ch2 he could have spoken and didn't; here
he *cannot*, and she is twenty yards away weeping.

Runners-up: Grace lying to Jim about the bank letter (the porch
scene's echo, with a lie in it); a second Nick–Rob exchange.

## 4. The rulings — two minutes

**Ruling 1 — the dock. Rule this first; it decides the most.** Does
Grace hear Nick's voice at ~60%?
- **1A — Yes** (recommended). The dock is a spoken two-hander per
  bible §4/§6 and the jacket copy. Grace knows he lives from 60% and
  keeps it. Costs: the trunk's "maybe it was a dream" re-points to a
  deliberate lie (an improvement); Grace's Book 3 captivity rebuilds
  around a woman who knows; the vanish is untouched, being
  town-scoped.
- **1B — No.** First words land at 90%. Costs: the romance rides one
  late scene, Grace's second act stays reactive, and bible §4, §6 and
  the jacket copy all need amending.

**Ruling 2 — the ch2 balk.** 2A: play it failed, ~12–20 lines, funded
by compressing Rob so chapter length stays flat (recommended). 2B:
hold the silence as drafted.

**Ruling 3 — the post-climax scene.** One scene, 600–900 words, away
from town, Grace awake in the air, ending on her choosing the silence
(recommended; §7.9b already implies it, nobody has written it).

**Ruling 4 — the addressee.** Nick talks aloud to animals as a running
device, planted at v2 l.87 and l.748, paid by the caribou
(recommended; costs nothing, needs no new canon).

If there is time for only one: **Ruling 1.**

## Hauge audit (compact)

- **Braid:** sound. The ruled climax is unwinnable from inside the
  identity, which is correct. Weakness: the identity is never tested
  and held on the page (Ruling 2).
- **Turning points:** Opportunity ~15%, on spec. **Drift at 75%:** the
  trunk leaves the gold behind and the very next chapter hands him the
  caribou — setback and cure in adjacent chapters, so "all is lost"
  never plays. The father conversation (3A) is what fills it.
- **Superior position:** the book's best asset, squandered by absence
  of shared scenes. Keep the stove-men's cheerful "positive sample"
  talk exactly as is — genuinely chilling.
- **Orphaned echoes:** the talking-aloud lines; the crevice that
  swallows a rock with no sound (a superb plant for the drop that
  nothing collects); "the order was the explanation," said twice and
  never turned — say it a third time at the cliff with a different
  meaning.

## What must survive revision

The Grace–Jim porch scene (v2 l.862–978) — *"There's tea. There's
nearly tea."* — is better than most published dialogue in this lane
and proves the book can do people in rooms talking. Don't touch it.
Also: the vanish as a town-scoped lock (protect it, stop over-reading
it), ch1's fragments-through-wind, and ch2–ch3's SC4 discipline —
every addition above is funded from existing lines, not added on top.

**And the silence itself.** The target is not a chattier book. It is
four conversations that don't exist, one running device for a man
alone, and one ruling about the dock. The loneliness is the point; it
just has to be *spent* rather than merely endured.
