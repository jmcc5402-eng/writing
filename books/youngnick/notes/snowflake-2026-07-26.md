## One-page overview

At twenty-one, Nick — a gentle, capable outdoorsman who has spent ten years
logging redwoods in Northern California — returns to Bronston, the starving
Alaskan gold-mining town of his childhood. He tells himself he came back
because he loves the place; the truth, which he cannot say aloud even to
himself, is that he came back for Grace, the childhood sweetheart he cannot
work up the nerve to speak to. He carries his dead father's survival creed —
*"Shelter, fire, food in the cold. Shelter, food, fire in the warm."* — and a
wound: his father let old man Grin push him around, Nick read that restraint
as weakness, and he was angry at his father the day the earthquake killed him.
He fears the weakness is in him too. The balk at Grace's door seems to prove
it.

On a sample expedition for Grin Bros Mining, Nick overhears a fragment of an
argument he isn't meant to hear. That evening the Grin brothers beat him at
the campfire and kick him off a hundred-foot cliff. He survives — caught by an
eagle's nest, evading the kill-check above — and shelters in a hidden cave,
where a red glow under the dirt leads him to a buried sleigh carved with
ornate grooves he half-recognizes, one middle groove empty, waiting. Days
later, in town, Grace — almost twenty, fiercely smart, bound to her widowed
father and dreaming of medical school in San Francisco to fight the typhoid
that killed her mother — learns that Nick is dead.

Presumed dead, Nick discovers the sleigh flies. He also discovers why he was
pushed: the Grins are not skimming the town's annuity gold, they have stolen
*all* of it, controlling the bank statements, printing forged invoices, and
stockpiling the gold in a hidden cove vault for their escape to San Francisco.
Nick becomes the town's secret benefactor — chocolate on every porch, then
gold — while a misread meeting at the dock convinces him Grace is Donald's,
and convinces her that the half-seen figure who called out and fled cares
nothing for her. When Grace intercepts a real bank letter showing the accounts
empty, she confronts the Grins and is taken hostage; Donald tells her the one
thing Nick never could — that Nick came back to propose.

An earthquake collapses the mine and forces the endgame. Nick tames three
caribou to pull the overloaded sleigh, steals the stolen gold off the Grins'
own escape boat, and comes back for Grace on the cliff — where he finally
faces Donald's sneer that he is soft like his father, and answers it with his
father's kind of strength. Grace frees herself; Donald, grabbing for gold
instead of ground, is taken by the river. Nick returns Grace and the town's
fortune to the dock by night and vanishes without credit. The town wakes
rich, calls it a miracle, and cannot remember his name — *"Nick who?"* After
the confession the two of them finally earn, Nick digs a glowing compass
stone from the cave wall, recognizes his father's belt emblems in its
markings, sets it in the sleigh's empty groove — and the North Star pulls him
into the sky, toward parts unknown. The reader, assembling sleigh, reindeer,
secret gifts, and North, realizes what December story they have been told.
The word "Santa" is never said.

## Four-paragraph overview

Nick, twenty-one, returns after ten years to Bronston, a remote Alaskan
mining town starving on hope: every family's savings sit in an annuity the
Grin brothers administer, payable next summer. He came back to propose to
Grace but balks at every chance, and she doesn't even know he's back
[PROPOSED: one failed conversation instead of a silent glimpse — see §4].
When Nick overhears the Grins' tent argument on a sample expedition, they
ambush him and kick him off a cliff. He survives unseen, shelters in a cave,
and digs a glowing red sleigh out of the dirt — carved with grooves he almost
knows, one socket empty — while in town, Grace decides to leave for medical
school the same week she learns Nick has "died."

The middle escalates on two rails. Nick, presumed dead, learns the sleigh
flies, feeds himself by eagle egg and Inuit trade, and starts giving in
secret — chocolate on every porch, then nuggets of gold quietly taken back
from the Grins' skim — while the town invents angels to explain its luck.
But the truth is worse than a skim: following the gold run to a hidden cove,
Nick finds a vault holding *all* of it — the mine is nearly dry, the annuity
is fiction held up by forged invoices, and the Grins are provisioning an
escape boat. The dock misunderstanding locks the lovers apart: Nick calls to
Grace as Donald's boat arrives, reads the scene wrong, and bolts; Grace is
left sure the figure she half-saw wants nothing to do with her.

The vise closes. A real bank letter reaches Grace showing $34.42 where her
father's three thousand should be; she confronts the Grins and is taken to
the cove cabin, where Donald — courting her by coercion all along — reveals
that Nick returned to propose. An earthquake collapses the mine, torches the
cabin, and starts the Grins' clock: load the boat and run. Nick's first
heist fails when the loaded sleigh won't lift; at his lowest, watching a
caribou drift near his camp, he has the idea that completes the picture —
three caribou in harness, a flying team — and in the chaos of Grace's
crowbar escape he lands on the Grins' own deck and takes the gold back.

Everything converges on the cliff point: Donald holds a knife, needles Nick
with his father's weakness, and Nick finally understands restraint as
strength; Grace frees herself with an elbow, and the aftershock takes Donald
down with the gold he lunged for instead of the ledge [PROPOSED rebuild —
the draft resolves this by luck]. Nick catches the falling Grace on the
sleigh's skis, lays her at the town dock beside the crates, and vanishes —
the town divides its restored fortune and toasts a miracle no one can name.
Grace, who knows now why he came back, finds him for the confession neither
could start; then Nick, alone in the cave, unearths a compass-inscribed
stone whose markings echo his father's belt, sets it into the sleigh's empty
groove, and is pulled by the North Star into the sky. The town wakes rich;
the reader wakes to the first Christmas morning.

## Chapter-by-chapter

1. **The Push** (Nick; old ch1) — Nick takes the summit sample for Grin Bros
   Mining, working by his dead father's creed and carrying one sour memory:
   he was angry at his father the day he died. Back at camp he overhears

2. **The Ledge** (Nick; old ch2) — The fall ends with Nick's arm rammed into
   an eagle's nest eight feet above the killing river, where he actively

   return — Grace outside the market, the balk — plants the arc's starting
   position, and the sunset traverse ends on the turn: "A cave."

3. **The Glow** (Nick; old ch5) — Nick survives the night by the creed —
   wind-wall, pine-needle bed, rationed jerky — and wakes from a dream of a
   grill's red glow to find the glow still there, deeper in the cave. Digging
   at the warm dirt he uncovers a handle, then glowing wood carved with
   ornate grooves he half-recognizes and cannot place, one middle groove
   empty — "A red glowing sleigh."

4. **The News in Town** (Grace; old ch3+ch4 merged) — Days later, Grace tells
   her father Jim she will enroll at the university, their porch conversation
   carrying her mother's typhoid death and her doctor dream, then walks Old
   Rob's thin-shelved market where the returned Grins' "positive sample" news
   has hope running high. The lowered voices tell her Nick [TK surname] was
   killed on the expedition; the flour drops, and she resolves to post the
   ferry letter anyway — "she had always assumed that one day Nick would be
   the man she married."

5. **The Counting House** (Grins; old ch6) — Donald and James count the day's
   take in their cold cabin, James mooning over New York flyers while Donald
   rehearses his public grief for the man he murdered. Duff's cargo will be
   ready in under two weeks, and Donald names the town's real asset: the
   metal, and its endless hope for more of it.

6. **The First Ride** (Nick; old ch7–8) — Starving, Nick frees the sleigh,
   finds a snowfield running a mile down toward forest, and rides the sleigh
   as a sled until the slope steepens toward a hidden ravine. Both brakes
   fail, he readies to jump — and the second lever lifts the runners off the
   snow: the sleigh is flying.

7. **Learning the Sky** (Nick; old ch8–9) — Nick learns the lever by nearly
   dying at a treeline, then spends the day mastering turns, dives, and
   landings, raiding eagle nests for the eggs he and his father once traded
   at Okikoke. Flying the pass no one can walk anymore, he trades with the
   Inuit, and decides: he didn't come back for gold, and he isn't leaving
   until he knows what the Grins are hiding.

8. **The Rock** (Grace; old ch9) — At the carved rock on the creek where she
   goes to think of her mother, Grace grieves Nick sideways — ashamed that
   her first feeling was hurt that he never came to find her. She resolves
   that when her fare arrives she leaves Alaska for good, and takes her
   father with her to a life where gold is not the only subject.

9. **Chocolate Morning** (Grace; old ch9) — On Jim's birthday, when there
   isn't flour enough for a cake, Grace finds a package of trader's chocolate
   on the porch — and so does every house on the street. The town splits it
   at the party and invents its first miracle story, hope arriving exactly
   the way the Grins never intended.

10. **The Weigh-In** (Nick; old ch10) — Nick glides down to the mine office
    at night and watches the evening ritual: receipts, scales, and the coal
    sack under the counter that lets the Grins swap weight for gold in every
    miner's sealed bag. He marks where the key to the hidden desk-safe lives
    and decides the town should get some of its own back.

11. **Robin Hood** (Nick; old ch11) — Inside the creaking office Nick
    rebalances the skim — gold out, coal in — freezing when Young Gus, son
    of the hound he grew up with, sniffs the door. He flies out with two bags to

12. **Gold on the Porch** (Grace; old ch12) — Mrs. Hannigan's dawn knock

### The audience ruling, applied

**Ruling:** older all-ages frontier myth (comps: *Klaus*, Baum's *Life &
Adventures of Santa Claus*, Guinn's *Autobiography of Santa Claus*), NOT MG
8–12. **Why:** canon fixes the leads at ~19–21 with a proposal plot and
coerced courtship; the swindle (vesting, forged invoices) is load-bearing
adult material; the draft's best beats ("Nick who?", chocolate morning) are
elegiac; Lane A would require rewriting the bible's spine. No analyst
evidence contradicts the character analyst's recommendation; the continuity
fact sheet (adult ages, annuity mechanics) independently supports it.
**Concretely, for ch1–4:**

- **Sentence length/register:** varied adult periods allowed; kill the
  MG-simple declaratives ("Falling would not be good") unless doing real
  voice work. Register target is the creed itself: plain, practical, quietly
  wise (bible §11). Fix the draft's tense instability — past tense
  throughout.
- **Content rendering:** the push is rendered with real violence and real
  fear — blows, the log, the kicks — unsoftened but not gory; the fraud is
  rendered as adult fraud, not a MacGuffin, though in ch4 its *mechanics*
  are capped (below) because full disclosure belongs to ch15–17.
- **Chapter length:** ~2,000–2,800 words per chapter (ch3 may run ~1,800);
  full novel targets 45–70k, so ch1–4 lands ~8,500–10,500 words total.
- **Density:** ≥80% scene by wordcount; summary only as connective tissue.
  No essay paragraphs — everything old ch3 told gets carried in dialogue or
  cut.
