## 1. THE REVIEW

*Register: The New York Times Book Review, holiday issue.*

The shelf of books that explain December's visitor is old and almost
uniformly gentle. L. Frank Baum raised the man among wood nymphs in
1902. Jeff Guinn took down his autobiography. Matt Haig sent a boy
called Christmas north to find the elves, and *Klaus* gave a feuding
arctic town a reclusive toymaker. Lovely books — and every one makes
the same opening move: it prints the name on the cover and spends the
rest of its pages explaining it. *Young Nicholas* [TK: author byline]
is the first book on this shelf with the nerve to run the trick in
reverse. It never says the name. It tells a gold-rush survival story so
flinty and so exactly observed that the other story — the one you have
known since before you could read — assembles itself behind your eyes,
like a present you were never meant to catch anyone leaving.

The survival story is this: Nick, twenty-one, a redwood logger come
home to a starving Alaskan mining town, is beaten at a campfire and
kicked off a cliff by his own employers, for a sentence he did not know
he overheard. "It was the Grin brothers." He lives by his dead father's
catechism — "Shelter, fire, food in the cold. Shelter, food, fire in
the warm." — and in a hidden cave, following a red light up through the
dirt, he finds the thing the book is too smart to explain. ★ He digs
"the way a starving man eats, past sense, past caution, grateful and a
little afraid of his own gratitude," and that sentence is the novel's
method entire: wonder arriving through frostbitten fingers, priced in
calories, paid on delivery.

What a dead man does with a buried marvel turns out to be a heist
story. The town's pooled wages are being stolen whole by the men who
keep the books, and Nick — who cannot be seen alive — becomes his
town's anonymous benefactor: chocolate on every porch one morning,
later gold in an egg sack, while the neighbors invent angels because no
likelier explanation is on offer. The romance runs on the same silence.
Grace — who "had always assumed that one day Nick would be the man she
married," and who learns he is dead across a store counter — is no
prize waiting to be airlifted; when a knife comes to her throat, she
elbows the man holding it before the sky can get there.

★ And the sky does get there: out of food and out of common sense,
Nick reasons his way to the maddest sane sentence of the season —
"Caribou increase the pulling power of a sleigh on the snow. Why
wouldn't caribou increase the pulling power of a sleigh through the
sky?" — and the word you have been holding behind your teeth for two
hundred pages still, magnificently, goes unsaid. ★ Every impossible
thing here is assembled on the page from things a patient reader could
learn — knots, weather, trapline arithmetic — until flight itself feels
less like sorcery than like the last merit badge.

The book spends its early flights like a miser — accidental,
overloaded, nearly fatal — and so has a fortune left for the end. ★ A
story that opens with a boy thrown from a cliff closes with him diving
from the sky to catch a falling girl at one, and everything the novel
has quietly taught — the knots, the beasts, the stolen gold, the unsaid
word — is in the air with him when he does.

Afterward the town wakes rich, calls it a miracle, and the book, with a
restraint its whole shelf should envy, declines the trumpet it has
earned. ★ Asked about her rescuer, the town gives the two most quietly
devastating words in this year's fiction: "Nick who?" Then a sleigh
turns north, a grey cloud takes it, and you may finally say — outside
the book, because the book never will — Santa.

*(~610 words)*
