Three of the author's four diagnosed faults are repaired. The goal is
now a person and a date rather than a place — the single biggest
improvement in the document. The antagonist is real and escalates on
a legible ladder (theft → latch → deadfall walkaround → destruction

## THE SINGLE MOST DANGEROUS WEAKNESS

**The book's engine stops at 75%.** The boat sails, the goal dies, and
the final quarter is a superbly built machine running with nothing to
race — and the document does not know it, because its own Hauge block
asserts the opposite.

---

## Tier 1 — these sink the book

### F1. The last quarter has no clock, and the essence choice costs him nothing

Q3 fires both clocks in one sentence — the sun sets AND "a steamer
takes on coal and casts off on the date they both said out loud" —
even though the premise's own CLOCK block specifies them as offset:
"the sun sets for the winter [TK date], **a measurable number of days
before the sailing.**"

Consequence: the outer goal dies at 75%, killed by three things done
*to* Nick (broken runner, dark stone, sunset). The essence choice
happens at 90%. **A choice made after the goal is already lost cannot
be the thing that costs the goal.** The Hauge map's claim — "the
essence victory is what costs him the outer goal" — is not true of the
text. The CLOCK block half-confesses it: "the clock does not stop, it
changes meaning — from *how long do I have* to *how long has it
been*." Beautiful line, structural admission.

**THE HIGHEST-LEVERAGE FIX, and it is a reordering, not an
invention.** Un-collapse the clocks: the sun sets at 75%; **the
steamer sails during the climax.** Nick reaches Q4 with runner broken,
stone dark, sun gone, and the sailing still barely reachable, with
margin for exactly one thing. He can bolt — half-finished, cache
untouched, and probably make the date. Or he can buy the window with
his own crossing rations, cut the last runner from the keeper's bench,
and do the work properly. He chooses the second. The boat sails while
he is in the air. That gives the last quarter a live clock, converts
the tragedy from something that happens to him into something he
chooses, and makes the Hauge claim literally true for the first time.

### F2. The station demotes Nick from originator to successor [R1 — CONFIRMED]

The LOCKS block names it: "a shop at the top of the world, kept by one
man, working through the dark, for people who will never learn his
name." Grammatically that is Nick; factually it is the keeper. **The
keeper kept it. Nick found it kept.**

A cold reader reaches the successor conclusion at the door and never
leaves it: the workshop existed, a man already worked there alone in
the dark, and our hero moves in, learns his method, uses his clamps,
cuts up his bench, and inherits his vocabulary. The recognition SC1
exists to produce — *oh, this is how it began* — attaches to the
corpse.

Open question 4 proposes the keeper be an unrelated stranger, which
satisfies §7.7 to the letter (that decision is about **bloodline and
magic**) while leaving the real problem untouched: **priority, not
lineage.** Santa's workshop is not a place Santa found. If the
building, forges, tools, seasoned stock and the practice of solitary
unwitnessed work all predate Nick, the jacket promise — *the secret
origin* — is false inside its own second volume, and stays false for
three more, because the sketch makes this station Book 3's supply base
and Book 4's home.

Also note what the keeper's death does to the myth: the first man to
keep a workshop at the top of the world was eaten by something.

One real defense worth preserving: Nick *surpasses* the keeper — "he
does what the keeper never dared." Keep that whatever else changes.

**Fix:** give the keeper a stated, mundane, non-gift purpose, so that
everything Santa-shaped about the place is something Nick *does to
it* rather than something he finds in it.

### F3. The station is the series' largest coincidence, and the cedar has no origin

The fair-play table says "Nothing resolved by luck." Nothing is
*resolved* by luck; everything is *premised* on it. A magic stone
deposits a man at random north of every map, and within walking
distance is the one building in a thousand miles containing forges, a
drawknife, a plane, clamps, and seasoned wood — precisely and
exclusively what is needed to repair the precise thing that broke.

Then the harder question nothing answers. The sensory block says
"forty years of cedar shavings, tallow, lamp oil, and cold iron."
**Cedar does not grow north of every map.** How did the keeper get
milled cedar, plural forges, and a fitted tool wall to a place with no
road, no trees, and no harbor? Either he had a supply line — and the
isolation collapses — or he had magic, and he is keeper #1, which
detonates F2. The climax's cost structure ("nothing left to cut a
runner from but the bench itself") depends on the wood being finite,
which depends on its having an origin. The steam-bending also needs
forge fuel, in the dark, in a place with nothing that grows.

(Companion: "forty years" is Nick's declared *miscount* of the wall —
the file's own red herring — quietly promoted to fact in the
apparatus.)

**Fix:** make the stone's landing causal rather than random (a compass
goes where it is keyed), and give the station a wreck-shaped origin
that supplies the cedar, the iron, and the dead man in one stroke.

### F4. The animal's rules contradict each other, and the hunger diagnosis is refuted by three quarters of the book

Q1: "the meat he left out is untouched, and every piece he shaped is
gone." Q4: "the gaps shorten as the country empties, which is not
menace, it is hunger."

Assembled cold: a starving animal stands beside meat and takes carved
wood instead, all book — then the book says it is starving. **What is
the wood for?** Without an answer it is not an antagonist with a life,
it is a device that removes runners. The reconciling rule offered (it
won't take food near worked wood or tools) is refuted by its own
established behavior: it comes into the shop, lifts a latched door off
its pin, and steals worked wood from beside the tools.

The intelligence ledger doesn't balance either: a creature that reads
a deadfall and walks its perimeter takes a bare cache from a man
standing thirty feet away, on the day it matters. The premise
pre-empts the objection with prose rather than logic.

**Fix — an upgrade, not a patch.** Drop hunger. Establish that what it
takes is **made things**, never food, which the book already shows
three times. Then at the climax Nick's crossing rations are not food
he offers — they are **his work**, packed and wrapped by his hands.
The man whose whole destiny is giving away what he made gives away
what he made, to something that will never know it was given.

### F5. He gives away everything he needs for the crossing, then makes the crossing on nothing

Q4: "carrying his own crossing rations — everything he needs to
survive days in the air" … nine lines later: "He flies south for days
on nothing." The book cannot have both: either the rations were
necessary and the crossing is a miracle, or they weren't and the
sacrifice is theatre. Compounding it, three "starving caribou" haul a
loaded sleigh a thousand miles after a season of starvation — and the
file contradicts itself on the ground (back cover: "nothing on it to
eat"; location roster: "The Flats… where the caribou feed").

**Fix:** the crossing must cost something countable — arrival
half-dead, a caribou that doesn't make it, or a staged crossing with a
second problem solved on the page.

## Tier 2 — a sharp reviewer draws blood here

- **F6. The 25% is a quantification, not a lock.** At 24% and 26% the
  plan is identical: fix the runner, fly to Grace. He acquires a
  number; nothing is forfeited. *Fix:* the arithmetic delivers bad
  news that forces a fork — he cannot both build a sound runner and
  make the date, and he picks the fast unsound one, which makes Q3's
  failure his own doing.
- **F7. Nick is passive for two quarters** — one trap attempt in the
  whole book; Q3's three beats all happen *to* him. The antagonist
  gets a four-rung ladder; the protagonist gets none. *Fix:* two
  failed campaigns in Q2–Q3.
- **F8. Why has it never killed him?** It killed the keeper. It has
  had a season, an unlatchable door, and a sightline bed. *Fix, free
  and better:* it does not hunt men, it takes their work — and the
  keeper died because a man who cannot work up here dies.
- **F9. The hardest obstacle is solved in a subordinate clause.** Q3:
  re-sighting the stone "is days of it, and he does not have days."
  Q4 disposes of it in nine words. The elegant answer is unclaimed:
  the polar dark that costs him the boat is what gives him the days.
- **F10. The barren premise bankrupts Books 3 and 4 [R1 — CONFIRMED,
  WORSE].** Q4's hunger logic requires "everything alive on that route
  is standing on his flats in harness," yet Book 4 needs wild herds at
  the station and Book 3 needs the station *stocked* for nightly
  epidemic resupply — while Book 2's best idea is spending it down to
  its last bench. *Fix:* rule now which of the three it is.
- **F11. Grace's six movements are five refusals and a departure.**
  Silent, seated, refused, refused, writes, departs — from the woman
  who set a fire and ran uphill in the dark. The A2 audit measured
  *function*, never "what does she want here and what does she risk."
  Her real drama is spent in one parenthesis ("there is no version of
  that sentence a sane person says in a kitchen"). *Fix:* one Q3
  movement where keeping the secret costs her something irreversible.
- **F12. The A-plot has zero dialogue, and the shelf changes between
  books.** Book 1 is Klaus / *A Boy Called Christmas*; Book 2 as
  premised is *The Terror*. Good book, different shelf — and the
  premise names no comps and never raises it.

## Tier 3 — fix before rung 2

- **F13. The wound is silently restated** from courage (bible §3:
  "never fought back… Nick read this as weakness") to credit (premise:
  "was never marked for it"). The premise needs the credit version so
  the wall is the wound made into an object — a lovely move — but
  Book 4 pays the *courage* wound. Tag it and rule it as a bible
  amendment; it is the one invention in the file left untagged.
- **F14. Grace's term of years is a pincer no number survives [R1 —
  CONFIRMED, WORSE].** Large enough to credential a doctor (3–4 years)
  strands Nick, Donald, and the creature inert for years; small enough
  to keep Book 4's fuse lit won't credential anyone. Decide at this
  rung.
- **F15. Three documents-for-a-stranger, and the book points at its
  own rhyme** (the wall, the log, the letter — then says so out loud,
  then explains it again in the apparatus). *Fix:* one wall line, cut
  in stone, no gloss.
- **F16. The last four beats are four people looking** — a good move
  used until it is a tic.
- **F17. The one sentence fails the jacket standard** — 74 words, the
  subject arriving at word 47. Book 1's lands in 55 on a clean
  condition. Also: the premise's accumulative McCarthy syntax strains
  §11's locked "plain, practical, quietly wise."
- **F18. The wink finding, re-aimed [R1 — NOT CONFIRMED AS STATED].**
  The back cover's Santa-leak is *licensed* by decision 2's marketing
  note. Where the wink genuinely runs hot is the LOCKS block narrating
  the recognition in plain English — and the workshop arriving at ~10%
  whole and furnished, where every other tradition in this series
  arrives late and as a consequence.
- **F19. Process.** Open question 2 proposes a **series-wide** POV rule
  from inside a Book 2 PR — that wants its own PR. And open question 3
  is understated: this book's entire outer goal depends on a plant
  that does not exist, in a Book 1 scene not yet written, whose climax
  variant is **PR #27, still open.** That is a hard dependency on an
  unratified PR, and a blocker on ratifying this one.

## Recommended order of repair

1. **F1** — un-collapse the clocks (a reordering; fixes the most).
2. **F2 + F3 as one author ruling** — what the station *is*.
3. **F4** — the made-things inversion.

Those three passes and the premise is strong.
