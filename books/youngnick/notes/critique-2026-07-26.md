# Young Nicholas — Outside Critique (v2, manuscript read)

*A junior critic's read, 26 July 2026. For the author's private use.*

**This supersedes the bible-only critique previously at this path.** That read
was written when this directory held a README and `STORY_BIBLE.md` and nothing
else; it reviewed a plan and said so. `manuscript/nick-manuscript-22.md` has
since been imported — 23,376 words, twenty-four chapter headings — and the prose
overturns a fair amount of it. `STORY_BIBLE.md` remains authoritative; where the
draft contradicts it, that is recorded as a finding, not fixed.

*The draft's "Heather" is read as Grace throughout and is not treated as an
error. The `(( ))` blocks are read as the author's own TODO markers.*

---

## PART 1 — CRITIQUE

**The opening hooks, and starts late.** Chapter 1 closes on "It was the Grin
brothers." — four words that reframe everything before them. But it is the
*second* cliff scare in eleven pages; Nick already slips at line 56 and saves
himself on a bush root, spending the reader's alarm before the ambush needs it.
The book starts at "Suddenly, Nick heard footsteps approaching."

**The voice is real and unstable.** At its best it is wry and plain: "It finally
made sense, but it made no sense at all." "The river below him was still daring
him to fall." The comedy even lands — "he was peeing." That voice is worth
protecting. But Chapters 1–2 cannot hold a tense: "Focusing on the task at hand,
Nick begins to takes the sample when it started to snow… He sees an eagle off in
the distant." The draft is also unproofed ("hypical", "the frictino of the
snow", "they just did4").

**The defining fact is that almost nobody speaks.** In 23,376 words there are
about 86 lines of dialogue. Nick speaks aloud three times in the whole
manuscript, once in a flashback. **Nick and Heather never exchange a word on the
page.** This is narrated summary with its scenes reported rather than played —
and that, far more than description, is what the missing words are.

**Nick acts; luck finishes for him.** He chooses to investigate the mine, to
steal back the skim, to rope caribou. But the sleigh first flies because he
grabbed a lever to pull himself upright; the crates come off it because "Nick's
temper got the best of him and he stomped his foot on floor of the sleigh. At
that moment there was a small aftershock"; the gold is unguarded because Heather
sets a fire; Donald dies in an aftershock. The house rule is *no solutions by
luck* — the draft breaks it at its climaxes.

**Heather half-refutes the earlier read.** It doubted her agency; the page gives
her a crowbar, a guard "wacked him in the head", an arson, an elbow to Donald's
stomach. But her capture, her confrontation, and her decision to burn the cabin
are **unwritten** — Chapter 14 ends "She was heading towards the mine" and she
next appears as spectacle seen from fifty yards, by a Nick who wonders "Could
she be part of the heist?" She is then unconscious for the climax and wakes to
have the plot explained to her. The earlier read's sharpest guess is confirmed:
her "amazing memory" never fires once — not even when a bank letter puts the
swindle in her hands.

**The villains are thinner than the bible's.** Donald gets the shrewd
"metal...and this towns endless hope for more it" — then almost nothing. He
never needles Nick about weakness, because Nick's wound is not in this book: the
father never appears, never dies on the page, and **the belt does not exist in
the manuscript at all.** Donald never courts Heather either; the triangle
survives as one line of James's gossip.

**The Santa material lands, elsewhere than promised.** Not the explanation
table — the chocolate morning, where Nick trades away eagle eggs he is starving
for and the town simply enjoys it: "Nice day for a treat, Sweet Pea". A gift, at
cost, anonymous. The caribou are second, and they refute the earlier read's
charge that the reindeer row was a stage direction: "Caribou increase the
pulling power of a sleigh on the snow. Why wouldn't caribou increase the pulling
power of a sleigh through the sky?" That is an argument, and it works. What
fails is the keystone: the town waking to its gold happens offstage and arrives
as her father's summary, "Thats yours sweety". The book's biggest image is the
one scene it declines to dramatize.

**Thinness, and damage.** The 29 `(( ))` notes cluster overwhelmingly on
*description* — sleigh, dock, cabin, vault, road, knots — with exactly two on
interiority. That map matters: the author diagnosed furniture where the hole is
scene and speech. The import is broken too; at least five chapter breaks were
swallowed ("A red glowing sleigh. C"), three inside Chapter 9, which is why it
runs 2,611 words across four unrelated scenes. This is a ~28-chapter book.
Chapter 20 ends with ~350 words of raw outline pasted into the prose.

**The ending, and its absence.** Chapter 22's "Nick who?" is the best thing
here — quiet, cruel, the strongest possible argument for the wink. The compass
stone and North Star warp are cleanly done. Then the text stops mid-word,
"entered the cloud. Cha", and **Chapter 24 is an empty heading.**

**Where this sits.** Against *A Boy Called Christmas* (~40k) and *Holes* (~47k),
this is at 45–50% of category weight. But the audience finding is the serious
one, and it is not the earlier read's. That critique thought audience was an
unresolved *document* problem. The prose shows it is a *sentence* problem: short
declarative MG syntax carrying vesting schedules, 75% retention, a
forged-invoice printing press, four payments of $24,000, tuition, and a mother
dead of typhoid. There are no children in this book and no child's concerns in
it. The sentences address a ten-year-old; the story addresses an adult.

**Verdict.** Further from submittable than the word count suggests, closer than
the state of the file suggests. A real first draft of three-fifths of a novel:
no final chapter, a five-chapter hole where the heroine's second act belongs,
its keystone summarized, its protagonist's wound unwritten. But the spine holds,
the turn from ten-percent skimming to the whole theft is properly built, the
caribou logic is inspired, and "Nick who?" is the work of someone who knows what
this story is. Two solid drafts from a submission package — not four.

## PART 2 — RECOMMENDATIONS

Proposals, not rulings. Nothing is canon until you write it into the bible.

**1. Write Heather's missing second act.** *Where:* between Chapter 14's last
line and her crowbar entrance in Chapter 19 — she reaches the mine, confronts
Donald with the bank letter, is taken, burns her way out. *Why:* it converts her
from a character with agency into one who causes the plot, and it houses the
bible's best unwritten beat, Donald telling her why Nick came back. It also
deletes the draft's worst moment, where her heroism arrives as spectacle to a
hero who wonders if she's in on it. *Cost:* ~5,000–6,000 words — the biggest
item here and the one worth most.

**2. Put people in rooms talking.** *Where:* everywhere; start with Nick and
Heather, who share no dialogue at all. *Why:* 86 lines in 23,376 words is why
this reads as a treatment. Playing out scenes already summarized — Nick and
James at the creek, Heather and Jim over the bank letter, the miners cornering
Donald — adds 6,000–8,000 words that are *story*, not padding, and fixes density
without inventing one new plot event. *Cost:* spread across the draft; the best
return per hour available.

**3. Dramatize the town waking to its gold.** *Where:* before Chapter 22, in the
town's POV — Mrs. Hannigan, Bramley and his boat, Joe, Old Rob. *Why:* the bible
calls this the first Christmas morning and the draft reports it secondhand. You
have written this scene twice already and well — the chocolate morning, the
nugget in the egg sack. Write the third properly and the book has its closing
image. *Cost:* ~1,500–2,000 words, no restructure.

**4. Write Chapter 24 — and note that the draft already chose the ending.**
*Where:* the empty heading. *Why:* the earlier critique asked what the warp
costs Grace and filed it open. **The prose has answered it and you may not have
noticed:** Nick never speaks to her, vanishes unexplained, and her last line is
"Maybe it was a dream, but I remember seeing him." Bittersweet, thematically
correct for giving-without-credit, and better than §6's version where they
confess and she returns his belt. Update the bible to match the prose. *Cost:*
1,000–2,000 words, one decision.

**5. Restore the father and earn one climax.** *Where:* seeded through Books
1–2, paid at the cliff. *Why:* the bible's strongest character material — angry
at his father the day he died, afraid he inherited that weakness — is absent
from the manuscript, while the climax is resolved by geology. Same problem:
nothing in Nick's history is asked to pay off. Give Donald his needle, and let
the father's restraint be what Nick finally understands. *Cost:* ~2,000–3,000
words distributed, plus a hard look at whether Donald should die by aftershock.

**6. Repair the import before revising anything.** *Where:* the five swallowed
chapter breaks (Chapter 9 is four scenes) and the raw outline pasted into
Chapter 20. *Why:* you cannot judge pacing against a chapter that is really
four. Renumber to the true count of ~28. *Cost:* an afternoon, mechanical.

**7. Reconcile the names.** Findings: the surname runs "Nick Johnson" three
times against "Nick Anderson" once, both inside Chapter 6 — the bible proposes
Anderson, so the bible wins. **James Grin is called "Jim" twice** in a book
where Heather's father is Jim. A "Gus Anderson" appears at the door while the
mine dog is Old Gus [TK: is the father's given name Gus, or is this drift?].
The Grins' father is "Gerred" on the page, unestablished in the bible [TK]. Ages
do not hold: Nick is twenty-one and left at eleven, but Chapter 5 says he grew
up in Alaska "until he was ten", Heather says "twelve", and California is three
years in Chapter 2 and ten in Chapter 8. *Cost:* an hour.

**What the draft has already settled.** §7.2 the wink is proved — "Santa" and
"Christmas" appear zero times and the story still reads as what it is; lock it,
drop "leaning". §7.4's "some" versus "all" gold reveal is fully executed and is
the best structural move in the book. §7.6 object-based magic is executed. But
§5's belt lineage has been quietly abandoned: no belt exists here, and the
compass stone is *found in the cave wall*, not inherited — which resolves the
heir-versus-self-made contradiction the earlier read flagged. **The prose voted
self-made**, and that is the stronger answer. Reinstate the belt or rewrite §5.
Still open: the three-day clock (§7.5) is absent, and James Grin's fate is never
resolved [TK].

**On the earlier "Write the novel first; retire the ladder" recommendation.**
The draft supports it and corrects it. Supports: none of what works here
survives 32 pages. "Nick who?" needs twenty chapters of a hero presumed dead;
the chocolate morning needs four chapters of established hunger; the caribou
argument needs Nick's logging years. Corrects: that critique called the novel
"~70% done", trusting the bible's asset table, which lists Books 1 and 3 as
final. Nothing in this file is final. It is a first draft at ~45% of category
weight with no ending. The sequencing stands — prose is Rung 1 — but the
schedule behind it was wrong by about a year.
