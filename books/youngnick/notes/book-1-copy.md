## One sentence

Pushed off a mountain and left for dead by the two brothers who own
his hometown, a young man survives the fall, finds a sleigh that
flies in the cave that shelters him, and realizes he can give a
starving town back every dollar those brothers stole from it — on
the one condition that nobody ever learns he is alive.

## Back cover

At twenty-one, Nick comes home to Bronston, Alaska with a ring he
hasn't found the nerve to mention and a job he's overqualified for:
reading ground samples on the mountain for the two brothers who own
the mine, the town, and every dollar the town is owed. He grew up
here. He left at eleven, angry at a father who never once stood up
to the Grins and then died in a quake before Nick could take it
back. He has come home for Grace, and for a chance to be the kind of
man he suspects his father wasn't. Three weeks later, the Grin
brothers throw him off a cliff. They have their reasons: Nick's
samples say the mine is dying, and the savings the whole town is
starving toward have already been rowed out to a boat in a hidden
cove. Nobody climbs down to check on a dead man. So the town buries
him, Grace books passage for San Francisco half sure her whole life
just ended, and the brothers go back to counting. But Nick doesn't
die. He wakes on a ledge with a ruined shoulder, and in the cave
that keeps him alive that night he finds a stone that glows and a
sleigh under a hundred years of dust, carved with markings he last
saw traced on his father's belt when he was small enough to sit on
the man's knee. By the time he can make the thing fly, he
understands exactly what he can do with it — and exactly what it
costs. A dead man can go anywhere. A dead man can put the gold back,
house by house, in the dark. A dead man cannot walk up to the girl
he came home for and say one word.

## Q1 — The Setup

A young man comes back to the town that raised him after ten years
in the California timber, and finds it thinner than he left it: the
mine paying less, the store shelves lighter, everyone living on the
promise of a payout that never quite arrives. He takes work on the
mountain because it puts him near Grace, and because his father's
old employers still know his name. On the bench above the river, two
brothers ask him what the samples say. He tells them the truth,
which is the last honest thing he does that day. They kick him off
the cliff together and stand at the top a while, and neither of them
climbs down, because there is no way down to climb. A hundred feet
below, in water and broken shale, the dead man crawls out. Night is
coming and the cold is the real enemy, so he does what his father
drilled into him on a trapline twenty years ago — shelter, fire,
food — and hauls himself up over a shelf of rock into a cave he
never knew was there. He builds a dry wall against the wind. He digs
at a red glow coming up through the floor. What he uncovers is not
gold.

## Q2 — The New Situation

Learning to fly a thing nobody has flown in a century is mostly
falling, and he does it in the dark, alone, on a mountain where
being seen would kill him twice. Down below, the town holds a
service for him. Grace, who thought she had years to say what she
never said, starts counting the days to the boat that will take her
south to medical school and away from all of it. Nick watches his
own funeral from the trees. He scavenges what he needs, keeps to the
ridgelines, and slips down one night to the creek rock where two
children once carved their names, and cuts a heart around them,
because he cannot leave a message any other way. Then, at a lit
window at the wrong hour, he sees the brothers' ledgers open on the
table and does the arithmetic — and the reason he was thrown off a
cliff stops being a mystery and becomes a number. It is not
skimming. It is everything, and it is nearly loaded.

## Q3 — Progress, and the Point of No Return

He starts giving it back. A little at a time, at night, doorstep by
doorstep, and Bronston wakes up to gold it can't explain and decides
— because towns do — that it has been visited by something. The
trouble is that a ghost cannot warn anyone. When he tries to reach
Grace at the dock, every word goes wrong; she sees only the man she
is waiting for arriving behind her, and he sees only that she is
waiting. He turns and goes. Following the brothers to the cove, he
finds the boat, the crates, and the vault of everything this town
was ever owed — and understands he was never in the way of a secret,
he *was* the secret. Then the ground moves. The mountain takes back
the workings, he escapes with nothing, and the woman he came home to
marry is dragged into a cabin by the man who wants her — where she
learns, from her captor's own mouth, that Nick came back to propose.
She sets a fire, takes his father's belt off the wall, and runs
uphill into the dark.

## Q4 — Climax and Aftermath

He is flying home with nothing when he sees them: animals moving
across open snow, unafraid, in a line, and an idea arrives that will
outlive everyone in this story. Then he sees her, small against the
cliff face, with a man climbing after her. What happens next happens
in seconds — the catch off the rock, the two of them tangled in the
harness over open air, the knot his father taught him holding what
his hands alone cannot. When the man who murdered him loses his
grip, Nick throws him the line anyway. That is the moment his father
is finally answered. Afterward there is a girl who knows everything
and a town that will never know anything, and those are two
different kinds of quiet. He gives her back the belt. He takes the
stone up to the cave, sets it into the socket cut for it, and goes
wherever it takes him. Down in Bronston, when the story is retold —
and it will be retold, at that table, every winter, for as long as
anyone is alive to tell it — someone always asks what happened to
the young man who came home. And someone always answers: *who?*
