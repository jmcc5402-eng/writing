## 1. Nick's interiority and the father wound

### What is on the page

Nick has plenty of *procedural* interiority — survival logistics, puzzle-work,
plan-making — and it is often good:

> "What drives a man to kill? Money...power...love...could that be it?"
> (Ch15)

> "Nick knew he had lost his mind, but he also felt it was worth a try."
> (Ch18 — the caribou decision)

His motive is stated plainly and warmly:

> "He returned because he loved this place. He loved the people, the creeks,
> the eagles, and even the mine." (Ch9)

And the bible's core character note — Nick balks — is staged exactly once, and
it is the single best interior beat he gets:

> "Maybe he chickened out. He rationalized that he didn't want to make the
> sleigh public knowledge." (Ch23)

That beat arrives two chapters from the end. Before it, Nick's emotional
interiority is limited to warm Grace-nostalgia (Ch2, Ch15) and one dream
(Ch23). He never doubts himself as a man, never measures himself against his
father, never fears he is weak.

### The father wound is not in this book

Verified against the full text:

- **The word "belt" never refers to the father's belt.** The only belt in the
  manuscript is the key on James's "belt loop" (Ch17). The bible's §3/§5 belt
  does not exist on the page — consistent with §7 decision 7 (the belt "unlocks
  nothing"), but decision 7's *recognition* beat — the compass stone's
  "markings echo the emblems Nick remembers from his father's belt" — is also
  absent: the stone's inscription is read only as "a compass" (Ch23). [TK: the
  locked recognition beat needs writing into the stone-discovery scene.]
- The father appears only as benign memory: the freezing-water warning (Ch2),
  "Nick's father was the real brains of the operation" (Ch8), the egg-craft
  and trader trips (Ch9). No anger on the day he died, no perceived weakness,
  no earthquake death on the page, and the survival creed ("Shelter, fire,
  food...") appears nowhere in the manuscript.
- Donald never needles Nick about weakness — the two exchange no words at all
  until Nick's single line at the climax ("it's over Don, I have the gold,
  you're going to jail," Ch21).
- [CHECK: Ch12's "Gus Anderson stood at the front door" — is this Nick's
  father's given name, or drift from Old Gus the dog? Unestablished in the
  bible.]
- [CHECK: Ch1 has "a large wooden log" swung at Nick's head; Ch16 remembers
  "the metal rod." For continuity lane, noted in passing.]

**Verdict:** the critique's claim that "Nick's wound is not in this book" is
CONFIRMED. The bible's arc (§2, §3: reading the father's restraint as weakness,
fearing he inherited it, Donald needling it) is the designed engine of the
climax, and none of its parts exist in the draft. The climax is currently
resolved by an aftershock, not by anything Nick understands about his father —
which is also why it breaks the every-setup-pays-off rule.

**One correction to the critique:** it says Nick "speaks aloud three times in
the whole manuscript, once in a flashback." Counted on the page, Nick has
roughly nine or ten spoken utterances (Ch7, Ch9, Ch11, Ch15 flashback x2,
Ch18 "Ho…!", Ch19 x3, Ch21). But almost all are whispers to himself or to
animals; he addresses exactly one on-page human outside the flashback (Donald,
one line, Ch21). The substantive finding survives the corrected count.

## 2. Grace in the opening, and the played Nick-Grace scene

### Her agency in ch1–4 as written

Grace's opening (Ch3–4) gives her a decision, a motive, and the book's best
early interiority — but no scene where she acts *on* another principal:

- The college decision is announced by her ("I decided, I'm going to enroll in
  the University," Ch3) — but the draft frames it as the fruit of Jim's
  campaign ("Jim had been trying to persuade Heather to apply to the
  university all winter long"). Arc-consistent with the bible (she starts
  ruled by others' expectations), but it means her first beat is assent.
- Ch4 makes her a receiver of news. The flour-drop is a fine physical beat,
  and the buried feeling is real: "Though she would never admit it, she had
  always assumed that one day Nick would be the man she married."
- Her best interiority comes just after the opening, at the rock (Ch9): "What
  type of terrible person thinks of herself first when hearing that another
  has died." That self-audit is sharper than anything Nick gets.
- Her bible-stated gift — "she had an amazing memory" (Ch3) — is told and
  never once used, including in Ch14 when the bank letter puts the swindle in
  her hands. CONFIRMED per critique.
- **Correction to the critique's emphasis:** Ch14's ending is genuine agency —
  she conceals the letter, decides alone, and "She was heading towards the
  mine." The failure is not that she lacks agency in the opening half; it is
  that every consequence of her agency (confrontation, capture, arson, escape)
  happens off the page.

### The prior finding, verified

**Nick and Grace never exchange a word anywhere in the draft.** CONFIRMED.
Grace never addresses Nick; Nick never addresses Grace; her last line is about
him, to her father: "Maybe it was a dream, but I remember seeing him." (Ch22).
The bible's one scripted Nick-Grace scene — the dock misunderstanding (§3, §6
Book 3) — was never drafted at all.

### Does canon allow a played Nick-Grace scene before the push?

Yes — nothing locked forbids it, and the bible arguably wants one:

- §7 locks touch names, the wink, and the stone — not the shape of ch1–4.
- §3 says Nick's arc starts "from balking (he can't even tell Grace he loves
  her)." A balk is a scene-event: it can only be *shown* if Nick gets near her
  and fails. Right now the balk is one narrated line — "He had seen her across
  the town as she left the market, but didn't have the nerve to call out to
  her" (Ch2) — reported after he is already off the cliff.
- §6 Book 1 has Grace "writes to Nick" before she learns he "died." [TK: does
  "writes to Nick" mean a letter to California, or does bible-Grace know Nick
  is back? The manuscript says flatly "She didn't even know he was back"
  (Ch4). If the bible means she knows, the draft already contradicts canon
  here and a played scene *repairs* the contradiction.]
- The binding constraint is the misunderstanding engine (§3): "Neither learns
  the truth (that Nick returned to *propose*) until the climax." A pre-push
  scene must therefore end in failure — no confession, no explanation of why
  he came back.

### What ch1–4 would gain (recommendation: add one scene)

One played scene, ~600–1,200 words, set in the three-week window the draft
already provides (Nick returned "over three weeks earlier," saw her "a few
days earlier"), placed as Ch2.5/Ch3-adjacent — Nick crosses her path (market,
creek, the rock) and fails to say the one thing he came back to say:

1. **The push acquires a witnessed cost.** "Nick Johnson was killed" (Ch4)
   would land on a live relationship the reader has seen, not on a ten-year-old
   memory summarized in Ch2.
2. **It stages the bible's stated arc origin** (the balk) instead of narrating
   it, so the Ch23 "Maybe he chickened out" beat becomes the second rhyme of a
   pattern rather than an isolated note.
3. **It sharpens Grace's Ch4 grief** from "Why hadn't he contacted her?" to
   the crueler *he found me and said nothing* — and gives her one opening
   scene where she acts on a principal, which she currently never does.
4. **It plants the proposal reveal** so Donald's Book-3 disclosure (bible §6)
   pays off something witnessed.
5. **It gives the draft its first Nick-Grace dialogue** — addressing the
   86-lines-of-dialogue density problem exactly where it hurts most.

**Cost / required flags:** two manuscript lines must be revised, not silently
— Ch2's "didn't have the nerve to call out to her" (becomes: called out, and
then couldn't say it) and Ch4's "She didn't even know he was back." Both are
draft lines, not bible canon; the bible's dock scene is untouched and its
misunderstanding is *strengthened* (each now has a failed meeting to misread).
Structural change — proposal only, per house rule 4; needs author approval.

## 3. The Grins in the opening: menacing or cartoonish?

**Split verdict: menacing in action, cartoonish in speech — and the menace is
never personal.**

- **Ch1 is genuinely menacing**, and for a reason worth protecting: the ambush
  is anonymous and nearly silent — "a dirty glove swinging a large wooden log
  at his head," two kicks, the reveal withheld to the last four words ("It was
  the Grin brothers."). Faceless violence from trusted people is the scariest
  thing in the book.
- **Ch6 splits the brothers.** James is written cartoonish by design — "a few
  eggs short of a dozen," "Look at this Don, dancers and dinner on every
  street corner" — and the bible endorses this (§3: dimmer, distractible,
  talks too much). Donald, though, gets two authentically cold lines:
  "Nick Johnson falling off that cliff was the worst moment of my life. At
  least thats what I keep repeating around town." and "The best thing we got
  going for us is the metal...and this towns endless hope for more it." That
  is a real villain voice — a man who has weaponized a town's hope.
- **After Ch6 the menace leaks away.** The Duff-peeing gag (Ch17) plays the
  operation for comedy; Donald's knife-to-throat (Ch21) is stock hostage
  blocking; and Donald dies by aftershock, un-faced. Crucially, the bible's
  two instruments of *personal* menace — Donald needling Nick's fear of
  weakness, and Donald courting/coercing Grace — are both absent (the triangle
  survives only as James's flashback gossip: "everybody knows that you're
  gonna marry her one day," Ch15). The Grins threaten Nick's body once and his
  town abstractly; they never threaten what either lead fears.

**Direction:** keep James comic (canon), rebuild Donald. The Ch6 register —
cold lines inside shabby rooms — is the correct one; it needs the needle
scenes and the Grace coercion to make the threat psychological before the
climax asks Donald to hold a knife.

## 4. THE AUDIENCE RULING (delegated to this run)

### The problem, verified

The critique's finding is CONFIRMED on the page: MG-simple declarative
sentences ("Falling would not be good." "That is not a good sign.") carry
adult material — "75% of their earnings would be retained by the bank
collecting interest" (Ch4), "four payments of $24,000" (Ch13), a forged-invoice
printing press (Ch20), a mother dead of typhoid, an attempted murder, a
coerced bride, a villain's death. There is not one child character in the
manuscript, and no child's concern anywhere in it. Meanwhile the bible
contradicts itself: §11 declares "Ages ~8–12 for the novel," while §3 and §6
fix the leads at ~19–21, returning *to propose marriage*, with Grace's arc
running through coerced courtship to claiming a medical career. Both cannot
hold. The ruling resolves the contradiction.

### The two lanes weighed

**Lane A — MG family-myth (the *A Boy Called Christmas* lane, 8–12).**
Comps prove darkness is not the obstacle: Haig's book carries a mother dead in
a well, hunger, cruelty — but through an eleven-year-old's eyes. MG's true
gate is protagonist age and concern: MG heroes run 10–13 and readers read up,
not twenty-one and proposing. To enter this lane, Young Nicholas must age Nick
down ~8 years, delete the proposal, dissolve the misunderstanding engine
(the bible's spine), rebuild Grace's arc (a 12-year-old cannot be coerced
toward marriage or leave for medical school), and invent a child cast. That is
a different book wearing this one's title.

**Lane B — older all-ages frontier myth.** The precedent for adult-led
Santa-origin is strong and named in the bible's own comps: *Klaus* (adult
protagonist, all-ages family audience — the closest cousin per §10), Baum's
*Life & Adventures of Santa Claus* (all-ages fable spanning adulthood), and
Guinn's *Autobiography of Santa Claus* (perennial adult holiday-gift shelf).
This lane keeps the manuscript's story and asks only that the sentences grow
up to meet it.

### Ruling: Lane B, for five evidence-based reasons

1. **Canon fixes the leads as adults.** §3's ages (~19–21), the proposal, the
   coercion of Grace, and the misunderstanding engine are the bible's spine.
   Lane A requires rewriting canon; Lane B requires only a prose register
   pass. The lane that preserves the source of truth wins under house rules.
2. **The page's material is adult and load-bearing.** The swindle *is* the
   plot: vesting schedules, controlled books, forged statements. In MG this
   must be simplified into a MacGuffin; in the all-ages myth lane it is the
   "grounded villain plot" §10 claims as differentiation.
3. **The draft's two best moments are elegiac, not MG payoffs.** "Nick who?"
   (Ch22) and the chocolate morning work as giving-without-credit melancholy —
   the lover vanishing unthanked. An 8–12 reader reads for the hero being
   seen; this book's whole point is that he isn't. The wink reveal (§7.2,
   "Santa"/"Christmas" appear zero times in the draft — verified) is likewise
   an all-ages device: it trusts the reader to assemble the myth.
4. **Category position is better.** The 8–12 Santa-origin shelf is benchmarked
   by *A Boy Called Christmas*; against it, Young Nicholas reads as a
   miscategorized romance. In the all-ages/holiday-gift lane the neighbors are
   Klaus, Baum, and Guinn — thinner, older competition, seasonal-evergreen
   economics, and every §10 differentiator (heist, romance, gold-rush Alaska)
   is an asset instead of a liability.
5. **The revision already required points this direction.** The father wound,
   Grace's second act, played dialogue, deeper interiority (Parts 1–3 above)
   all mature the book. Fixing the sentence problem in Lane B rides the same
   revision; fixing it in Lane A fights it.

### What Lane B costs (named honestly)

- **The bible must be amended:** §11's "Ages ~8–12" is overruled; §8's ladder
  re-sequences — the picture book stops being a proof-of-the-same-text and
  becomes a downstream adaptation in its own register (Klaus shows the myth
  adapts down; the text itself no longer tries to serve both).
- **Loses the clean shelf:** "all-ages" is a positioning risk; the query must
  pick a home (adult/holiday gift fiction a la Guinn, or crossover family
  read-aloud) and defend it. The school/library MG channel is forfeited.
- **Raises the craft bar:** the entire draft needs a register pass — the
  current prose is below even MG polish (unproofed, tense-unstable), and
  all-ages myth prose (Baum, Haig at his best) is deceptively demanding.
- **The kid-reader-panel agent becomes peripheral** for the novel (still
  useful for the picture-book adaptation).
- **Word-count expectations rise modestly** (all-ages holiday novels run
  ~45–70k; the projected ~40–50k finished draft sits at the low edge).

**RECOMMENDATION: Publish *Young Nicholas* in the older all-ages frontier-myth
lane — keeping the adult leads, the proposal plot, and the annuity swindle,
and raising the prose register to match them — and do not rebuild it as an
8–12 middle-grade novel.**
