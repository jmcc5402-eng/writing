# Chapter 4 — The News in Town

Grace came home from Mrs. Jones's earlier than usual.
The evening was cold and clear,
the kind of evening that made the whole street hold still,
and her father was on the front porch with his coat on,
taking the last of the light like it was owed him.

"You're early," Jim said. "She run out of mending?"

"I ran out of patience." Grace came up the steps
and stood in front of his chair,
and found that her hands had gone and folded themselves
the way they did when she had rehearsed a thing too long.
"I decided," she said. "I'm going to enroll in the university."

Jim closed his eyes and leaned back and smiled,
and for a moment he didn't say anything at all,
which from her father was a kind of applause.

"This is the proudest moment of my life," he said.

"You've said that before."

"It keeps coming true." He stood and hugged her,
wool coat and pipe smoke and the whole of her childhood,
and held on one beat longer than usual.
"Come on. Let's celebrate."

"Celebrate with what?"

"There's tea," he said. "There's nearly tea."

She laughed, and then made herself stop laughing,
because the next part was the part she had rehearsed.

"I want you to hear why," she said.
"Not because you've been at me about it all winter.
You could have kept at me another ten years.
I decided because of Mother."

Jim's hand paused on the door latch.

"They gave her nine kinds of nothing," Grace said.
"Boiled water and prayers and a man from Juno
who charged four dollars to look at her tongue.
Typhoid isn't a mystery, it's in the books —
somebody is going to run it to ground,
and I mean to be in the room when they do.
I'm not going south to get out of Bronston.
I'm going south to become a doctor."

Her father looked at her a long time.

"Your mother used to say you had a memory like a bear trap,"
he said. "Nothing that walks into it walks back out.
I'd quiz you on the bones of the hand
when you were six years old, and you'd rattle them off
and then correct my order."

"Your order was wrong."

"My order was wrong," he agreed. "Eighteen months of medicine
in San Francisco and the bones still beat me."
He said it lightly, the way he always said it,
eighteen months of school traded for a shovel and a boat north,
a whole life folded into a joke by long practice.

"Come with me," Grace said. "There. That's the rest of it.
Come with me and finish what you started."

"Gracie."

"You hate the mine."

"I don't hate the mine. I know the mine.
There's a difference, and at my age
the difference is nearly a comfort." He shook his head slowly.
"I don't want a ship, and I don't want a new house,
and I don't want to learn a new town's streets.
I want to know my girl is where she belongs.
That I want badly."

"The mine kills men, Papa. I've been to the funerals.
So have you. You've carried the boxes."

"And it has fed us for twenty-five years," he said,
gently, closing it the way he closed things,
not with a door but with a curtain.
"Write your mother's people when you're settled.
She'd want them knowing a doctor was coming up
out of her side of the family."

"I'm not a doctor yet, Papa. I'm a letter and a boat."

"You're the doctor end of a letter and a boat,"
he said. "I've watched you split kindling.
You don't stop in the middle."

"But the money," she said. "Won't you need—"

"I haven't needed it for the last four years.
I'll be fine if I never see it."
He tapped the porch rail once, done with the subject.
"Tomorrow morning, write your letter to the cruise line
and reserve your fare."

The line company could take its payment
straight from the bank once the letter came;
confirmation took a few weeks coming back up the water.
By mid-December she would hold tickets to Portland
in her own two hands.

She went to bed that night with the route unrolled
on the ceiling above her, and did not sleep for an hour,
and did not mind.

Saturday she walked to Old Rob's with the letter
in her coat pocket and flour money in her glove.

The market used to be crowded on a Saturday.
Lately it was quiet everywhere,
the miners idle at the end of the week
and every one of them worried out loud or silently
that the mine was going dry underneath the town.
Rob's shelves said what nobody would:
gaps where the tinned goods used to stand,
one sack of coffee where there had been a row,
prices chalked over twice, both times upward.
Since the avalanche closed the pass,
everything the town ate or wore came in by boat,
and the boat charged what the boat charged.
Hope was the one thing on the shelves
that hadn't gone up in price,
and the town bought it by the pound.

Her letter sat against her ribs, patient as a stone.
Every few steps she was seventeen feet tall with it,
and every few steps after that
she was a girl about to get on a ship alone
to a city of strangers, and her breath went thin.
Excited and terrified had stopped taking turns
and settled in to live together. She let them.

Mrs. Carver was ahead of her at the dry goods,
turning a tin of syrup over in her hand
the way you turn a thing you are not going to buy,
reading the chalked price like it might have been
a mistake the first two times.
She set it back on the shelf, squared it neatly,
and bought salt.
Nobody in Bronston walked out of Rob's empty-handed;
they had all just gotten quietly expert
at deciding which hand.
[TK: "Mrs. Carver" is a placeholder townswoman —
no named background townsfolk exist in canon]

Flour, then. Her father's birthday was Thursday next,
and a cake was foolishness they couldn't afford,
and she intended to be foolish.
Her mother's cakes had come out of this same store —
flour, and chocolate got off the Inuit traders,
chocolate that had no business existing
within a thousand miles of this coast.
She used to wonder at that, and her father would say:
"Anyone can get anything, once gold is discovered.
Men will travel thousands of miles
to take part in the fever."
The traders had been at it for a thousand years,
carrying the whole world port to port,
and they would be at it long after
the last miner went home with empty pockets.

By the stove at the back, Old Rob was holding court
with a handful of men off the Saturday shift
that no longer existed —
Saturday men with nowhere to put their Saturdays —
and for once the talk coming off that stove was warm.

"—clearest sample yet, is what they said."

"Two, three years of metal still in the ground. Maybe more."

"That carries us well past the summer and the payout."

"That carries us just fine until the new machinery's here."

"Money?" one of the men belted out, and laughed.
"According to the news, we're gonna have more money
than we ever dreamed of. The Grins just got back,
and the sample came up positive.
Last time a sample read that clear,
we pulled better than eighteen pounds
out of that mountain in the next six months."

"The whole town's gone ape," Rob said.
"They can't hardly wait to get up there
and start the machinery."

Grace kept her face on the flour and smiled at the sack.
Everything her father had earned for four years —
three parts of every four —
sat in the annuity at the Juno Bank,
collecting interest against the payout due next summer.
Two or three more years of gold in the ground
meant the payout was safe, and school besides,
and maybe — the thought arrived all by itself —
maybe a year from now, with money in his hand,
her father could be talked onto a boat after all.

She carried the flour toward the counter,
and the men's voices dropped.

Voices dropping was its own kind of news.
She slowed by the dry goods and did not hurry.

"—a tragedy is what it is," one of them said, low.
"And the boy hadn't hardly been back a month.
I never saw him but the once
before they went off on the expedition."

"Strong young fella, too. His father all over again."

"Lost his footing on the mountain, the way they tell it.
Sometimes fewer men come down than go up. It's the country."

She came to the counter and the talk stopped,
and the men smiled at her,
every one of whom had known her since she was a girl
riding her father's shoulders down this same aisle.
The smiles were wrong. They fit badly, like borrowed coats.

"What was the tragedy?" she asked.
"I haven't heard anything."

The men looked at each other, and then at the floor,
which had nothing in it either.

"I don't mean to be the bearer of bad news," Old Rob began,
and stopped, and pushed at the counter dust with one thumb.
"And you might not even remember him.
But Nick [TK surname] was killed during the expedition."

The store went on standing around her.
Somewhere behind her the stove ticked.

Nick.

He had been her best friend for years —
years when a day had twelve usable hours in it
and every one of them was theirs —
until the mountain came down and took his father,
and the boat took Nick south, both in the same season,
when they were children.
And a flood came through her now, all of it at once:
the flat rock at the creek with the seat worn smooth,
the counting games he always lost on purpose
and swore up and down he hadn't,
the winter he pulled her out of the snow well by the hood
and never once told anyone she'd fallen in.
She had a memory like a bear trap; everyone said so.
It had simply never occurred to her until this exact minute
how much of what was in the trap was Nick.

He had been back. Back — in Bronston,
walking streets she walked every day of her life.
How long? A month, the man said. A month.
And she was hearing it from Rob's counter, after,
the way you hear about weather in another town.
Why had he not come to find her?

She would have — she would have wanted —

The sack of flour slipped out of her hand.
It burst at her feet,
and a white cloud rolled up to her knees
and hung there, settling on her boots,
finer than any snow.
Rob was saying her name.
She turned and walked out the door with it half-said,
out onto the boardwalk, into the cold,
and the empty feeling went the whole way through her,
floor to crown, wind through an open house.
Though she would never have admitted it to a living soul,
she had always assumed that one day
Nick would be the man she married.

She stood on the boards until her breath came back.
The street ran gray to the gray water.
Somewhere out there was the channel, and the sea,
and every mile of the distance he had crossed
to come back and die on a mountain
three days' walk from her front door,
without her ever once knowing he was near.

The letter was still in her pocket.
She had meant to hand it across the counter with the flour money
and had carried it right past the till.

She stood a moment longer.
Then she turned around and went back inside,
past the settling flour, straight to the counter,
and laid the envelope down in front of Old Rob.

"Grace," Rob said. "Honey, sit down a minute. I'll—"

"This goes out on the next boat," she said.

Her voice held, which was information;
some part of her was already taking notes for the woman
she was going to have to become.
Rob looked at her, and took the letter,
and wrote her name in the mail book without another word.

The boat came Thursday.
She would be at the dock to watch her letter go.
