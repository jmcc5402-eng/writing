# Chapter 5 — The Counting House

The office was cold with the fire going.
It had always been cold with the fire going.
The stove drew well and warmed its corner,
and the rest of the room made do.
Outside the wind went along the eaves
and found nothing loose to take.
Their father had built it that way.
He was not a man who paid to heat air
he was not sitting in,
and he never built a thing twice.

Donald sat at the scale with the day's take.
He had been at it better than an hour.
Weigh, enter, pour off. Weigh, enter, pour off.
The books lay open at his right elbow,
and the lamp was turned high enough to read the needle
and no higher.
Gold came across the desk from his left
and went down into the columns under his right,
and that was the whole trade,
once you cleared the noise off it.
Metal on one side. Paper on the other.
A man in the middle, keeping the numbers.

The take lay in its tin by his left hand,
dust and small grain, a color men in town
dropped their voices to talk about.
Donald had never dropped his.
It weighed what it weighed.
The voice was for the other side of the desk,
where the numbers lived,
and even there he kept it to himself.

James worked beside him at the sacking,
filling, tying off, setting each sack in the row.
For a stretch the only sounds in the room
were the fire, the scale,
and the small dry run of dust into the pan.
The cold sat in with them all the same.
It always had.

James lasted about as long as James ever lasted.
He had a flyer in his shirt pocket,
gone soft at the creases from handling,
and now he had it out and tipped toward the lamp.
"Says here there's dancing seven nights a week,"
he said. "Dinner on every street corner.
A man could eat at a different table every night
for a year and never repeat himself."

"A man can be heard through a wall, too," Donald said.
"Put it away."

James put it away, grinning.
"You'll thank me when I know which corner to steer for."
He laughed at his own joke.

The laugh was back, then.
It had gone missing for a day or two on the mountain,
and on James a missing laugh was a loud thing.
In town somebody would have remarked on it.
In the office it could come and go as it pleased,
and Donald was glad to have it home where it was useful.
A James who laughed was a James nobody looked at twice.
The laugh had never been the trouble with James.
The trouble was the mouth it lived in.
He talked when he was pleased,
he was never so pleased as when he talked New York,
and some warm evening at Rob's stove
the talking could spend everything in this room.
Donald had half a mind to feed the flyer to the stove.
He let it be.
A man needed something to steer for,
and a steered James was a quieter James.

They worked a while without words.

"Any more talk about Nick?" James asked it quietly,
not looking up from the sack he was tying.

"The same talk," Donald said. "I give them the same answer.
Worst moment of my life, watching that boy
go off the mountain." He set the next weight
and read the needle. "I've said it so often now
it about says itself."

He had done the grieving where Bronston does its grieving,
at Rob's counter with his hat in his hand.
He had it down to a length by now: the line,
the look at the floor, the slow shake of the head,
and then a word about the sample,
so a man could feel all right about brightening.
They brightened every time.
He never once had to change the order.
There was a handshake that came with it,
a two-handed one men saved for funerals,
and Donald had learned to stand still for it.
He counted those. He could not help it.
Eleven, since the news went round.

He had turned the line loose the first morning back,
*sometimes fewer men return to camp than leave,*
and by the Saturday market it had come home to him
from two other mouths,
worn down to *fewer men come down than go up,*
which was shorter, and not his, and did the same work.
That was the convenient thing about a town.
You never had to carry a story anywhere.
You set it down and it walked.

"And nobody asks past it," James said.

"They ask about the sample," Donald said.
"They ask when the machinery goes up the mountain.
Riches keep a memory short.
By summer they won't recall his name."

James's hands slowed on the tie.
"No body's come down."

"No body's coming down." Donald leveled the pan.
"There was no way down to that water without rope,
and nothing lives in that water above a minute.
The river keeps its own books."

He had stood at the ledge himself while the light went,
and watched the water for a coat, a boot, anything,
and the water had given him nothing,
and he had entered nothing, and closed the account.

The climber had been good. Donald would enter that much.
Good on rock, quick with his hands, civil to everybody.
Back three weeks, and the old crew already had it going—
his father over again, they said, his father's son—
and there was the whole trouble in four words.
It was never the boy that Donald could not afford.
It was what the boy was handed on sight,
free, at every fire and counter in Bronston,
before he had done one thing to earn it.

He finished the weigh, poured off,
and pulled a clean sheet toward him instead of the next sack.
There was the other side of the desk to feed.

The sample report went down to Juno on the Thursday boat.
It had gone down the Thursday after every expedition
for years running.
The bank expected it, the town knew the rhythm,
and a rhythm broken is a question asked.
For years the reading had come up riches,
and the whole town downstream had bet on it every time.
Nobody at Rob's stove had ever thought past the boat.
Donald thought about little else.
Thursday was why. Thursday was the whole of why.
The report was due out on this week's boat,
and the man who drilled the tins was coming down
to make his reading,
and there had been two days on that mountain to decide
what Thursday's boat would and would not carry.

He squared the sheet on the blotter and let it lie.
There would be a report. There was always a report.
He had until Thursday to make the paper agree
with what the town already believed,
and he had never yet missed a boat.
The first sheet after the mountain would be the hardest.
After that the rhythm would carry it on its own.
Rhythm was the most believable thing there was.
Nobody audits the boat whistle.

The writing did not worry him. He was good at the writing.
The schedule was another matter.
A lie told once was a payment made and done with.
This one drew interest.
Every boat day the story wanted feeding,
and it would go on wanting, Thursday on Thursday,
until the day they were gone and it could starve.

He capped the ink and sat a moment with the desk.

The desk had been their father's desk.
The scale had been their father's scale.
Before the brothers counted, they had watched him count—
stood against that wall, the pair of them,
winter evenings, not talking, because talk threw the count.
The old man never asked either boy to hold anything,
pour anything, or learn anything.
They were there to see what the family was,
and to keep still while it was shown to them.
Their mother died when Donald was six and James four,
and after that the watching was most of the raising they got.

[TK: the mother's death at six and four is mined from the old
draft and not yet bible canon; the concrete incident of how
old man Grin used his sons is likewise open—canon says only
"unappreciated"—and is gestured here, not staged.]

Down at the workings it had gone otherwise.
The men had a whole day's warmth in them for Nick's father,
the second man at the mine and the first man in the town,
and warmth left over for the boy trailing after him.
For the owner's sons they had hats touched, and nothing after.
A boy could stand in that yard at eight years old
and learn exactly what his name bought
and exactly where it stopped buying.

Keep the place in good repair.
That had been about the whole of the old man's instruction,
and the brothers had kept it.
Donald looked at the tight walls, the swept floor,
the stove warming its corner,
and gave one short laugh with nothing in it.
The place was in good repair.
He had been owed more than a well-kept cabin,
owed it early and owed it long,
and the row of sacks filling at James's elbow
was that account settling at last.

He caught the drift of that and cut it off.
Sentiment threw a count worse than talk did.

"There'll be women in New York," James said.
He had circled back to the flyer in his head.
"Rich men marry well there. I read that too."

"Then you'll be married by spring," Donald said.
"I'm not shopping in New York.
I mean to speak for Grace before we go."

James looked over at him,
and for once had the sense not to laugh.

[TK: the exact shape of Donald's design on Grace—open
courtship or an engagement pressured through Jim's dependence
on the mine—is unresolved and must square with the ch1
"engagement needle" graft; the line is played neutral here.]

The quiet sat a while. James was the one to move it on.
"Duff got word to me this morning.
Under two weeks now.
Once the cargo's in place, we're on our way."

Donald ran the sum without meaning to run it.
Under two weeks was two more Thursdays.
Two more boats to feed. He could carry two.
He had carried fifteen years of them.

"Nothing changes before then," Donald said.
"Not a word, not a habit.
The town sees the two of us grieving
and getting on with the work,
every single day it cares to look."

James tied off the last sack and sat back.
"I'm trying to decide if I'm going to miss this place."

"Take your time," Donald said. "You've got two weeks."

"I'm serious, Don. Fifteen years in this room.
More, if you count the old man's years."

"I've counted the old man's years," Donald said.
"There isn't a good memory in the lot.
The day we're out of it, I hope the name Bronston
never crosses my mind again."

James thought that over and let it go,
which was the other reliable thing about him.

Donald reached for the last of the take,
poured the dust into the pan,
and watched the needle swing, and settle, and hold.

"You want to know what this town is," he said.
"It was never but two things.
The metal, and its endless hope for more of the metal.
The best thing we have going was never in the ground.
Hope doesn't sit in a safe.
Hope walks itself down to the bank and signs."

James laughed, since it was Donald's joke
and safe to laugh at.

The needle held. Donald wrote the number,
drew his line under the day, and closed the book.
He turned the lamp down.
The fire had the corner.
The cold had the rest.
