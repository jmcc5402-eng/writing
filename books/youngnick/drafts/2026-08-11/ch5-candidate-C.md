# Chapter 5 — The Counting House

Donald Grin had been at the scale above an hour
and the day's take was not yet half-counted.

The office was cold with the fire going.
It was always cold with the fire going.
The stove owned its corner and nothing past it,
and the lamp over the desk gave light
and only claimed to give heat.
Donald worked in his father's fingerless gloves,
because a man cannot read a beam in mittens
and cannot hold a pen bare past the first hour.
He set the next pan on the brass,
walked the weights across with one finger,
waited out the needle,
and wrote the figure in the book at his elbow.

The desk was the whole business in one piece of furniture.
Gold came to it on the left, in pans, off the day's take.
Paper left it on the right—weights entered,
lines squared, the book ruled to the ounce.
The scale had weighed their father's gold before theirs,
and it was accurate, because it was kept accurate,
the one thing in the room that had to be.
Everything the mine was,
and everything the town believed about the mine,
crossed this desk going one direction or the other,
and Donald sat in the middle with the pen.

James sacked the weighed gold at the bench beside him,
tying each sack at the neck and setting it down soft,
the knots quick and the breath over them white.
He was a few years younger than Donald
and had sacked at that bench since his hands were big enough,
and he could do it without looking,
which was the trouble,
because a man who can work without looking will talk.

The wind got a fingernail under a roof shake and worried it.
Their father used to say a man keeps his buildings tight
or learns why,
and the two of them had kept this cabin tight for him
every year of their boyhood, on ladders, in the wet.
Donald listened to the shake work loose and let it.

James had left off sacking
and was standing at the lamp with his flyer again.
He had carried it a month and it had gone soft at the folds.
NEW YORK CITY, it said, over a drawing of a street
with more lamps in it than Bronston had windows.

"It says there's dinner and dancing
on every corner of every street," James said.
"Every corner, Don.
What do you suppose they get for a dinner like that?"

"More than a man makes reading flyers."

"First night, I'm having the dinner and the dancing both.
Same corner, same night.
I've been deciding which order."

James laughed.
He laughed at everything; he always had.
For one morning on the mountain he had not,
and Donald had minded that quiet
more than he had ever minded the noise.
The laugh was back. Good.
A Grin who didn't laugh was a thing a town noticed,
and this was no season to be noticed in.

"When we're in New York—" James said.

"Put it away," Donald said.
"And mind who hears you say New York."

"Nobody hears me but you and the stove."

"That's the count I like. Hold it there."

James put the flyer inside his coat, grinning,
and went back to the sacks.

For a while there was the beam and the wind
and the small dry sound of gold going into canvas.

"Any more talk about Nick?"
James asked it quietly, eyes on the string.

"Talk, yes. Questions, no."
Donald lifted the pan clear and tared the beam.
"Worst moment of my life,
watching that boy lose his footing."
The sentence came out level and unhurried,
the shine worn off it from use.
He had said it at the store, on the street,
to every hat that stopped in front of him,
and he had said it so many times now
that it ran itself and left him free to read the scale.

Early on he had given the town one other sentence.
Sometimes fewer men return to camp than leave it—
said once, hat in hand, to nobody in particular,
and inside the week it had come back to him twice,
worn into a different shape and still doing its work,
from men who believed they had thought of it.
That was the whole of the job, done right.
You did not tell a town a story.
You handed it the first line
and stood out of the way while it told itself.

"They have it the same all over, then?" James said.
"The footing?"

"Rob has it word for word.
Nobody descended. There was no way down to start,
and no man lives in that water."
Donald set the next pan on.
"Come spring he's a sad story off a mountain.
By summer he's a line in his father's story.
Memories are short in a town
that believes it's about to be rich,
and this town has never believed anything harder.
They're not thinking about the boy.
They're thinking about the gold."

James nodded at the string and tied it.
He was quiet the length of another sack.
"I keep waiting for somebody to ask me something," he said.
"At the store. I stand there waiting for it."

"Nobody is going to ask you anything.
Say it here if you have to say it,
and say it nowhere else."

Donald left the scale to settle
and turned to the right-hand side of the desk.
The ink had gone thick with the cold
and came off the pen grudging,
and he stood the bottle near the lamp to loosen
while he read over what Thursday wanted of him.

The sample report was due out on the Thursday boat.
It had been due out on the Thursday boat all along,
fixed before the expedition ever left town,
and nothing that happened on the mountain had moved it.
The boat did not ask who came down.
The boat came Thursday and left Thursday,
and paper went out on it,
or questions came back up on it.
There had been a time when Thursday
was only the day the boat came.
It was a hard time to remember from here.

He did what could be done at night with cold ink
and set the sheet square in the middle of the desk,
where it would be the first thing morning showed him.
Gold was honest work by comparison.
You dug it once and it stayed dug.
Paper wanted feeding every boat day,
and it did not tire, and it did not forget,
and there was nobody else who could feed it.
James could sack. Duff could haul.
The paper was his,
every Thursday there was ever going to be,
for as long as they stayed.

The desk he fed it at had been their father's desk.
Donald had counted at it for almost fifteen years,
and before he counted, he had watched the counting,
he and James both, boys on the floor by the stove,
where the heat was and the counting wasn't.
Their father ran the beam and wrote,
ran the beam and wrote,
and if a figure came out wrong anywhere in the room,
he would find whose it was without looking up.
The lesson of those evenings was quiet,
and the boys learned it.
Gold made one sound going into the pan
and another going into canvas,
and a man could know the whole day by its sounds
and never once be spoken to.

Days, the boys had the run of the mine,
which sounds a better boyhood than it was.
The men coming off shift walked past the owner's sons
to gather around Nick's father,
and they saved their day up to spend on him—
sore hands, small jokes, whose timber had started singing—
and he took all of it and gave back better,
and his boy stood in the middle of that warmth
and thought it was weather.

Nobody saved anything up for the owner's sons.
The owner's sons got the wage-book nod.
Donald had decided young that the men were wrong,
and he had gone on deciding it most of his life,
with the particular diligence a man gives
to a thing he has to keep deciding.
Warmth was not wages.
It hauled nothing and it banked nothing,
and the man they had all loved died owning a belt.

That was old arithmetic and he did not run it anymore.
He ran the beam, and wrote.

"I'm going by Jim's before Thursday," he said.

James looked up with the grin already built. "Grace."

"Somebody ought to look in on that household,"
Donald said, and did not look up from the book,
and let the grin die unspent.
Grace was the one thing in this town
worth the cost of carrying out of it.

James held his peace the length of one sack,
which was as long as he held anything.
"Duff got word to me," he said.
"The cargo's ready inside two weeks.
Once it's in place, we're on our way."

Donald nodded and found he had counted it in boat days
without meaning to.
Two more Thursdays of feeding the paper,
and then the paper was somebody else's problem,
or nobody's.

James tied off the last sack and sat back on his heels.
"I'm trying to decide if I'm going to miss this place."

Donald gave the room more time than the question was worth.
The stove in its corner. The sacks. The beam gone still.
The desk with the lamp on it and the night on the window.

"I've spent most of my life in this room," he said.
"Name one thing in it worth missing."

"There's the money."

"The money's coming with us."

James laughed, pleased with the both of them,
and got up and set the sacks away.
Donald went back to the book.
He had gone through this town more than once, looking,
and come out with his hands empty every time,
and he hoped to live a long life
in which the name of it never once crossed his mind.

The last pan crossed the beam late.
Donald entered the weight and drew the rule under the column,
and the figure at the foot of the page was a good figure.
The town would hear a version of it and be proud.

"Thursday, then," James said, at the door.
He took his coat off the peg and said his goodnight,
and when the door opened,
the night reached in and helped itself
to what little the stove had put by.
His boots rang on the path, going away.
The door shut, and the lamp stood the flame back up straight.

Donald sat on alone with the lamp and the book.
People thought the Grins' business was gold.
Gold was the smaller half of it.
The best thing they had going was the metal
and this town's endless hope of more of it,
and hope was the easier dig.
It cost nothing to raise and nothing to carry.
The town produced it by the pound,
and nobody had ever once asked to see it weighed.
