# Chapter 5 — The Counting House

The gold went across the scale a pan at a time,
and the figures went into the book,
and between the pan and the book sat Donald Grin,
who had spent the last hour turning the one into the other,
and most of his life before that.

The office was one room and one lamp,
a cold walk out from where the brothers slept,
and Donald had never once minded the walk.
A man thought better with the town behind him.
The stove had been going in the corner since dark
and the cold sat in the walls anyway;
the cabin had never held its heat in Donald's memory,
and he had long ago stopped billing it for that.
He worked in his coat.
The ink thickened when the bottle stood too far from the stove,
so it stood in by the scale weights,
where his hand knew the reach to both without looking,
and when it dragged even so
he rolled the pen between his palms and went on.

The desk was laid out as it was always laid out.
Metal on the left, paper on the right.
The take came up the left side in pans,
got weighed, got written, went into the sacks,
and by the time it crossed to the right side of the desk
it wasn't gold anymore, it was figures.
Gold had to be guarded.
Figures sat quiet in a book all winter
and guarded themselves.

James worked at the table behind him, sacking.
He was better at it than the town would have guessed,
tying off each neck in the same two motions,
setting the sacks along the wall in rows,
never a grain spilled that Donald had ever found.
Whatever else there was to say about James,
and Donald had said most of it over the years,
his hands were honest.
It was the rest of him that talked.

The tying stopped.
Donald did not need to turn around to know why.

"Don. Listen to this."

"No," Donald said.

James read it out anyway, which Donald had known he would.
The flyer was New York City,
smoothed flat on the table with both hands,
the paper gone soft at the folds from riding in his coat.
Dancers, it said. Dinner on every street corner.
James read it slowly, giving every word its full wage.

"They dance past midnight, Don. Every night of the week there is."

"Put it away," Donald said. "The gold first."

"It is away." A pause, while it went away in fact.
"Dinner on every corner," James said to the sacks.
"A man wouldn't cook once the rest of his life."
He laughed at that, pleased with it,
a beat past what the joke was worth,
and took up the tying again.

The laugh was back.
It had gone missing for one day, up the mountain,
and Donald had not missed it.
A man who laughed at everything
was a man the town never looked at twice,
and that had its uses.
The trouble was the freight that came with him.
James talked.
He came in every evening loaded with the town's talk
and stood by the stove and unloaded it,
who said what at Rob's, who owed, who ailed,
and a man who carried talk in one direction
could carry it in the other without ever meaning to.
Donald kept that thought where he kept the others like it,
near the top.

For a while there was the stove and the wind
and the small dry sounds of the work.

"Any more talk about Nick?"
James asked it quietly, and he did not look up from the sack.

"Some," Donald said. "It's settling."

"What do you say, when they raise it?"

"That watching that boy fall was the worst moment of my life."
The line came out of him worn smooth as a scale-weight;
he had said it so often around town it ran without him now.
He had built it with some care the first evening,
chosen the words, set them in their order,
and by the tenth telling it cost nothing to say.
He had waited, the first day or two, for it to cost—curiosity,
he supposed, more than conscience—and it hadn't,
and he had stopped waiting.
"Sometimes fewer men return to camp than leave," he said.
"I say that as well.
One of the men at Rob's stove said it back to me this week
and thought it was his own."

"Lost his footing," James said, nodding at the sack.
"It's the country."

"It's the country," Donald said.

The country. Nobody had gone down after a body;
there had been no way down to go,
and the river under that cliff ran fast and black and cold,
and water like that settled its own accounts.

A careful boy, the stove men kept saying. Careful.
Donald had carried tins up a hillside himself at that age.
There was nothing in the work a mule couldn't do
if a mule could hold a trowel.
The town had made a craft of it
because the town had loved the father,
and the boy got the shine off the old coin.

The town would want the story simple, and it wanted it brief.
A town waiting on gold could not afford long grief.
Memories were short up here—the country saw to that—and by
the time the new machinery came up the water
every man at that stove would be thinking of the mountain
in pounds and ounces again, and of the boy not at all.

He finished the pan on the scale, wrote the figure,
and turned his chair to the right-hand side of the desk,
because the metal could wait and the paper could not.

The boat came Thursday.
The sample report was to have gone out on it.
That had been the arithmetic of the week—the boy down off
the mountain with his six tins,
the reading due in Juno by the Thursday boat—and the week
had been handled.
But the boat was still coming,
and something still had to be on it.

He drew a clean sheet to him and squared it.
Before, the books had only to agree with themselves.
Now they had to agree with a dead man,
and they had to do it fresh every boat day,
and boat day came once a week,
and the week never stopped coming.
He sat with that no longer than it deserved.
It was not a thing to feel. It was a thing to schedule.

"Don." The tying had stopped again.
When Donald turned, the flyer was nowhere in sight,
and James stood looking at the rows of sacks
with his head to one side, counting nothing.
"Who climbs for us, come the next sample?"

It was the right question.
That was the thing about James
that the town would never credit
and that Donald never said aloud:
the man dreamed through nine hours of a working day,
and in the tenth he put his finger
on the one seam you had not caulked.
The hope out there would want feeding again before long,
and the man who fed it was in the river.

Donald let the question stand in the room
while he trimmed the lamp.

"Count your sacks," he said.

James counted his sacks.
But he did not laugh while he did it,
and Donald put that down too,
in the ledger he kept nowhere.

The desk had been their father's desk.
The brothers had sat in this room counting
for almost fifteen years,
and before they counted, they had watched their father count,
two boys stood against that wall
with the day's cold still on them,
learning the one lesson on offer,
which was that the count went on
whether boys were tired or not.

*Keep the cabin in good repair.*
The old man's voice still lived
in the walls he'd made them keep sound.
Every fall of their boyhood the two of them were up a ladder
in the wind, re-laying shakes, chinking the walls,
and no word came after, fall upon fall,
because a word was a wage,
and their father paid no wage he could keep.
Donald looked at the tight wall and the sound roof
and gave the reminder the short breath through the nose
he had been giving it for years.

From the office steps, in those years,
you could watch the shift come down the hill at dark.
Nick's father came down in the middle of the men,
and the talk went where he went,
and when his boy ran up the road to meet him
the whole line called the boy's name too, hats and all,
and the day ended right there in the road
for every man on it.
Nobody ever once called up the hill to the office steps.
That was all. It was an old sum,
and Donald did not re-run old sums.

They were owed.
He had believed it at ten,
standing on those steps with James's cold hand in his,
and he believed it now,
and the difference was that now he kept the books.

"I'll call on Grace tomorrow," he said.

James looked up, and laughed, because he was James.

"A town likes to see grief kept company," Donald said,
and let it stand at that,
and so did the part of him that had more to say on the subject.

The last sack went into the row.
James stood back from it and wiped his hands on his coat.

"Duff sent word today," he said. "Under two weeks.
Once the cargo's in place, we're on our way."

Donald nodded and wrote nothing.
Some figures a man carries; they go in no book.
Two weeks, against a boat that came every Thursday.
He ran the two clocks against each other once, cleanly,
and put them away.

"I'm trying to decide," James said,
"if I'm going to miss this place."
He said it to the sacks, trying the idea on.

Donald ran that inventory honestly, since it was short.
Fifteen years in this room.
The wall, the bench, the office steps before that.
Not one thing in the town he would cross a street to see twice.

"No," he said. "And once we're gone,
I hope the name of it never crosses my mind again."

James thought it over and decided to laugh at it,
and went back to squaring his rows.

The last of the take went across the scale pan by pan,
dust and small stuff and one piece worth a second look,
and Donald weighed it, wrote it, totted the column,
and closed the book on it.
He sat a moment with his hand flat on the cover.
The stove ticked. The wind tried the door and let it be.
Out there the town was in its beds and talking in them still,
most like—clearest sample yet, two more years of metal,
maybe three, and the payout riding safe on all of it.

Donald put on his coat and buttoned it to the throat.
The path home would have ice on it by now;
it froze early, this late in the year,
and a careful man walked it slow.

He had the true line half in mind, going out,
the one he never said aloud, James included.
The best thing they had going was the metal,
and this town's endless hope for more of it,
and of the two, only the hope never needed weighing.
