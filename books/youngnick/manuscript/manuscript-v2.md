# Young Nicholas
# Chapter 1 — The Push

He was probably the first human being ever to set foot
on this shoulder of the Alaskan mountainside.
Mountain goats might have tried,
but honestly, what reason would a goat have to come up this far?
Nick had a reason: gold.

The ground sample in his pack would tell Grin Bros. Mining
whether this side of the mountain was worth the cost of working it.
The method wasn't perfect,
but it had called the ground right for years running,
and the company had learned to lean on it.

He scaled the last few rocks below the summit
and reached into his pack for his equipment,
and the wind chose that moment to pick up.
With his one free hand Nick dragged his stocking cap down tighter.
He was dressed for work, not for weather,
and he was already looking forward to the descent to the base camp.
Going down ought to be easier than climbing up.
He knew better. That was the trouble with going down.

Three weeks back in Alaska
and his blood was still California-thin.
Ten years, he thought. Had it really been ten?
He did the math while his hands did the work.
He had left when he was eleven,
and he had turned twenty-one last month —
some birthday, alone and freezing
on the bottom deck of the barge from Seattle to Juno,
listening to the water hiss along the hull all night.
Ten years, then. The math never came out any warmer.

It started to snow while he drilled.
He had figured it might
and was glad it had held off this long.
He bored a few holes into the rock,
leaning his weight into the brace
until the bit chewed through the frozen crust
and brought up spoil the color of old iron.
Then he used the small trowel
to pack a handful of tin containers with earth,
working bare-fingered because the trowel demanded it,
warming his hand in his armpit between tins,
tamping each one and sealing it against the wet.
Six containers, six depths, as the Grins wanted it.
What anybody could read out of six tins of dirt
was past his understanding,
but the reading had come up riches before,
and the whole town downstream was betting it would again.
His hands knew the work well enough
that his head was free to run the other arithmetic,
the one his father had drummed into him
before Nick was old enough to carry a pack:
*Shelter, fire, food in the cold.
Shelter, food, fire in the warm.*
Shelter was base camp, two hours down if the ice behaved.
Fire was already burning there.
Food was jerky in his pocket and stew in the pot if he was lucky.
In the cold you counted them in that order,
and you started counting before you needed them.
His father had never explained the creed past saying it.
The order was the explanation.

When he finished, Nick took a quick look around,
the kind of look you take from a place
nobody has ever stood before
and nobody is likely to stand again.
An eagle hung off in the distance, riding the wind.
While he watched, it folded and dropped —
straight down, a stone with a purpose —
and pulled up at the last moment, empty.
Whatever it had marked in the snow
had scurried somewhere better in time.
"Some days it goes like that," Nick said to the eagle,
and the wind took the words off his lips and away north.

The buckle of his pack strap was ice under his thumb
when he cinched it for the descent,
and for no reason he could name
he thought of his father's belt —
dark leather gone soft at the fold,
a row of emblems worked into it,
shapes he had traced with a finger as a boy
until he could draw them in the dirt from memory.
His father had worn it every day of his life.
Nick let the thought go as he had learned to,
quick, before it could open any further,
and started down.

Ice forms fast on any surface in that kind of cold,
and it does not announce itself.
He came down the rocky peak toward the ledge
testing each hold twice, moving on three points,
the way a man climbs when there is nobody to carry him out.
Five feet above the ledge a rock shifted under his boot.
His hand found the root of a scrub bush
before his weight went anywhere it shouldn't,
and that was the whole of it —
one hard beat of his heart, one breath let out slow.
The rock he'd trusted went over the edge without him.
He listened for it to land in the crevice below
and heard nothing. Nothing at all.
He gave the crevice one look, a cold one,
the look you give a thing that has just told you the truth,
and moved on down.

The quiet of that no-sound stayed with him a few steps.
Deep places did that to him.
The morning the earthquake came, ten years ago,
he had been angry at his father —
over old man Grin, over how his father took it,
quiet, every time, never once standing square to the man.
Nick had been angry all that day,
and by dark his father was gone,
and there had never been a place to set the anger down since.
He put his hand to the next hold. The mountain didn't care.

The snow let up as he worked down out of the high rocks,
and the walking got easier,
and by the time he saw the camp below him
the light had gone long and yellow across the snow.
The camp sat on a bench of level ground
between the mountain's foot and the cliff over the river:
the one big wall tent, the fire ring,
the two sleighs drawn up nose to tail,
the gear lashed under canvas against the weather.
Smoke stood up from the tent.
He could nearly feel the fire from here.
The dogs were loose, digging themselves burrows for the night,
turning three times and screwing themselves down into the snow
with their noses under their tails,
which surprised him —
he had hurried down hoping the party would push
on to the lower camp before dark.
Evening was the time to travel:
the sleighs ran fast on the icy trails after sundown,
and morning always brought fresh snow to bog the dogs.
If the Grins were bedding down here, they had a reason.
The Grins generally had a reason.

He stopped outside the tent and shrugged off his pack,
and that was when he heard them going at it inside.
The wind was gusting and stealing words,
so it reached him in pieces,
a lull handing him a whole line
and the next gust taking three away.

"—won't be time in San Francisco!"
That was Donald, pitched low and hard.

James answered, too low to carry. He always answered too low.

"I don't care about the damn redwoods, we have to keep moving."
Donald again, and then the wind, and then scraps:
"...New York..." "...the bank..." "...three more weeks?..."
and, almost lost, "...if just one person finds out..."

Nick shook his head and picked up his pack again.
Ten years later and those two were still going at it.
The redwoods line snagged on him for a second —
he was the only man in this camp
who had ever put a saw to a redwood,
and he wondered what his trees were doing
in a Grin argument about banks and cities.
Then the cold got a vote, and the vote was the fire,
and he let it go. Brothers were brothers.

The tent flap moved.
James Grin backed out of it,
turned around, and looked straight at Nick.
Surprise came up in his face first,
and behind the surprise something else,
something that didn't belong on a man
looking at his own sample climber home safe.

"You're back," James said.

"Snow held off long enough." Nick lifted the pack an inch.
"Got your sample."

James's eyes went to the tent and came back.
"We didn't figure you until dark," he said. "If at all."

"It's a friendly mountain," Nick said,
"if you don't believe anything it tells you."

James didn't laugh.
James laughed at everything —
it was the most reliable thing about the man —
and he didn't laugh.

"You come straight down?" James asked. "Just now?"

"Straight as it let me."

"Well," James said.
He stood there a moment longer than a man stands for nothing,
his eyes doing a thing Nick couldn't read,
some sum being run behind them and coming out poorly.
Then he went back inside without another word.

Nick spent a few minutes squaring away his equipment,
coiling the line, wiping down the drill and casing it,
setting the sample containers in their case by the fire ring
where the Grins could count them if counting made them happy.
Habit, all of it, his father's habit before his:
gear squared away tonight is gear that works tomorrow.
Then he knelt down at the fire and put jerky on to warm.
The heat worked into his hands and face
like something he'd been owed for a long time
and was finally collecting.
His shoulders let down a half-inch at a time.
It had been a long climb, and a longer three weeks,
and for a minute, chewing warm jerky by another man's fire
under the first stars he'd trusted since Seattle,
Nick came as close to easy as he'd been since he landed.
One of the dogs lifted its head to watch him eat,
decided he wasn't going to share, and went back to its burrow.
The light went from yellow to gray.
The river, somewhere below the cliff at the camp's edge,
kept up its noise as it had all week,
a sound you stopped hearing until you stopped hearing anything else.

Footsteps came up behind him, fast on the frozen ground.

He turned to look,
and all he saw was a dirty glove
swinging a large wooden log at his head.

The blow caught him square on the forehead.
White went across his eyes.
He fell back, got an arm up,
and two strong arms took him from behind and pinned it.

The second man hit him twice in the body,
short blows, thrown by somebody who had thrown them before,
and Nick doubled over around them.
Then he was off the ground —
picked up whole and slung across the camp,
and the direction he was slung was the cliff.

He hit and rolled, trying to break loose,
and a boot came down square in the middle of his back
and drove the air out of him against the frozen dirt.
He heard himself groan.
His head wouldn't line the pieces up.
Was the camp being raided?
They had nothing worth taking —
tents, dogs, a case of dirt in tins.
Where were the Grins?
He dragged in breath to shout for them,
and the two strong arms hauled him up
and ran him at the edge of the cliff.

He dug his hands into the ground,
fingers ripping through snow-crust into frozen gravel,
and came to a stop with his head hanging over the edge.

Below him — a long way below him,
a hundred feet of dark air below him —
the river ran loud and black between its shelves of ice.

He got one knee under himself.

Both men kicked him at the same time,
and the ground went away,
and the river swung up to take its turn.

He flipped once in the falling
and slammed off the mountainside,
and, flailing, dropping, he looked up
and saw his assailants at the cliff edge,
two shapes against the last of the sky,
shapes he had known his whole life.

It was the Grin brothers.

# Chapter 2 — The Ledge

The kick sent him out into open air.

His left arm and shoulder slammed into a rock below
and the impact spun him,
and after that the mountain and the sky traded places
faster than he could keep the count.
He clawed at the cliff face as it went by,
tearing his fingers on rock and ice,
slowing nothing.

From the corner of his eye he caught the branch of a big bush
standing out from the rock,
and he reached — too late, a fistful of air —
but the reach turned his body,
and by throwing his weight against the spin
he changed where he was falling,
which was the only thing left in the world he could change.
He came down hard in a loose spill of rocks,
and now the water was thirty feet below him
instead of straight underneath,
and he was on his seat, sliding for it,
heels dug in and cutting two useless grooves.

He rolled onto his stomach
and spread his hands against the moving gravel.
To his left, in the rock, a hole —
deep, round-shouldered, lined dark: an eagle's nest.
His feet went off the edge of the cliff,
and he drove his arm into the hole to the armpit.

He stopped.

Pain went up through the arm and burst in his shoulder,
and for a while he just lay there and let it,
his cheek against cold rock,
his waist and legs hanging off the cliff,
his arm socketed in an eagle's nest
that some bird had packed with sticks and moss
for reasons that had nothing to do with saving his life.
The arm didn't feel broken.
It didn't feel like anything at all below the shoulder,
which he decided to call good news.

Eight feet under his boots the river went by,
loud, fast, dragging its ice along the shelves.
Eight feet. He could have spat into it.
Anything — anything on this mountain — was better than that water.

Rock clattered somewhere above him.

Nick went still.

He turned his head, slow, only his head,
and looked up the way he had come.
At the top, at the ledge line, dark against the last light,
a man's silhouette leaned out over the drop.

They were looking for him. Of course they were looking for him.
You don't kick a man off a cliff
and then not look over the edge.

He was in open view where he hung,
one more gray shape among gray rocks — until he moved wrong,
or until the light found his face,
the one pale thing on a mountainside at dusk.
He put his face down against the rock.
He drew his arm out of the nest a finger-width at a time,
taking the weight on his other hand,
letting no stick snap and no gravel run.
His breath wanted to come fast and it wanted to come white,
a little flag of steam standing up over the rocks,
so he breathed into his collar, long and slow,
and counted the seconds going in and the seconds going out.
When the wind moved, he moved.
When the wind died, he was a rock among rocks.
Six feet to his left a grouping of boulders stood higher,
and he worked himself into the seam behind them
one wind-gust at a time,
and pressed his back flat against stone,
and waited.

The silhouette stayed at the ledge a long time.
It moved along the edge, stopped,
leaned out to search the water downstream —
looking for a body, he understood.
Looking for the river to show them a coat, a boot,
something to carry back to the fire.
Nick's shoulder was a nail being driven in slow,
and the cold came up out of the rock into his chest,
and a stick from the nest lay across his neck like a finger,
and he held.
A pebble ticked down the face somewhere to his right,
knocked loose by wind or by a boot at the top,
and he heard it hit the water,
and he held that too.
Nobody started down. There was no way down to start;
the drops were too sharp for a man without rope,
and no one who had just thrown a man off a cliff
was going to spend rope on making sure.

The silhouette drew back and the ledge line stood empty.

He gave it another fifty breaths, counted into his collar,
before he trusted the emptiness.
That was his father in him too, probably.
The man had never once trusted quiet
just because it was quiet.

Nick let his breath out of his collar
and lay in the seam of the rocks a few minutes,
shaking now that shaking was allowed.

What had just happened?
He set the fact out where he could look at it:
the Grin brothers had tried to kill him.
Not robbed him. Not run him off. Killed him.
He knew he wasn't loved by the Grins —
he had never once been loved by the Grins —
but there was a distance between not loved and this,
and he couldn't find the road that crossed it.
Three weeks since he'd stepped off the barge,
three excursions into the mountains for samples.
This one was the riskiest;
they had all agreed it might carry the biggest payoff.
He thought of the sample case sitting by the fire ring
where he'd set it out for them, neat as an offering.
Not that any of it mattered now.
Whatever they had wanted, it wasn't in those tins.

He turned the day over looking for the crack in it.
The climb — no. The sample — no.
The camp, the smoke, the argument in the tent.
James backing out of the flap
and looking at him like a column of figures
that refused to come out even.
*If just one person finds out.*
Finds out what? Nick had heard nothing —
scraps, brother-noise, ten years of the same bickering
he'd grown up hearing through thinner walls than canvas.
But the Grins didn't know what he'd heard.
The Grins only knew he'd been standing there.
It wasn't an answer. It was the shape of one, maybe,
a track too old to read in failing light,
and he was too cold to stand over it any longer.

He looked up the mountain.
Half an hour, he guessed, since he'd been pushed.
The cliff bulged out above him in slick shelves;
without equipment he'd be off it again in twenty feet,
and the second fall would finish what the first had started.
Up was closed.

He took stock of the rest, left and right.
Left, an icy slope ran down and out
and delivered everything it carried into the river.
Right, more rocks —
rough, black, stacked away toward a rise.
That was the whole menu. Right, then.

The river kept talking underneath him while he rested,
and he made the mistake of listening.
Men fell into rivers like that one every year or two.
They never lived.
Even the few who got pulled out or crawled out
never made it more than a few hours after.
His father had said it the way he said everything,
once, plainly, and never again:
"Freezing water shuts down the heart."
He'd said it at the edge of spring ice
with his hand on Nick's shoulder,
and the hand had weighed more than the words.
Nick looked at the black water and heard it in his ears
and edged right.

He was four feet from the first solid boulder
when the dirt under him let go.

He slid, everything sliding with him,
and there was no time to be careful and less to be smart —
he twisted his body and lunged,
both arms thrown around a rock the size of a stove.
If it pulled loose, he and the rock
would go into the river together
and the river would not much care about the order.

It held.

He hung on it a moment longer than he needed to,
then pulled himself up and over,
and lay on the boulder's flat back looking at the sky.
This was too much adventure for one day,
and the day, by the light, wasn't done spending him yet.
From up here he could finally see his position whole:
the rocks ran on to his right, rising,
a rocky hill a man could climb without wings.
For the moment, nothing under him was moving.
He stopped to catch his breath and count his working parts.

His shoulder throbbed where it had met the mountain,
a deep slow beat like a second heart,
and for some reason the throb made him think of Grace.

He'd seen her once since he came back. Once.
It felt like a long time ago
and it had been all of a few days.

He had come up the channel three weeks back
on a mail boat out of Juno,
and the town had been smaller than he remembered it,
and thinner, the paint gone gray on the buildings,
the street quieter than a street ought to be.
He'd told the barge captain in Seattle
that he was going home because he missed the country,
and that was true as far as it went.
He had never once finished the sentence,
not out loud, not even in his own head.

A few days ago he had come into town for provisions,
and Old Rob had been out front of the general store,
sweeping snow off the boards like snow could be discouraged.
Rob had squinted at him.

"I know that face," Rob said. "Don't tell me. I'll get it."

"Nick," Nick said.

"I'll be." Rob leaned on the broom.
"The boy that went south. Look at the size of you.
Your father was the best man this town ever had, you know it?"

"I know it," Nick said.

"Best man this town ever had," Rob said again,
the way old men drive a nail twice.
"What's it like down there in the States, then?
They say the trees run three hundred foot."

"Taller," Nick said. "You cut all day
and the top of the tree hasn't heard about it yet."

Rob laughed at that until he coughed.

And that was when the feedstore door across the street opened
and Grace came out of it with a basket on her arm.

Ten years, and he'd have known her at twice the distance —
known the walk, known how she pushed her hair
off her forehead with the back of her wrist,
known her in the dark, probably, by the shape of the air.
She stopped on the walk to settle the basket.
If she had looked up she'd have been looking straight at him.

Her name was right there in his mouth.
One word. Two steps off the boards and one word.

He stood there and held his sack of beans
like a man holding sacks of beans was his whole trade,
and said nothing,
and she turned down the street and was gone around the corner.

"You all right, son?" Rob had asked him.

"Long trip," Nick had said. "It catches up."

She probably didn't even remember him.
That was the story he'd told himself walking back to camp,
and he told it to himself again now, lying on a boulder
with the river underneath waiting to hear how it ended.
He didn't believe it either time.

They used to spend whole days climbing rocks like these.
Nick, Grace, and the Grin brothers — quite the group.
But that was years ago,
before Nick's father died, before the earthquake,
and before the Grins became killers.

A cold gust came off the water
and brought him back to the mountain,
and the mountain's facts had not improved.
The sun was setting.
Night up here was two kinds of dangerous, cold and teeth,
and he had no fire, wet clothes, and a shoulder half-made of pain.
If he didn't find shelter he would freeze,
simply and without ceremony,
and the Grins would turn out to be right about him after all.
Grace would have to wait.

He crawled on over the rocks, rightward and rising,
testing every hold before he trusted it,
resting when the shaking in his arms demanded it.
The wind came again and made his nose and ears shout with pain.
The light was going purple in the west,
the river below fading to a sound.
He hauled himself over a shelf of rock,
turned to his right,
and there — set back in the rocks where nothing above
and nothing on either side could see it —
was exactly what he was looking for.

A cave.

# Chapter 3 — The Glow

A slow sleet had started, which was bad two ways.
Wet clothes on a freezing night would kill him
almost as surely as the river,
only slower and with more time to think about it.
And sleet greased every rock between him and the cave.

He crossed the last of the cliff a hold at a time,
resting on a collection of rocks
about six feet from the opening.
The cave mouth stood to his left;
to his right the ground fell away steep and sudden
to the river, which was still daring him to fall.
He almost hopped straight inside, glad to be done —
and his instincts grabbed him by the collar and stopped him.
No reason to rush now.
He had not come this far
to be torn apart in the dark by a polar bear,
or a wolverine with prior claim to the property.

He found a fist-sized rock and held it,
which proved mostly that he owned a rock,
and he waited, and listened.
Sleet ticking. Wind. The river.
Nothing in the dark breathing that wasn't him.
After a few minutes of that he felt it was safe to proceed,
or as safe as anything had been since noon.

The mouth of the cave had a flat base,
six feet wide and maybe three deep, sturdy underfoot,
and he stopped on it to catch his breath.
The opening itself ran about six feet wide and five tall.
He'd be able to walk in, but bent.
Bent, at the moment, was no setback at all.

Better still was what the cave did not do.
It could not be seen from above —
he'd crossed within feet of it without seeing it —
and he doubted it showed from either side.
If the Grins ever came looking, and they wouldn't,
they could stand on the ledge above
and look straight past his front door.
He filed that away and felt a notch safer for it.

Inside, the last daylight reached about eight feet
and gave up.
Past that the cave went back into the mountain, pitch black,
and he didn't attempt it.
But in the reach of the light, in the bottom corner,
the first thing he saw gave him a glimmer of hope:
pine needles, a whole drift of them,
packed into the corner by animals
that had known this trick longer than men had —
a dry bed's worth of warmth and insulation,
free for the raking.

The right course was a fire.
He gave the cave one quick look and erased the thought.
No wood, no kindling, nothing to make a spark against.
He considered himself an expert woodsman —
Alaska until he was eleven,
ten years in the California timber after —
and expert, tonight, meant knowing what wasn't there.
The need here was plain: dry, and out of the wind.

His father used to make him say it back,
walking the trapline with their breath going up like smoke.

"Cold country first," his father said. "Say it."

"Shelter, fire, food in the cold," Nick said,
eight years old, hurrying to keep inside his father's tracks.

"And in the warm?"

"Shelter, food, fire in the warm."

"That's it. That's the whole of it."

His father never explained it any further.
The order was the explanation.
Shelter first, always;
and if fire couldn't be had, shelter counted double.

So: shelter, twice.
He gathered rocks for about twenty minutes,
prying them out of the slope by the cave mouth
with fingers that had stopped reporting for duty,
and stacked them across the entrance,
biggest at the bottom, faces fitted,
chinking the gaps with smaller stone,
a dry wall a foot high, then two, then waist-high,
his bad shoulder billing him for every stone above the second course,
until the wind stopped reaching in to feel around.
The quiet that came then was worth the twenty minutes alone.
Outside, the sleet kept ticking against the mountain.
Inside, for the first time since the fire ring at camp,
the air stood still enough to warm a half-inch of itself
against his skin, and he stood in that half-inch
and could have wept for how much it felt like wealth.

Then the bed.
He raked the pine needles into a long pile
and packed handfuls of them down his sleeves
and into his pant legs,
until he rustled when he moved
and looked, he suspected, like a scarecrow
that had lost an argument with a mountain.
He wanted his boots off — his feet were soaked —
but bare toes freeze faster than wet ones,
and he kept them on.

He was lying down before he remembered the jerky.
Four strips in an inside pocket, fire-warmed once, long cold.
The hunger arrived all at once,
now that the body had decided the emergency was over.
He ate two strips, slowly, making each one last,
and put the other two back.
The arithmetic on those two strips was short and unfriendly,
but a man can't sleep on an empty stomach,
and sleep tonight was worth more than breakfast tomorrow.
He closed his eyes.
Within seconds he was gone.

The dream was very simple.
He was sleighing down the mountain in the dark,
runners hissing on the icy trail,
and the base camp was ahead of him,
and he could smell meat on the outdoor grill.
The red glow of the grill grew bigger as he came on,
red and warm and sure of itself,
and he leaned toward it.

He woke.

No sleigh. No camp. Cave.
His whole body reported in, one ache at a time.
He turned toward the entrance:
pitch black past his rock wall, no stars, no gray.
He had no idea what time it was
or how long he had slept.

But the red glow was still there.

It was behind him — deeper in the cave —
a dull reddish hue standing in the dark,
too dim to light a path, too steady to be imagined.
He lay still and looked at it for a long moment.

"That's not a fire," he said, mostly to hear a voice.
The voice came out cracked and strange, and the glow,
being a glow, did not answer.
It wasn't the color of fire — no flicker, no climb —
and it didn't seem to be far away.
There was no sense lying still with his mind racing,
so he stood up, slowly, everything protesting,
and went to find out what the mountain kept in its back room.

He stepped deeper in.
The ceiling here let him almost straighten —
five and a half feet, he judged,
half a foot short of the six his frame wanted.
He kept one hand on the rock wall to his right
and slid his feet instead of stepping,
and even so a snag of rock caught his boot twice
and nearly laid him out.
The glow grew as he came on,
larger and no brighter, red as banked coals,
until he stood in front of the thing that was making it.

A mound of dirt blocked the passage,
higher than his waist,
and the light was coming from underneath it.

The glow was coming up through the dirt.

Behind him the wind leaned on his rock wall,
and he remembered, distantly, like news from another country,
that his clothes were still half-soaked,
that when the sun came up
survival would own every hour of his day.
That was all true and it could wait.
He put his hand flat on the mound.

The dirt was warm.

He was very cold, and the dirt was warm,
and after that there was never really a decision to make.
He began to dig with his hands.

The dirt came away dry and loose,
and with every double-handful the glow strengthened,
red light rising through the thinning ground
like something surfacing from deep water.
The warmth came up with it,
into his fingers first, then his wrists,
thawing him from the hands inward
while the rest of him knelt in the cold and shook,
and he dug the way a starving man eats,
past sense, past caution,
grateful and a little afraid of his own gratitude.
Then his fingers met a thing that didn't fit.
Not rock. Smooth. Cooler than the dirt, harder —
metal, he thought first, and then, no — softer than metal.
Wood.

He dug around it, both hands now,
and the shape came up out of the ground:
a handle. A worked, curved, deliberate handle,
made by somebody, for holding.

He found a flat rock and used it like a trowel,
and the digging went faster.
Below the handle, glowing through its skin of dust,
was wood — dark red, smooth, sound as the day it was cut,
and the light was coming from inside it,
from the wood itself.
He kept digging.

A long edge came clear under his hands —
a rail, curving, carrying the handle —
and down the face of the rail, carving.
His fingers found it before his eyes did:
grooves cut into the wood,
fine and deep and ornate,
loops and lines turning through each other
in a pattern too regular to be accident
and too strange to be decoration for decoration's sake.
Whoever had made this had meant every cut.

And in the middle of the pattern, one groove stood empty.

It was deeper than the others, and simpler —
an oval socket the size of his palm,
its edges worn smooth as a river stone,
shaped to hold something that was not there.
The carvings flowed toward it from both sides.

Nick went still with his fingers resting in the grooves.

He half-knew these shapes.
He knew them the way you know a word in a dream —
certain, on the tongue, and gone when you reach for it.
Somewhere. He had seen something like them somewhere,
sometime, and he could not for his life say where,
and the harder he reached the further it slid.
He took his hand away and let it go.
The mountain had given him enough riddles for one night;
it could keep one till morning.

He dug until his arms gave out,
and by then there was no more mistaking it.
It finally made sense, but it made no sense at all.

In the middle of the Alaskan mountainside,
buried under the dirt floor of a hidden cave,
glowing from inside its own wood like a coal that would not die,
was an old sleigh.

A red glowing sleigh.

# Chapter 4 — The News in Town

Grace came home from Mrs. Jones's earlier than usual.
The evening was cold and clear,
the kind of evening that made the whole street hold still,
and her father was on the front porch with his coat on,
taking the last of the light like it was owed him.

"You're early," Jim said. "She run out of mending?"

"I ran out of patience." Grace came up the steps
and stood in front of his chair,
and found that her hands had gone and folded themselves
as they did when she had rehearsed a thing too long.
"I decided," she said. "I'm going to enroll in the university."

Jim closed his eyes and leaned back and smiled,
and for a moment he didn't say anything at all,
which from her father was a kind of applause.

"This is the proudest moment of my life," he said.

"You've said that before."

"It keeps coming true." He stood and hugged her,
wool coat and pipe smoke and the whole of her childhood,
and held on one beat longer than usual.
"Come on. Let's celebrate."

"Celebrate with what?"

"There's tea," he said. "There's nearly tea."

She laughed, and then made herself stop laughing,
because the next part was the part she had rehearsed.

"I want you to hear why," she said.
"Not because you've been at me about it all winter.
You could have kept at me another ten years.
I decided because of Mother."

Jim's hand paused on the door latch.

"They gave her nine kinds of nothing," Grace said.
"Boiled water and prayers and a man from Juno
who charged four dollars to look at her tongue.
Typhoid isn't a mystery, it's in the books —
somebody is going to run it to ground,
and I mean to be in the room when they do.
I'm not going south to get out of Bronston.
I'm going south to become a doctor."

Her father looked at her a long time.

"Your mother used to say you had a memory like a bear trap,"
he said. "Nothing that walks into it walks back out.
I'd quiz you on the bones of the hand
when you were six years old, and you'd rattle them off
and then correct my order."

"Your order was wrong."

"My order was wrong," he agreed. "Eighteen months of medicine
in San Francisco and the bones still beat me."
He said it lightly, as he always said it,
eighteen months of school traded for a shovel and a boat north,
a whole life folded into a joke by long practice.

"Come with me," Grace said. "There. That's the rest of it.
Come with me and finish what you started."

"Gracie."

"You hate the mine."

"I don't hate the mine. I know the mine.
There's a difference, and at my age
the difference is nearly a comfort." He shook his head slowly.
"I don't want a ship, and I don't want a new house,
and I don't want to learn a new town's streets.
I want to know my girl is where she belongs.
That I want badly."

"The mine kills men, Papa. I've been to the funerals.
So have you. You've carried the boxes."

"And it has fed us for twenty-five years," he said,
gently, closing it as he closed things,
not with a door but with a curtain.
"Write your mother's people when you're settled.
She'd want them knowing a doctor was coming up

Saturday she walked to Old Rob's with the letter
in her coat pocket and flour money in her glove.

The market used to be crowded on a Saturday.
Lately it was quiet everywhere,
the miners idle at the end of the week
and every one of them worried out loud or silently
that the mine was going dry underneath the town.
Rob's shelves said what nobody would:
gaps where the tinned goods used to stand,
one sack of coffee where there had been a row,
prices chalked over twice, both times upward.
Since the avalanche closed the pass,
everything the town ate or wore came in by boat,
and the boat charged what the boat charged.
Hope was the one thing on the shelves
that hadn't gone up in price,
and the town bought it by the pound.

Her letter sat against her ribs, patient as a stone.
Every few steps she was seventeen feet tall with it,
and every few steps after that
she was a girl about to get on a ship alone

Excited and terrified had stopped taking turns

"—clearest sample yet, is what they said."

Grace kept her face on the flour and smiled at the sack.
Everything her father had earned for four years —

collecting interest against the payout due next summer.
Two or three more years of gold in the ground
meant the payout was safe, and school besides,

maybe a year from now, with money in his hand,

She carried the flour toward the counter,

She came to the counter and the talk stopped,

every one of whom had known her since she was a girl

The smiles were wrong. They fit badly, like borrowed coats.

"I don't mean to be the bearer of bad news," Old Rob began,

"And you might not even remember him.

Nick.

He had been her best friend for years —

and every one of them was theirs —

when they were children.
And a flood came through her now, all of it at once:
the flat rock at the creek with the seat worn smooth,

and swore up and down he hadn't,
the winter he pulled her out of the snow well by the hood

She had a memory like a bear trap; everyone said so.
It had simply never occurred to her until this exact minute
how much of what was in the trap was Nick.

He had been back. Back — in Bronston,
walking streets she walked every day of her life.
How long? A month, the man said. A month.

It burst at her feet,

and hung there, settling on her boots,
finer than any snow.
Rob was saying her name.
She turned and walked out the door with it half-said,

and the empty feeling went the whole way through her,
floor to crown, wind through an open house.

she had always assumed that one day

She stood on the boards until her breath came back.

and every mile of the distance he had crossed

without her ever once knowing he was near.

# Chapter 5 — The Counting House

The gold went across the scale a pan at a time,
and the figures went into the book,
and between the pan and the book sat Donald Grin,
who had spent the last hour turning the one into the other,
and most of his life before that.

The office was one room and one lamp,
a cold walk out from where the brothers slept,
and Donald had never once minded the walk.
A man thought better with the town behind him.
The stove had been going in the corner since dark
and the cold sat in the walls anyway;
the cabin had never held its heat in Donald's memory,
and he had long ago stopped billing it for that.
He worked in his coat.
The ink thickened when the bottle stood too far from the stove,
so it stood in by the scale weights,
where his hand knew the reach to both without looking,
and when it dragged even so
he rolled the pen between his palms and went on.

The desk was laid out as it was always laid out.
Metal on the left, paper on the right.
The take came up the left side in pans,
got weighed, got written, went into the sacks,
and by the time it crossed to the right side of the desk
it wasn't gold anymore, it was figures.
Gold had to be guarded.
Figures sat quiet in a book all winter
and guarded themselves.

James worked at the table behind him, sacking.
He was better at it than the town would have guessed,
tying off each neck in the same two motions,
setting the sacks along the wall in rows,
never a grain spilled that Donald had ever found.
Whatever else there was to say about James,
and Donald had said most of it over the years,
his hands were honest.
It was the rest of him that talked.

The tying stopped.
Donald did not need to turn around to know why.

"Don. Listen to this."

"No," Donald said.

James read it out anyway, which Donald had known he would.
The flyer was New York City,
smoothed flat on the table with both hands,
the paper gone soft at the folds from riding in his coat.
Dancers, it said. Dinner on every street corner.
James read it slowly, giving every word its full wage.

"They dance past midnight, Don. Every night of the week there is."

"Put it away," Donald said. "The gold first."

"It is away." A pause, while it went away in fact.
"Dinner on every corner," James said to the sacks.
"A man wouldn't cook once the rest of his life."
He laughed at that, pleased with it,
a beat past what the joke was worth,
and took up the tying again.

The laugh was back.
It had gone missing for one day, up the mountain,
and Donald had not missed it.
A man who laughed at everything
was a man the town never looked at twice,
and that had its uses.
The trouble was the freight that came with him.
James talked.
He came in every evening loaded with the town's talk
and stood by the stove and unloaded it,
who said what at Rob's, who owed, who ailed,
and a man who carried talk in one direction
could carry it in the other without ever meaning to.
Donald kept that thought where he kept the others like it,
near the top.

For a while there was the stove and the wind
and the small dry sounds of the work.

"Any more talk about Nick?"
James asked it quietly, and he did not look up from the sack.

"Some," Donald said. "It's settling."

"What do you say, when they raise it?"

"That watching that boy fall was the worst moment of my life."
The line came out of him worn smooth as a scale-weight;
he had said it so often around town it ran without him now.
He had built it with some care the first evening,
chosen the words, set them in their order,
and by the tenth telling it cost nothing to say.
He had waited, the first day or two, for it to cost—curiosity,
he supposed, more than conscience—and it hadn't,
and he had stopped waiting.
"Sometimes fewer men return to camp than leave," he said.
"I say that as well.
One of the men at Rob's stove said it back to me this week
and thought it was his own."

"Lost his footing," James said, nodding at the sack.
"It's the country."

"It's the country," Donald said.

The country. Nobody had gone down after a body;
there had been no way down to go,
and the river under that cliff ran fast and black and cold,
and water like that settled its own accounts.

A careful boy, the stove men kept saying. Careful.
Donald had carried tins up a hillside himself at that age.
There was nothing in the work a mule couldn't do
if a mule could hold a trowel.
The town had made a craft of it
because the town had loved the father,
and the boy got the shine off the old coin.

The town would want the story simple, and it wanted it brief.
A town waiting on gold could not afford long grief.
Memories were short up here—the country saw to that—and by
the time the new machinery came up the water
every man at that stove would be thinking of the mountain
in pounds and ounces again, and of the boy not at all.

He finished the pan on the scale, wrote the figure,
and turned his chair to the right-hand side of the desk,
because the metal could wait and the paper could not.

The boat came Thursday.
The sample report was to have gone out on it.
That had been the arithmetic of the week—the boy down off
the mountain with his six tins,
the reading due in Juno by the Thursday boat—and the week
had been handled.
But the boat was still coming,
and something still had to be on it.

He drew a clean sheet to him and squared it.
Before, the books had only to agree with themselves.

"Don." The tying had stopped again.

It was the right question.

the man dreamed through nine hours of a working day,

The desk had been their father's desk.
The brothers had sat in this room counting

*Keep the cabin in good repair.*
The old man's voice still lived

"Duff sent word today," he said. "Under two weeks.

Donald nodded and wrote nothing.

"I'm trying to decide," James said,

Fifteen years in this room.
The wall, the bench, the office steps before that.

He sat a moment with his hand flat on the cover.

Donald put on his coat and buttoned it to the throat.

it froze early, this late in the year,

and this town's endless hope for more of it,
and of the two, only the hope never needed weighing.
