## Hand-sell line (twelve words)

**Clearing Grandma means proving she did every prank but the
frightening one.**

---

## One sentence

On a Toronto island with no cars, where everybody knows which boat
you came in on, Amanda and Andrew Wilson have to prove their new
friend's grandmother didn't stage the prank that stopped being
funny, and the only alibi that can clear her is the secret that
would ruin her, because she has been pranking that little airport,
gleefully and expertly, since before her granddaughter was born.

## Back cover

The Wilsons' address for the next three months doesn't have a road.
It has a ferry — fifteen minutes of diesel smell and lake wind and
gulls yelling over the rail — and on the far side, a world of
narrow lanes where families haul their groceries home in wagons,
every bike is rusty and unlocked, and the loudest thing all day is
a screen door. Blazer thinks it is the greatest place ever built.
Amanda and Andrew figure they can survive it, right up until their
parents volunteer them as junior counselors at the island day camp,
which means a summer of six-year-olds, life jackets, and juice
boxes. Out at the little airport on the west end there's a push to
bring in jets, the island votes on it in seventeen days, and the
date is pinned to the ferry dock's bulletin board where anyone can
count. So when the airport's big jets-are-coming weekend starts
going hilariously wrong — the campaign's lawn signs replanted in a
tidy row marching into the lake, its shiny model runway occupied
overnight by two hundred toy geese — the island laughs until it
aches, and nobody is in a hurry to find out who. Then a prank lands
that isn't a prank. It's cable-tied to the airport fence at first
light, it is not funny at all, and by lunchtime the whole island is
looking at one grandmother: their friend's grandmother, the general
behind the no-jets petition since the sandbag summers, who will not
say where she was that morning. Nobody listens to camp kids. The
twins don't even have phones. What they have is a pair of
walkie-talkies, a notebook full of Andrew's extremely boring dawn
counts, and a printed ferry timetable, which turns out to be the
one thing out here that cannot lie. And what they find, four days
before the vote, on a hook in a shed ten feet from their own back
hedge, is proof that the woman they are trying to clear has been
lying to everybody who loves her for forty years.

## The four quarters

### Q1 — The lane, the board, and the man with the clipboard

The twins meet their new home from the deck of a boat, because
that is how everybody meets it. The ramp clangs down, the gulls
scream, and the Wilsons walk off into a place with lanes instead
of streets and wagons instead of cars, where their rented cottage
turns out to share a hedge with a woman who is already up a ladder
at eight in the morning and wants to know if the dog bites. The
usual rules get set on day one — how far the twins may roam, and
Andrew's running tally of family dinners, which he keeps the way a
lawyer keeps evidence. Then comes the humiliation. Camp, their
mother says brightly, and not as campers: the island day camp
needs junior counselors, she has already said yes, and the twins
spend their first week being outranked by an eleven-year-old
islander who has done this two summers running and hands them the
life-jacket bin. That night the last plane of the evening goes
over, and then there is nothing at all, and the quiet is so total
that Andrew sits up in bed to check it.

The island beats them again at the dock. There's a rack of bright
orange rental bikes and a kiosk that wants an app, and two kids
without phones can only stand there while island kids coast past
on rusty bikes nobody bothers to lock. Dad solves it the way dads
do, logging his own account into Andrew's tablet and forgetting he
ever did. Andrew, consoling himself, asks about the tunnel he read
about, the one that runs under the water to the airport, and
learns two things that stick. It is real, it is open all night,
and it goes to the airport and nowhere else — you come out the far
side inside the fence, among the badges and the doors that beep.
It is the adults' door. It is no use to them at all.

The dock is also where this island keeps its news. A petition
against the jet plan, a stack of campaign flyers with a schedule of
demo-weekend events printed right on them, and a notice with the
date of the vote and the hour the polling table opens, which their
friend from camp reads out loud like a countdown, because the
general behind that petition is her own grandmother, the woman over
the hedge, the one this island has followed before. A pleasant man
in good shoes is working the comment table with a clipboard, and
he gives them each a hot dog and asks what they think about jets,
and writes their answers down, and tells them his name.

Then the demo weekend starts going wrong. On Saturday morning the
campaign's lawn signs have been replanted overnight in a tidy,
purposeful row that walks straight down the beach and into the
lake. On Sunday the gleaming model of the extended runway inside
the marquee is found occupied by two hundred plastic geese from
somebody's craft box, with a small hand-lettered card propped
against it reading PROJECTED TRAFFIC. The island laughs for two
days. The constable comes over on the ferry, looks at the geese,
writes one line in his book, says the word "pranks" in the voice
adults use when they have decided nothing will be done, and goes
home. And their friend, who has not laughed once, says the thing
that turns the summer over. Whoever is doing this had better stop,
because if the campaign ever pins it on our side, we lose. By the
end of the weekend the twins have quit performing outrage about
camp. They have a case, and the case is to find the funniest
person on this island before anybody else does.

### Q2 — Two different people, and the night the water came up

Andrew starts a ledger, because Andrew starts a ledger. Every
incident gets a row: what it was, where it stood, what it was made
out of, who saw it, and what somebody would have had to know to do
it. He also starts getting up in the dark, which he explains as a
time-lapse for Kekoa — the island waking up, one frame every few
minutes, the pocket camera propped at the bike rack — and which
means that by the second week Andrew Wilson is the only person
alive who knows how many orange bikes stand at that rack at five in
the morning, and exactly which six people come off the first boat.

Amanda, meanwhile, is being humiliated by a canoe. She has watched
enough of them to be certain she will be good at it, and she is
not; she is broadside, wet, and slower than a nine-year-old with
one usable arm, and she has to be all of that in front of the
little kids she is supposedly in charge of. Camp gives back
better things. The overnight campout on the lagoon, which happens
at all, the friend explains, because on this island you can hear
the lake at night, and jets are how you stop being able to. The
lighthouse keeper who vanished one winter and gets blamed by
firelight for everything. And, from a canoe at dusk, a clear view
of every way onto this island there is — the club's private dock
with its own little member ferry, a water taxi burbling in on
somebody's phone call, the public boats coming and going on the
timetable, and far off past the fence line, a man walking down into
the mouth of the tunnel like a coin going into a slot.

The third incident is when the summer stops being funny. It lands
on the morning the campaign has inspectors visiting, it is aimed at
something no flyer ever mentioned, and it is mean in a way the
geese were not. Andrew's ledger has the shape of it before anyone
says it out loud: the pranks are getting sloppier and better
informed at the same time, which is a thing that does not happen to
one person. Two hands, he says, and nobody believes him, because
by then the island has decided it only needs one, and hers is the
name on the petition. The kids at camp are less careful than the
grown-ups. A six-year-old confesses at length, with robots. Three
different islanders describe three different strangers to the
twins — a surveyor with a clipboard, a birdwatcher, a man carrying
a delivery — and every one of them mentions, as an afterthought,
that he was reading his phone while he walked, which out here is
like whistling in a library.

Then the wind comes. It blows hard out of the southwest for a
night and the lake comes up the low end of the lane, and at four in
the morning a phone tree that has not changed since 1980 puts the
entire island outside in headlamps, in a chain, passing sandbags
hand to hand while the city burns bright and useless across the
water. The boats run the storm schedule; nothing crosses before
noon. And the grandmother, who is running the chain the way other
people run countries, has her shed standing wide open all morning
because that is where the burlap lives — and Amanda, sent in for
twine, sees what else is in there. Stencils. Coils of garden line.
A crate of plastic geese with nine still in it. Forty years of the
funniest things that have ever happened on this island, hung up
neat on a wall, and one more half-built on the bench under a
cloth. The grandmother is standing in the doorway. She hands
Amanda the twine, and says nothing at all, and goes back to the
chain.

The next afternoon their friend's family gets it in public and to
their faces — that the pranks are hers, that the petition was
always the point. The friend goes white. And Amanda promises, out
loud, in front of all of them, that she and Andrew will prove it
wasn't her. Andrew, who does not like promising things, promises
too, and only afterward, on the walk home past the hedge, do the
two of them work out what they have actually just sworn to do.

### Q3 — The fence, one long day, and the island they come back to

Someone cable-ties a laminated page to the airport fence at first
light. It is a photograph of the approach path with times written
on it and one place circled, and it is printed on the back of a
petition sheet. Nothing is touched. Nobody goes near an aircraft.
It is still the most frightening thing that has happened here in
years, because it is a picture of somebody watching, and by
lunchtime every name on that petition has to answer for it.

Amanda gets there first, standing on the public side of the fence
where the boardwalk runs. Whoever has been pranking this airport
respected that line every single time — the joke never crossed it,
not once, not in forty years — and she is the only person on this
island who can say exactly how she knows. This is somebody
imitating the pranks badly and on purpose, at the precise moment a
scandal sinks the petition. It is a good deduction. It earns them
a pat on the head, a suggestion that they enjoy their summer, and
one hard question they cannot answer: where was she, then, at
first light? Because the grandmother will not say. She was out.
She was alone. She won't discuss it, not to the constable, not to
her own family, and her silence sits in the middle of the island
like a stone.

Then the trip they have been counting down to since week one
arrives, at the worst imaginable moment, and it takes one entire
day — the first boat off the island, ninety minutes each way, home
on the last boat — and the day it takes is a dawn, which in this
book is the only currency there is. Niagara is worth it anyway.
They walk the tunnels cut in behind the sheet of water and stand at
an opening with the falls going past like a moving wall; they hear
about the winters when the river froze thick enough to walk out on
and about the schoolteacher who went over in a barrel in her
sixties and survived it and never made a dollar; they eat far more
ice cream than two people should before a ninety-minute bus, and
Andrew learns this in stages. Neither of them mentions the case
once, which is the exact difference between the twins and the man
they are hunting.

They come back to a different island. While they were gone, in
daylight, in front of the Saturday crowd, a fourth incident was
staged at the hall behind the dock — the hall where the island
votes in four days. Names are coming off the petition. The
grandmother is talking about withdrawing it, and still not talking
about that morning. And their friend has found out, from somebody
else, that Amanda has known about the shed for three days and said
nothing, and the look on her face is worse than being shouted at.
Nobody on this island wants to hear another word from two camp
kids.

### Q4 — Every door but one

It is Andrew's boring numbers that crack it, with Amanda reading
them right. The two hands sort clean once you stop asking who and
start asking what each hand needed. Hers needed a shed and a
lifetime on this island. His needed a boat — every staged incident
lands on a morning the campaign has something happening, and on
the one morning the wind cancelled both the event and the boats,
nothing happened at all. So he comes over the water. And here
Uncle Jacob's sign-in sheets finally go off in Amanda's head like
a flashbulb, because there are four ways onto this island and
three of them write down your name. The tunnel is badges and
doors that beep and it only lets you out inside the fence. The
water taxi bills a phone and its driver keeps a fare log on a
clipboard, and the fare log has this man's name on it, twice, from
early in the summer, before he learned better. The club's ferry
signs its guests in at the gate. That leaves the public boat,
which takes cash and asks nothing and remembers nobody, and which
writes down the one thing he could not avoid leaving behind, which
is the hour. The first boat lands before this island is awake and
carries six regulars whose names everybody knows. On three
mornings this summer it carried seven, and one boy with a camera
propped at the bike rack has all three of them on film.

The escalation says the rest. Each staged incident has been aimed
at a bigger audience at a better moment, and the last place left is
the queue at the polling table on vote morning, which opens at
nine, which means the boat, which means dawn. One dawn. One boat.

They don't sneak. They pitch it — to their parents, to the
islanders, at the dock, out loud, the way this island has organized
itself since a siren brought everybody to the bridge in 1980.
Positions along the lane and the plaza. The walkie-talkies from
Hawaii. The new deckhand, who has been on this route three weeks
and knows the boat far better than he knows the faces, holding the
one piece of paper that says how many walk-ons landed on each
early sailing. And then Amanda does the hardest thing in the book,
which is to walk through the hedge, sit down in the shed, and tell
a seventy-year-old woman that they can prove she didn't do the
frightening thing, and that the proof is every funny thing she has
ever done, and that she has until nine o'clock to decide.

Dawn. Two kids in a canoe on the flat gray water off the club's
docks, because it is the only place on this island where a tablet
can find a signal, and because Amanda has finally stopped fighting
the wakes and started meeting them early, which is not a paddling
trick, it's Coach Chen's voice from three thousand miles away
telling her not to chase the ball, to go where it's going to be.
Andrew, dry in the bow, borrows the guest network for ninety
seconds and reads the one number that matters. There are four
orange bikes at the far rack. There should be five. It is
ten past five in the morning, and there are no tourists on this
island, and every islander's bike is rusty and unlocked and
theirs. Somebody with a phone is already here.

The first boat lands, the ramp clangs, and a pleasant man in good
shoes walks off it into an ordinary island morning that is not
ordinary at all, and is met at the hall door with the roll of
laminated pages still under his arm and a rented bike leaning
against the wall behind him. He is very calm until Andrew says the
time out loud. Everything with an app leaves footprints, and the
only two people on this island who don't have phones are the ones
who followed his.

Then the harder half. At nine o'clock, on the ramp, in front of the
line of people waiting to vote, a woman who has never once been
caught tells the island exactly what she did — the signs, the
geese, the forty years, every one of them, and where the line
always was and why she never crossed it. Some of them laugh. Some
of them don't. Her granddaughter finds out standing right there,
with everyone else, which is Amanda's fault and Amanda says so, out
loud, before anybody can thank her for anything. Then the island
votes, and the twins are close enough to hear the number when it's
read.

There is a feast in the lane afterward, because there is always a
feast. Dad tries to take a wagon home. The last plane goes over at
some point in the evening, and after it there is nothing but the
lake, and everyone on the lane stops talking at the same time
without deciding to.

## Hauge frame

**Outer goal (the finish line a reader can picture).** Their
friend's grandmother's name cleared, in public, before the island
votes.

**Inner journey.** Explicitly NONE this book — the millimeter is
"none," locked in `plots/book-03-toronto.md` (author, 2026-07-29).
The pressure point that would move it, if the author ever wants a
millimeter here, is Amanda learning that protecting somebody and
being honest with them are not the same act; this build presses on
it and deliberately does not convert it. Practice sequels carry
reflection without moving the needle.
