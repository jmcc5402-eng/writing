## THE MIDDLE OF THE BOOK (ch6–9) — card A5 priority

### [CHECK 3] Koban culture — how a police box handles minor vandalism

**Verified.** The koban system works exactly the way the outline needs,
and there is a real legal mechanism that makes the officer's shrug
truthful rather than lazy:

- Kobans are small staffed neighborhood police boxes; officers take
  walk-in reports of minor crime, record them, give directions, do
  bicycle foot patrols, and act as community liaisons. Minor incidents
  are handled as after-the-fact written reports.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dban;
  https://japanhandbook.com/koban-and-police-box-101-how-to-report-incidents-in-japan/;
  https://japan-dev.com/blog/what-is-koban-in-japan)
- **The load-bearing fact:** graffiti is prosecuted as property damage
  under Penal Code Article 261, and Article 264 makes Article 261 a
  *shinkokuzai* — an offense prosecuted **only upon the victim's
  formal complaint**.
  (https://www.japaneselawtranslation.go.jp/en/laws/view/3581;
  https://sherloc.unodc.org/cld/en//legislation/jpn/penal_code/part_ii_-_chapter_xl/articles_260-261/)
  If no stall owner files a damage report, the officer genuinely
  *cannot* pursue it — he can only log it. And in this community, no
  one wants to file, because filing makes the shame official (see
  CHECK 4). "Too small to chase" is therefore not police indifference;
  it is the market itself declining to make it a case. That is a
  better, truer beat than a shrug — the officer can even say so:
  "If a shop owner files, I can act. Nobody will file."
- **Demeanor, kid-accurate:** polite, patient, unhurried; a small desk,
  a bicycle outside, a hand-drawn area map on the wall; lost umbrellas
  in a rack; the officer writes in a notebook. Kobans famously handle
  lost property and small kindnesses — an officer being gentle with
  three kids is fully in character.
  (https://kawaraban.jp/en/living-en/50657.html)
- **One adjust:** ch3 has the officer "show them the incident log."
  An officer would not hand official records to children. Have him
  read the dates aloud from his own memo pad, or confirm the twins'
  dates against it — the ally-held-proof beat survives intact, and in
  ch9/ch12 the officer can state the dates himself, which is stronger.

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep, with the two adjustments** — ground the
"won't chase" in the no-complaint mechanism (one line of dialogue), and
make the log something the officer reads from, not hands over.

### [CHECK 4] Graffiti's shock-weight — "barely a crime, an earthquake"

**Verified — the asymmetry is truthful, with one refinement.**

- Graffiti is rare in Japan; commentators consistently attribute this
  to deep norms of respect for shared/public property and cleanliness.
  A painted mark on a shop shutter is genuinely startling in a way it
  would not be in most Western cities.
  (https://upmag.com/japanese-graffiti/;
  https://www.yokogaomag.com/editorial/graffiti-culture-japan)
- On paper the law is harsh — up to 3 years or a ¥300k fine, and courts
  read "damage" broadly (merely spoiling appearance counts).
  (https://japancompliance.com/more-than-an-eyesore-how-graffiti-on-a-public-toilet-redefined-property-crime-in-japan/)
  So the asymmetry is precisely: *severe in principle, unpursued in
  practice without a complaint* (CHECK 3), *devastating socially*.
- The social weight runs through two concepts the book never needs to
  name: **meiwaku** (causing trouble/burden to others — drilled
  into children from early childhood) and **sekentei** (the
  family's/shop's standing in others' eyes). A mark on the row is not
  one owner's problem; it is the whole row's face. A suspected child
  shames his family's stall, which is why the friend scrubbing mark #0
  to "protect the stall's name" is *exactly* what a market kid raised
  on meiwaku would do — his silence in ch6 is culturally load-bearing,
  not just plot convenience. His family "sitting out the festival"
  (ch9) is likewise a real-shaped response: withdrawing so as not to
  burden the group.
  (https://kokoro-jp.com/columns/2117/;
  https://takeshimori.com/blog/what-is-meiwaku;
  https://jobsinjapan.com/living-in-japan-guide/why-meiwaku-helps-japanese-society-work-so-smoothly/)

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep.** The premise's central asymmetry is
factual. Let meiwaku/sekentei drive behavior on the page without
lecturing the reader about the words (a one-line gloss from the friend
— "it's not the paint, it's what people think of us now" — carries it).

### [CHECK 9, partial] The association and the camera vote (ch7, ch12)

**Verified as a body; the vote's meaning needs one reframe.**

- Shotengai merchant associations are real and often formally
  incorporated (shinko kumiai — promotion associations); they maintain
  the arcade, the lanterns, the festival, and make collective decisions
  at member meetings. A market row voting as a body is authentic.
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dtengai;
  https://www.gotokyo.org/en/see-and-do/culture/local-market/index.html)
- **Refinement — cameras are not alien in Tokyo.** Security cameras
  (bohan kamera) are widespread in Tokyo shopping districts and are
  routinely *subsidized* by ward and metropolitan government; many
  shotengai install them proudly.
  (https://japantoday.com/category/crime/16-security-cameras-installed-on-akihabara-streets;
  https://geinokai.jp/blog/2024/12/16/tokyo-surveillance-cameras/)
  So the elders' grief cannot plausibly be "cameras are newfangled."
  What rings true: **what installing them says about the row** — that
  the market now suspects its own; that the lens would point at their
  neighbors' children; that trust, the row's actual operating system,
  has failed. "The market we know is over" survives — it just means
  *we have become a place that watches itself*, not *we hate machines*.
  This also sharpens ch12: the vote flips not because cameras are bad
  but because the mystery's answer restores the trust the cameras were
  meant to replace.

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: adjust** the vote's emotional logic as above;
keep the vote itself (mechanically realistic, including that a ward
subsidy application could be mentioned as already half-filled-out —
a nice "this is really happening" clock-tightener for ch7).

## SETTING & ARRIVAL (ch1–3, ch7 set piece)

### [CHECK 1] Market naming — Tsukiji Outer Market vs Toyosu

**The facts:** Tsukiji's *inner* wholesale market (and the tuna
auction) moved to Toyosu in October 2018. The **Tsukiji Outer Market**
never moved: 400+ shops and stalls, many family-run, still
operating. Toyosu is the modern wholesale complex with the auction;
Tsukiji Outer is the stall rows.
(https://www.gotokyo.org/en/spot/65/index.html;
https://tokyocheapo.com/entertainment/sightseeing/toyosu-fish-market-what-to-know/)

**The problem for the book:** the real Tsukiji Outer Market is today a
major international tourist attraction — mobbed daily. A painted mark
there would be absorbed by crowds and cameras within hours, and a
tight-knit "everyone knows everyone" row where one mark is an
earthquake reads false at that address. The story the outline tells —
family stalls, a renovation, an association that all knows one kid by
sight — lives at the scale of a *neighborhood* fish-and-food shotengai
(real Tokyo models exist: old-school covered food streets with
fishmongers, where locals shop daily and tourists are rare).

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: fictionalize the market; keep Toyosu real.**
- Invent the market: a small covered fish-and-food market street,
  [TK market name], in an unnamed/lightly named shitamachi-flavored
  Tokyo neighborhood — Tsukiji-inspired texture (pinned faded photo,
  turret-cart deliveries, rubber boots, ice trucks) at shotengai scale.
  This clears the series brands gate, dodges the tourist-crowd realism
  problem, and frees the renovation/mural backstory from real history.
- Keep **Toyosu** by name for the ch7 auction — it is a public
  institution, safely nameable, and grounds the wow set piece.
- One guardrail: never call the row a "shutter street" —
  (shatta-dori) specifically means a *dying* street of permanently
  closed shutters (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dtengai).
  This market is alive; its shutters are down only at dawn and dusk.

### [CHECK 2] Shutter art — is the lost row-length mural true to life?

**Verified — strong real precedent.**

- Shutter art (*shatta ato*) is an established genre: shop
  shutters as canvas, usually community/revitalization projects, often
  painted by local artists, students, and volunteers.
  (https://bigart.co.jp/faq/;
  https://arumatie.co.jp/shutter-art/)
- **The exact shape of the book's mural exists:** the Nakamise shopping
  street in Asakusa has shutter paintings conceived as *emaki* — picture
  scrolls — running scene by scene along the row, painted in 1992 by
  artists under the direction of painter Ikuo Hirayama.
  (https://xperience-japan.com/asakusa-picture-scroll-of-shutter-at-nakamise-street/;
  https://en.japantravel.com/tokyo/asakusa-shutter-art/70749)
  A single local amateur (the grandmother) painting a smaller row is a
  modest, plausible scale-down of a real pattern.
- **Erased in a renovation:** true to life. Shutter art is painted on
  the physical roll-shutter; when shutters are replaced or repainted in
  a refurbishment, the art simply goes. New plain-gray shutters after
  "the renovation" (ch1) is exactly how such murals die.
- Grid-and-panel reproduction of murals (Uncle Jacob's ch8 assist) is
  standard muralist practice worldwide; no Japan-specific issue.
- **The kana signature (see also CHECK 8):** Japanese paintings are
  traditionally signed, often with a small signature and/or seal
  (rakkan) in a corner. An amateur grandmother signing each panel with
  a single small hiragana — the first syllable of her given name — is
  plausible **as her personal mark**, and should be framed that way,
  not as "a Japanese tradition." Bonus authenticity: women of older
  generations very often had kana-only given names (katakana was the
  default for girls' names from Meiji through WWII; hiragana in some
  regions) — so a grandmother with a hiragana name whose mark IS a
  hiragana is quietly, factually right.
  (https://japantoday.com/category/features/why-elderly-japanese-women-have-names-in-katakana)
  UNCERTAIN/aging note: a kana-only *given name* fits a woman born
  before ~1950; the painter's grandmother could be born 1950s–60s, so
  either give her a kana name as a slightly old-fashioned touch (fine)
  or just make the mark her chosen signature quirk (also fine).

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep** — mural, renovation erasure, and
corner mark all check out. One craft adjustment: the original mural was
almost certainly brush-and-enamel, not spray. If the little painter
uses the grandmother's own brushes and leftover tins with the traced
stencils, the dawn work is quieter (spray cans hiss and reek), the
"cap" Andrew rescues from the grate can be a paint-tin lid or brush cap
that was *hers* (which is why the kid freezes — the object matters more
than being caught), and it sidesteps the question of a small child
buying spray paint. Author's call; flagged below.

### [CHECK 5] Toyosu tuna auction — visitor logistics (ch7)

**Verified, current as of 2025–26 guides:**

- Auctions run roughly 5:30–6:30 a.m.; market closed Sundays,
  Wednesdays, and holidays — check the story's weekday.
- Two ways to watch: (a) the **free 2nd-floor observation corridor**,
  big windows above the floor, open from about 5:00 a.m., no booking;
  (b) the **lower observation deck**, closer to floor level, ~100–120
  people, allocated by **monthly online lottery** — apply about a month
  ahead. No public floor access, period; everyone is behind glass.
  (https://tokyocheapo.com/entertainment/sightseeing/toyosu-fish-market-what-to-know/;
  https://whereandwander.com/toyosu-fish-market-a-secret-guide-to-the-tuna-auction/;
  https://www.timeout.com/tokyo/news/the-tuna-auction-observation-deck-at-toyosu-market-is-now-open-to-public-012119)
- Sensory kit for the scene: the floor is kept refrigerated (frost on
  the fish, breath fogging), rows of frozen tuna laid out like felled
  logs, hand bells rung to open bidding, auctioneers' rapid chant,
  buyers' finger price-signs (*teyari*), hooked flashlights checking
  tail cuts, turret trucks whining past. Whole bluefin genuinely run
  Andrew-sized and beyond (fish over 200 kg are routine).

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep**, written from behind glass. Either
tier works; the lottery deck is closer and louder. If the parents
"happened to win" the lottery, note it took an application weeks ago —
that is either a quiet competence beat or, played neutrally, just
Dad-planned-ahead. Do not put the twins on the auction floor; kids
know how to spot a faked access beat, and the glass costs the scene
nothing.

### [CHECK 6] Tokyo drone rules — the one-sentence version

**Verified.** Central Tokyo is almost entirely classified as a Densely
Inhabited District (DID) under the Civil Aeronautics Act: any drone of
100 g or more needs registration and government permission to fly
there, applied for well in advance — and Tokyo's metropolitan parks ban
drones outright regardless of weight.
(https://www.propelrc.com/drone-laws-in-tokyo/;
https://drone-laws.com/drone-laws-in-tokyo/)

Kid-accurate line for ch1 (shape, not prose): *the entire city counts
as a no-fly zone unless the government stamps your paperwork — and even
the parks ban drones, including the tiny ones.* That is accurate and
sufficient; it also honestly reseeds T11 (drones are a rules problem,
not a money problem).

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep.**

### [CHECK 7] Blazer's arrival — pet import reality

**Verified, with a gift for continuity:** Japan designates exactly six
regions as rabies-free for animal import — Iceland, Australia, New
Zealand, Fiji, Guam, and **Hawaii**.
(https://www.maff.go.jp/aqs/english/animal/dog/import-other.html;
https://resources.realestate.co.jp/living/bringing-your-pet-dog-or-cat-to-japan-steps-and-requirements/)

- **From Hawaii (designated region):** no rabies shots or 180-day
  antibody wait needed — microchip, advance notification to Animal
  Quarantine Service ~40 days before arrival, health certificate, and
  a short on-arrival inspection (typically hours, not weeks) —
  *provided the dog has been continuously in Hawaii for at least 180
  days (or since birth) immediately before export.*
- **From the U.S. mainland (non-designated):** microchip, two rabies
  vaccinations, FAVN blood test, then a **180-day wait from the blood
  draw** before entry — roughly 7–8 months of lead time — or the dog
  sits out the balance in a quarantine facility on arrival.
  (https://www.aphis.usda.gov/print/pdf/node/3658;
  https://japanhandbook.com/bringing-a-pet-to-japan-import-regulations-quarantine/)

**Continuity risk, honest flag:** the clean path runs through the
Book 1 Maui stay. If Blazer had been in Hawaii 180+ days before the
Tokyo flight, the arrival line writes itself. If Book 1's stay was
shorter, Blazer's paperwork must have started on the mainland the
better part of a year before Tokyo — doable, but it means the parents
were preparing for Japan long before they "got the assignment," which
is either a problem or (careful) a series-level breadcrumb. The wink
budget is spent (waiver: 1), so if the author doesn't want an
accidental clue, the safe line is the Hawaii-route one.

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: adjust** — author must establish (a) how long
the family was on Maui, or (b) that Blazer's paperwork "started months
before Dad even said the word Tokyo," consciously choosing whether
that reads as a clue. One line either way; never a scene. [TK: Maui
stay length — check Book 1 canon before drafting ch1.]

### [CHECK 8] Amanda's kana — right first step? Corner-syllable workable?

**Verified — kana is exactly the right first step.**

- Market and food signage runs heavily on kana: fish names appear
  mostly in hiragana/katakana (maguro, ebi, tamago, samon), because
  the kanji for fish species are obscure even for many adults;
  handwritten price cards use kana plus simple numerals.
  (https://kanapow.com/how-to-read-a-sushi-menu;
  https://www.sljfaq.org/afaq/names-for-people.html)
- Timeline check: motivated kids learn hiragana in days-to-weeks;
  Amanda getting functional (sounding out short words, recognizing
  individual characters) inside the book's three-week clock is
  realistic — full sign-reading is not, and the outline already plays
  it that way (her code hunt "gets her exactly nowhere" except the
  corner syllable). Good.
- The corner-syllable clue is workable and fair: recognizing ONE
  repeated hiragana is precisely what a three-week learner can do, and
  matching it to the signature in the faded photo (ch8) then saying it
  aloud (ch11) requires nothing beyond that. Craft note: pick a
  visually distinctive hiragana for [TK grandmother's mark] — e.g.,
  something loopy a beginner would remember — and note that handwritten
  /brush kana can look different from workbook kana; Amanda almost
  missing it for that reason is a free, authentic beat.
- Tiny authenticity upgrade: tamago at the market — Tsukiji's outer
  market is famous for tamagoyaki (rolled omelet) stands; a slice of
  tamago on a stick is THE kid street food there. Ch7's "slippery slice
  of tamago" checks out.

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep.** [TK the mark's actual hiragana — tie
to the grandmother's name when named.]

## REPRESENTATION NOTES ([CHECK 9] and sweep)

**The festival.** Anchor it in the real pattern: Tokyo markets have
guardian shrines, and shrine festivals (matsuri) are the year's
biggest community day. Real local precedent: Namiyoke Inari Shrine
beside Tsukiji — the "wave-repelling" shrine — holds the Tsukiji Shishi
Matsuri (lion-head festival), and its grounds hold memorial mounds
honoring sushi, shrimp, and eggs traded at the market — a wonderfully
kid-friendly, deeply local detail worth echoing in the fictional
market's shrine.
(https://www.gotokyo.org/en/spot/ev051/index.html;
https://assets.atlasobscura.com/places/namiyoke-inari-shrine)
Cautions: (a) a matsuri is a religious event, not set dressing — keep
the association's camera vote at the association meeting ON festival
day, not inside the shrine ritual; (b) skip postcard shorthand
(cherry blossoms, geisha, ninja); build the festival from mikoshi or
lion heads, happi coats, paper lanterns the row hangs itself, yatai
food stalls, and the market's own pride; (c) if the story sits in
June, Tokyo's rainy season (tsuyu, ~June–mid-July) makes the rainy
dawns statistically honest — worth aligning. [TK: season/month —
affects school schedule and rain plausibility.]

**Family stall life.** Multi-generational family shops are the
shotengai's soul; a kid helping at the stall before school and on
weekends is authentic. Keep the help informal (carrying, calling
prices, minding the till with a parent) — not a child running the
stall alone.

**The friend and the little painter.** Two traps to avoid: the
"inscrutable quiet Japanese child" flattening, and Japanese characters
existing as props for visiting American kids' cleverness. The outline
already defends against both — the friend addresses the association
"as one of their own" (ch12), and the resolution runs through the
association's own vote — protect those beats in drafting. Give the
little painter specific wants and humor once trust lands; the silence
before that is grief and fear of meiwaku, not national character. The
late-grandmother material is handled with appropriate gentleness; the
one-line rain-ritual backstory (ruled) is the right size.

**The koban officer.** Kind, constrained, procedural — accurate and
respectful. Avoid comic-bumbling-cop shading; his constraint is legal
(CHECK 3), not incompetence.

**Gags.** The toilet gag laughs at Andrew, not Japan — safe, and
genuinely universal kid-traveler experience; keep every touch aimed at
his awe/defeat. The wasabi beat bites Dad's overconfidence, never the
cuisine — as the outline already specifies. Both pass.

**Language — the biggest unaddressed realism gap.** Nothing in the
premise or outline says how the twins communicate. An association
meeting happens in Japanese; koban conversations happen in Japanese.
Workable, honest solutions: the friend speaks decent English ([TK
reason — returnee year abroad? English-crazy? author's call]); Amanda's
kana is explicitly NOT conversation; the ch12 presentation is
deliberately visual (marks laid over the photo — it plays without
words) with the friend voicing it. Recommend deciding this before ch2
is drafted, and letting the twins be visibly language-limited — it's
authentic, it's humbling in a good way, and it makes the friend
structurally indispensable rather than decorative.

**Sensitivity read.** Before publication, a reader who grew up in (or
has family in) a Tokyo shitamachi/shotengai merchant community would
add real value — specifically on the association meeting scenes, the
accusation dynamics (ch6, ch9), and the family's festival withdrawal.
Those are the scenes where community-shame texture must ring true to
people who have lived it.
