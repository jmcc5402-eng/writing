## SLOT 1 — [TK friend's name/stall type] (boy, 11–12, born ~2014)

Era note, load-bearing: names checked against the Meiji Yasuda annual
rankings for his birth cohort — 2014's top boys' name-kanji were Ren,
Hiroto/Haruto, Hinata
(https://japantoday.com/category/national/ren-most-popular-name-for-baby-boys-in-2014-rin-for-girls),
and Haruto has led the reading rankings for over a decade
(https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h02237/).

### A (RECOMMENDED) — Kenta Hamada, fresh-fish stall
- Twins call him **Kenta**; market aunties "Ken-chan"; the koban
  officer "Hamada-kun" — a free authenticity micro-beat (Andrew
  baffled that one kid has three names).
- Stall: the family sengyoten (fresh-fish stall). Texture: Kenta's
  dawn job is raking the crushed ice flat before the fish go out —
  his hands are always cold when they shake.
- Why this works: fishmonger families are genuinely up pre-dawn
  (Toyosu stock runs), which puts Kenta near the mark sites at dawn
  with zero contrivance — the false accusation (Q2) builds itself.
  And mark #0, the one he scrubbed, was on HIS family's shutter.
  KEN-tah is effortless in romaji. Slightly retro for his cohort
  (peak 1990s–2000s) but entirely plausible.
- Contrarian comp: "Kenta" is Japan's everyday nickname for KFC — a
  food-stall boy named like fried chicken; Kenta Maeda is a
  well-known MLB pitcher; Hamada = Masatoshi Hamada, one of Japan's
  most famous comedians; and Kenta/Kekoa share the "Ke-" opening —
  Kekoa is name-checked in ch1 via the drone thread (T11).

### B — Sota Isobe, family tamagoyaki stand
- Twins call him **Sota** (SOH-tah). Top-5 name throughout the 2010s.
- Stall: tamagoyaki (rolled-omelet) stand — the classic fish-market
  kid food (research pass, CHECK 8). Texture: the copper pans and the
  hypnotic roll-flip-roll rhythm; sweet steam in his hair.
- Why this works: hard-wires the food beat — the ch7 "slippery slice
  of tamago" can be measured against HIS family's, which he insists
  is better than anything at Toyosu; a food-stall kid sells to kids,
  instant peer credibility.
- Contrarian comp: Sota Fujii, the shogi megastar, is Japan's most
  famous living Sota; "Souta" is the male lead of Shinkai's *Suzume*
  (2022, big US release); Isobe reads faintly punny to Japanese
  readers (isobe-yaki is a mochi-and-nori snack).

### C — Haruto Kihara, katsuobushi / dried-goods stall
- Twins call him **Haruto** (HAH-roo-toh). Statistically the truest
  pick — effectively the #1 name of his cohort (sources above).
- Stall: katsuobushi (smoked bonito) and dried goods. Texture: the
  shaver's whirr and curls that melt on the tongue; the smoke smell
  lives permanently in his school blazer. Bonus: a shop cat who
  guards the bonito — a free recurring bit.
- Why this works: the most sensorily distinctive stall (smell as
  signature), and the most era-accurate name on the slate.
- Contrarian comp: Haruto of K-pop group TREASURE; sheer ubiquity
  makes it the "Liam" of his generation — reads generic; three
  syllables is the longest lift for US readers; anime-savvy readers
  may flash on the villainous Kihara clan (*A Certain Magical
  Index*).

## SLOT 2 — [TK the little painter's name] (7–9, grandchild of the
## late muralist; different family from the friend)

Family-name note: simplest clean geometry is the paternal line — the
painter carries the grandmother's surname, so the ch8 faded-photo
caption and the family connection read in one step. Proposed family
surname (pairs with all Slot 3 options): **Ogawa** ("little stream" —
quiet rain-ritual resonance; common, no dominant famous bearer for US
kids). Alternate if disliked: Shimizu ("clear water" — collides
mildly with the construction giant and the soccer club). Age note:
8–9 is more plausible than 7 for tracing stencils and brush-painting
panels alone at dawn.

### A (RECOMMENDED) — Rin Ogawa (girl, 8)
- Era-accurate to the millimeter: Rin was the #1 girls' name-kanji of
  2014 and has stayed top-tier through her 2018 cohort
  (https://japantoday.com/category/national/ren-most-popular-name-for-baby-boys-in-2014-rin-for-girls;
  https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h02237/). One syllable — the
  easiest name in the book for US readers.
- One line: answers in single words until trust lands, then won't
  stop narrating every color Grandma ever mixed.
- Contrarian comp: heavily anime-coded — Kagamine Rin (Vocaloid),
  Rin Nohara (*Naruto*), Rin Tohsaka (*Fate*); savvy readers may
  clock it as the "default anime girl" name.

### B — Sora Ogawa (boy, 8)
- One line: a sky-name for the kid whose grandmother only painted
  when it rained — the rain in her ritual literally comes from his
  name.
- Contrarian comp: Sora is the protagonist of *Kingdom Hearts* — one
  of the most famous game characters US 8–12s know; the name is also
  used for girls, which may wobble for readers who've met one.

### C — Hina Ogawa (girl, 7)
- One line: the smallest person in the market, named like the
  festival dolls, carrying the market's biggest secret.
- Contrarian comp: one vowel away from Hinata — protagonist of
  *Haikyu!!* and a beloved *Naruto* character — and the
  doll-festival association can read decorative; at 7 she also
  strains the "paints panels alone at dawn" plausibility hardest.

## SLOT 3 — The grandmother: name + hiragana signature mark

Era facts, load-bearing (per the research pass, CHECK 2/8): kana-only
given names were the norm for girls from Meiji through WWII —
katakana as the default script, hiragana in some regions — so a
kana-only registered name fits a woman born before ~1950
(https://japantoday.com/category/features/why-elderly-japanese-women-have-names-in-katakana).
Born 1950s–60s, she'd more likely carry a kanji name, and the
hiragana mark becomes her chosen painter's quirk. Both paths were
blessed by the research note; the options below span them. Timeline
sanity: a grandmother born 1946–58 with an 8-year-old grandchild is
comfortably plausible. In every option the mark is the first syllable
of her given name, painted small in one corner of each panel.

### A — Yukie Ogawa, born ~1949 — mark: ゆ (yu)
- Registered hiragana given name — the regional-hiragana variant of
  the pre-1950 kana-name pattern (source above); slightly
  old-fashioned even for her cohort, which is exactly the flavor.
- Why ゆ reads distinctively: a single vertical stroke through a
  curled open loop — Amanda's mnemonic writes itself: "the little
  fish on a hook." A fish-shaped signature in a fish market is the
  kind of detail 8–12s keep.
- Contrarian comp: Yuki/Yukie is anime-ubiquitous (*Fruits Basket*,
  *Vampire Knight*) plus actress Yukie Nakama; and a Yu- mark means
  any future Yu- named kid in the market reads as a suspect —
  a standing constraint on later casting.

### B (RECOMMENDED) — Fumi Ogawa, born ~1946 — mark: ふ (fu)
- The era fact used at full strength: registered in **katakana**
  (フミ) — the documented default for girls of her generation
  (https://japantoday.com/category/features/why-elderly-japanese-women-have-names-in-katakana)
  — but she signed her paintings in **hiragana ふ**, "because
  hiragana is the brush's script." Era-accurate AND a chosen quirk;
  one line of the friend's dialogue can carry the whole nuance.
- Why ふ reads distinctively: arguably the most visually distinctive
  hiragana there is — and in brush it famously breaks into four
  floating strokes that barely touch. That powers the exact beat
  CHECK 8 asked for: workbook-ふ and brush-ふ look different, so
  Amanda almost misses it — then the faded photo's signature sits
  beside the mark and it locks. Ch11 read-aloud: "fu." Clean.
- Bonus resonance: fumi means "letter, message" — the mural was
  a message, and the S06 lesson is "ask who it's for."
- Contrarian comp: US kids may misread it "FYOO-mee" or hear
  "fumes" (mitigate with one phonetic beat: "FOO-mee"); and the
  scattered-strokes property cuts both ways — a skeptical reader may
  wonder how a three-week learner clocked it at all (the photo
  match is the answer; keep them adjacent on the page).

### C — Nobuko Ogawa, born ~1957 — mark: の (no)
- Ordinary kanji name, right for a later birth year; the hiragana
  mark is purely her painter's quirk. の is the character every
  beginner learns first — a single confident spiral, genuinely the
  easiest brush form for Amanda to recognize.
- Contrarian comp: the craft reason not to pick it — の is the
  possessive particle plastered on every handwritten price card in
  the market ("today's tuna"), so Amanda's repeated-hiragana hunt
  would drown in false positives and the clue logic frays; also
  "Nobu" is a globally famous restaurant brand.

## SLOT 4 — [TK market name] (fictional neighborhood shotengai)

Collision standard (per CHECK 1 ruling): must sound like a real
shotengai without being one US readers or tourists could map. Famous
real names checked and avoided: Ameyoko, Nakamise, Togoshi Ginza,
Yanaka Ginza, Sunamachi Ginza, Musashi-Koyama
(https://blog.japanwondertravel.com/shopping-street-shotengai-in-tokyo-20906;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dtengai). Note: the obvious
"fish-town" names are OFF the slate deliberately — Uomachi is
Uomachi Gintengai in Kokura, Japan's first covered arcade, a real
landmark (https://www.kitakyu-air.jp/travel/en/uomachi-gintengai),
and "Sakanamachi" is the Nihonkai Sakanamachi market hall in Fukui
(https://enjoy.pref.fukui.lg.jp/en/spot/spot-62/).

### A (RECOMMENDED) — Mutsumi-dori Shotengai
- Reading: moo-tsoo-mee-DOH-ree. Meaning: mutsumi = harmony,
  getting along; "Harmony Street shopping arcade."
- Why this works: follows the warm-abstract-name pattern real local
  shotengai actually use; no famous bearer (searched — nothing
  notable surfaces for a Mutsumi-dori arcade:
  https://www.gotokyo.org/en/story/guide/neighborhood-shopping-streets/index.html).
  And it's quietly load-bearing: a street named Harmony voting to
  surveil its own children is the book's whole ache — the friend can
  gloss the name once, in ch12, right before the vote flips.
- Contrarian comp: Mutsumi is also a common female given name (e.g.,
  Mutsumi Otohime, *Love Hina*), and once glossed, "Harmony Street"
  risks reading a shade on-the-nose; "tsu" is the slate's one
  trickier syllable (though the book already teaches it via Toyosu
  and tsuyu).

### B — Isaba Yokocho
- Reading: ee-SAH-bah yoh-koh-choh. Meaning: isaba is a real
  Edo-period word for the fish market / fish trade
  (https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%BA%94%E5%8D%81%E9%9B%86) —
  "Fish-Market Alley," in a word so old it flatters the row's age.
- Why this works: deepest-cut authenticity on the slate; hands the
  market free lore (the friend's granddad: "old word — older than
  the arcade roof") that supports the mural-legacy theme.
- Contrarian comp: obscure enough that even many Japanese readers
  need the gloss — can read as research showing off; Hachinohe in
  Tohoku brands its morning-market circuit "Isaba"
  (https://visithachinohe.com/en/stories/tatehanaganpeki-asaichi/),
  a regional (not famous) overlap; and US kids may hear "is-ABBA."

### C — Uroko-dori Shotengai
- Reading: oo-ROH-koh-DOH-ree. Meaning: uroko = fish scales —
  "Fish-scale Street," named for the scale-pattern pressed into the
  arcade's old awning glass (a detail Amanda would sketch on page
  one).
- Why this works: concrete, visual, kid-sticky, and unmistakably a
  fish street without borrowing any real market's name; no famous
  arcade bearer found.
- Contrarian comp: translated aloud, "Scale Street" invites "eww"
  jokes from exactly the readers it's aimed at; mild echo of Kobe's
  tourist-famous Uroko House for well-traveled families.
