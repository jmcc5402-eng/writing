## Part 1 — Seven story-usable aspects of the Toronto Islands

### 1. An accident of weather: the islands used to be a peninsula

The islands are a sandbar eroded off the Scarborough Bluffs and carried
west by storms; until 1858 they were a curving peninsula sheltering
Toronto's harbour. The storm of April 13, 1858 tore the sandbar open at
the east end, and the city kept the new channel (the Eastern Gap) instead
of repairing it — the islands were born by breach
(https://www.heritagetrust.on.ca/plaques/toronto-island,
https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/toronto-islands).
Long before that, the Michi Saagiig (Mississaugas) knew the place as
Mnisiing, "on the islands" — a place of healing, ceremony, birth and
burial, where the sick were brought to recuperate
(https://nowtoronto.com/news/how-torontonians-from-all-walks-of-life-are-shaping-our-beloved-island-park/,
https://mncfn.ca/the-toronto-purchase-treaty-no-13-1805/).

**Story use:** a land made of moving sand that the lake can reshape at
will is quiet thematic ground under a plot about who gets to decide the
island's future. A camp counselor or the friend's grandmother can carry
the Mnisiing history in one respectful beat (see Flags).

**Sensory:** fine pale sand that squeaks underfoot on the lake side, and
the two-water feel of the place — choppy open lake south, glassy harbour
north (inference from geography).

### 2. The ferry is the island's clock

Three city routes leave the Jack Layton Ferry Terminal at the foot of Bay
Street — Ward's Island, Centre Island, Hanlan's Point — a roughly
15-minute crossing; there is no bridge or public road, so the boat (or a
water taxi) is the only way over. Summer boats run as often as every
15–30 minutes; Ward's is the year-round workhorse route the residents
live by
(https://www.toronto.ca/explore-enjoy/toronto-island-ferries/ferry-routes-schedules/,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toronto_Island_ferries,
https://toronto-islands.ca/ferry-schedule-info/). Ferries carry bikes,
wagons, strollers, dogs.

**Story use:** the Q4 hinge. A printed timetable is a perfectly
kid-readable, fair-play clue surface — anyone can check when the first
morning boat lands, and the fence incident NEEDED that boat. Islanders
schedule their whole lives around departures, so "which boat were you
on?" is a natural island question, not detective talk.

**Sensory:** diesel-and-lakewater smell, gull racket, the steel boarding
ramp clanging down, engine shudder in the deck underfoot (inference from
standard ferry operation).

### 3. The car-free world: wagons, rusty bikes, and lanes

Ward's and Algonquin Islands hold roughly 260 homes and about 650
residents with no private cars — often cited as North America's largest
urban car-free community; only a handful of service and emergency
vehicles exist
(https://dailypassport.com/toronto-islands-north-americas-largest-urban-car-free-community/,
https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/wards-and-algonquin-islands-toronto-harbour-ontario).
Residents haul groceries from the ferry dock by wagon and bike; the
cottages sit on narrow, treed lanes with gardens instead of driveways,
and Algonquin has a spot where islanders leave free stuff for neighbours
(https://www.nomadotravel.app/en/attractions/wards-island).

**Story use:** the premise's wagon-and-ferry world is real and exact.
The status inversion writes itself: the twins' city instincts (apps,
rideshares) are useless; an island kid's rusty, unlocked bike is the
badge of belonging the app-locked orange rentals can never be.

**Sensory:** wagon wheels rattling over lane pavement, bike bells and
screen doors instead of traffic — and under it, only boats, birds, and
the airport's planes (inference consistent with the car-free sources).

### 4. A community that fights: the eviction wars and the sandbag summers

The residential islands survived because they refused to disappear.
Metro Toronto bulldozed most island homes for parkland through the
1950s–60s; the holdouts on Ward's and Algonquin fought on, and on July
28, 1980, when the sheriff came with eviction writs, a siren and a phone
chain summoned residents and supporters to face him at the Algonquin
Island bridge — he backed down
(https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/toronto-feature-toronto-islands).
In 1993 Ontario's Toronto Islands Residential Community Stewardship Act
let islanders buy 99-year land leases from a trust
(https://www.neighbourhoodguide.com/toronto/downtown/toronto-islands/).
The community mobilized again in the flood years: record Lake Ontario
levels in 2017 closed the park for nearly three months (45,000+
sandbags, ~800 residents affected), and 2019 topped the record by ~10 cm
(24,000 sandbags, 30 pumps)
(https://trca.ca/conservation/infrastructure-projects/toronto-island-park-flood-mitigation-project/,
https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/lake-ontario-water-levels-toronto-islands-1.5148313).

**Story use:** this is WHY the grandmother is a plausible petition
general and why the island rallies with sirens, phone trees, and radio
nets — the dawn radio-net finale has a true historical rhyme in the 1980
siren. Leftover sandbags and high-water marks are free scenery and
small-talk for islander characters.

**Sensory:** sun-bleached sandbags gone stiff and gritty at a shed
corner; the smell of damp burlap and lake mud after high water
(inference from the flood-response sources).

### 5. Billy Bishop, the jet ban, and the fence

Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport occupies the islands' west end. A 1983
Tripartite Agreement between the city, the federal government, and the
port authority bans jets and sets noise limits and a curfew; changing it
needs all three parties
(https://www.torontotoday.ca/local/history/billy-bishop-airport-history-where-did-it-start-12067469).
Porter Airlines' 2013 bid to fly jets off an extended runway died when
Ottawa refused to reopen the agreement in November 2015, after a fight
led by waterfront advocates including NoJetsTO
(https://globalnews.ca/news/2336979/federal-transport-minister-nixes-billy-bishop-island-airport-expansion-plan,
https://pressprogress.ca/federal-government-steps-in-to-stop-controversial-billy-bishop-expansion-after-overwhelming-opposition-from-toronto-residents/).
The fight reignited in 2025–26 when Ontario legislated itself into the
city's seat and pushed expansion; the federal government declined in
July 2026 after mass public opposition
(https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-billy-bishop-airport-toronto-jets-carney-ford/,
https://openjaw.com/breaking/2026/07/25/feds-kill-billy-bishop-airport-expansion-plan/).
Since 2015 a pedestrian tunnel under the Western Gap links the airport
to the mainland — the islands' only fixed link, and it serves the
airport alone
(https://www.billybishopairport.com/to-from/pedestrian-tunnel/,
https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/billy-bishop-airport-s-new-pedestrian-tunnel-what-you-need-to-know-1.3172557).

**Story use:** the premise's spine, fully grounded in real politics —
petitions, demo weekends, and dueling lawn signs are how this fight has
actually been fought for 40 years. The tunnel is a great "adults have a
secret door the kids can't use" geography beat, and the perimeter fence
is a real, visible line, honoring the locked safety rail.

**Sensory:** the flat drone of turboprops on approach every few minutes,
windsock snapping, chain-link ticking in the harbour wind (inference
from airport operations; jets are banned, so the sound is propellers).

### 6. Centreville, the petting farm, and camp-kid summer

Centreville amusement park (opened 1967) is a little-kids' park: its
jewel is a 1908 Dentzel antique carousel that the flood-broke owners
agreed to sell to Carmel, Indiana for about $3 million in 2017 — saved
at the last minute when Carmel's council refused the funding, so it
never left the island
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centreville_Amusement_Park,
https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/centreville-carousel-no-go-carmel-1.4296625).
Far Enough Farm, the free petting farm beside it, dates to 1959
(https://centreisland.ca/far-enough-farm/). The island camp scene is
real: YMCA day camps base juniors near Snake Island (via Ward's ferry)
and seniors near St. Andrew-by-the-Lake (via Centre ferry), alongside
city-run CampTO island sessions
(https://www.ymcagta.org/toronto-island,
https://www.camps.ca/city-of-toronto-camps/1604/session/17000).

**Story use:** the too-old-for-this comedy engine — twelve-ish twins
herded past a kiddie coaster and a petting farm. Camp is also the
twins' warrant: a reason to be everywhere daily, with counselors and
camper witnesses. The carousel's near-loss is living memory of "the
island almost losing a treasure" — petition-rally rhetoric an islander
elder would actually use.

**Sensory:** band-organ carousel music drifting with popcorn and fry-oil
smell, then hay-and-goat warmth at the farm fence (inference from park
attraction mix).

### 7. The back-alley waterways, and the kid-lore layer

The park's ~15 islands are laced with lagoons and channels crossed by
little bridges; the Boat House on the lagoon near Centre Island rents
canoes and kayaks, and paddlers can cross most of the park by protected
water without touching the open lake
(https://www.torontoisland.com/boathouse.php,
https://paddletoronto.com/toronto-island-paddling-map/). Two real yacht
clubs anchor the harbour side: the Royal Canadian Yacht Club (on the
island since 1881) and Queen City Yacht Club on Algonquin Island (since
1921, with its own member ferry)
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_City_Yacht_Club_(Toronto),
http://torontoplaques.com/Pages/Royal_Canadian_Yacht_Club.html).
The kid-lore layer on top: Gibraltar Point Lighthouse (1808) is the
oldest surviving lighthouse on the Great Lakes, and its first keeper,
J.P. Radelmüller, was killed by soldiers in January 1815 — Toronto's
oldest cold case, and the ghost story every camper hears
(https://history.torontoisland.org/radelmuller-new-light-on-torontos-oldest-cold-case/,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibraltar_Point_Lighthouse). The William
Meany Maze on Centre Island is a real hedge maze — 1967 original,
removed in 2011, rebuilt with 1,200 black cedars and reopened June 2015
after a Calgary businessman found it gone and paid to restore it
(https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/centre-island-maze-reopens-after-donation-from-william-meany-1.3121032).
Ward's Island also has a free 18-hole disc golf course with a skyline
view (https://udisc.com/courses/toronto-island-disc-golf-course-aCoS).

**Story use:** lagoons are the kid-navigable back alleys — Amanda's
canoe skill, the Q4 paddle-and-dash, and the yacht-club-wake Wi-Fi
unlock all live here. The lighthouse ghost is the banked red-herring
generator; the maze is the scavenger-hunt beat; disc golf is free
islander-kid texture. Age-band framing for the ghost: the keeper
"vanished one winter night and the lamp went dark" — a 200-year-old
unsolved mystery, told competitively at camp. Keep the documented
grisly details (dismemberment, bootleg liquor) OFF the page.

**Sensory:** under the willows the lagoon smells green and warm,
paddle-drip loud in the quiet, then a yacht wake slaps the hull and the
canoe bobs like a cork (inference from lagoon paddling sources).

## Part 2 — Toronto facts beyond the islands (sprinkle list)

1. **The PATH.** Downtown hides roughly 30 km of underground walkways —
   Guinness lists it as the world's largest underground shopping
   complex, linking 50+ towers; office workers navigate by colour-coded
   letters (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_(Toronto)). A spy-adjacent
   gift: a whole city level the twins' parents could "run errands" in.
   **BANKED (author, 2026-07-30):** not for B3 — saved for a future
   Toronto return book. See `series-bible/city-bank.md`.
2. **The CN Tower.** 553.3 m, the world's tallest freestanding structure
   for over three decades; the EdgeWalk lets harnessed visitors lean off
   a ledge 356 m up — Guinness's highest external walk on a building
   (https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/architecture/cn-tower,
   https://www.destinationtoronto.com/listing/edgewalk-at-the-cn-tower/30799/).
   From the island, it's the compass needle: you always know which way
   the city is.
3. **Streetcars.** Toronto runs the largest, busiest streetcar network
   in North America — the city famously refused to rip out its tracks
   (https://www.urbanrail.net/am/toro/tram/toronto-tram.htm,
   https://sanantonioreport.org/toronto-city-saved-streetcar-tracks/).
4. **The ravines.** A hidden forest network threads under the city,
   home to about 1,700 plant and animal species; the city maintains a
   whole Ravine Strategy for it
   (https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/accountability-operations-customer-service/long-term-vision-plans-and-strategies/ravine-strategy/,
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toronto_ravine_system).
5. **"The 6ix."** Coined by Toronto rapper Jimmy Prime and popularized
   by Drake, from the 416/647 area codes and/or the six municipalities
   that merged into Toronto (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6ix). Kid-safe
   slang beat; don't overuse.
6. **Most multicultural big city.** Over half of Torontonians were born
   outside Canada, with ~160 languages spoken
   (https://immigration.ca/a-snapshot-of-toronto-51-of-residents-were-born-outside-canada-vital-signs-report-finds/).
   Portray through specifics (a camp roster of names, dock-food variety),
   never through accents-as-jokes.
7. **Bonus, island-adjacent:** Babe Ruth hit his first professional home
   run at Hanlan's Point Stadium on September 5, 1914 — legend says the
   ball is still in the lake; a plaque marks the spot
   (https://torontoplaques.ca/Pages/Babe_Ruth_at_Hanlans_Point). A
   ready-made camp-counselor fact and "lost thing in the water" motif.
