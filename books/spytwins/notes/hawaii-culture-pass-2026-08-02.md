## 1. The annual blessing ceremony (ch2 placard l.396–398; ch4
##    restatement l.817–819; ch12 ceremony l.3323–3342)

**The skeptic's case.** "You built your whole plot clock on a Hawaiian
ceremony you never name, never describe until the last chapter, and
then hand to an anonymous 'elder.' Either you researched what a
blessing actually is, or you used my culture as a kitchen timer."

**What survives.** More than the skeptic expects. Blessing ceremonies
are a living, everyday part of public life in Hawaiʻi — buildings,
businesses, events, and openings are routinely blessed, typically by a
**kahu** (a Hawaiian minister/officiant). The recognizable shape:
**pule** (prayer) and often **oli** (chant); **pī kai** — the kahu dips
a ti leaf in salt water and sprinkles it to cleanse and dedicate; for
openings, the **untying (not cutting) of a maile lei**. Sources:
Kaʻahele Hawaiʻi on hoʻolaʻa/blessings
(https://www.kaahelehawaii.com/na-ho%CA%BBola%CA%BBa-consecrations-blessings/),
practitioner descriptions of kahu-led blessings
(https://www.hawaiianceremonies.com/hawaiian_blessings.php,
https://hawaiian-blessing.pages.dev/posts/hawaiian-blessing/).
[ESTABLISHED]

The specific institution — an *annual re-blessing of a collection* — is
not a documented pan-Hawaiian rite; it is a plausible tradition a
specific cultural center could hold (annual community celebrations,
hoʻolauleʻa, commonly open with a blessing). [FICTION-OK] — but only if
the text frames it as *this center's* tradition, not as an ancient
ceremony the author is describing from the outside.

What the book already gets right: barefoot guests, slippers piled at
the door (l.3329–3330), the reverent silence, the feast afterward, the
community-wide attendance. These read true.

**Fixes.**
- Do **not** invent a Hawaiian name for the ceremony. Keep "the annual
  blessing" in English throughout. Resolves the ch2 [CHECK] at l.397:
  the placard simply announces "the center's annual blessing" — delete
  the marker, no Hawaiian coinage needed.
- ch12 l.3337: change "An elder blessed the artifacts" to a **kahu**,
  with a light gloss, e.g.: "A kahu — Kekoa whispered that it meant
  something between minister and guide — blessed the artifacts and the
  hall and the people in it." Keep the untranslated-chant beat that
  follows; "they could follow the silence around the words" is the
  right instinct (respect without appropriating the words).
- Add one sensory beat kids would actually see: the kahu sprinkling
  water with a ti leaf from a koa bowl as he moves past the cases.
- Optionally, one line from Mrs. Kalani in ch4 rooting it locally:
  "We have blessed the collection every year since the center opened.
  My mother stood on these mats." This makes the tradition explicitly
  institutional, which is the honest framing.

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep-with-adjustments.** Keep the annual
blessing as the public clock (it carries the weight well); name the
officiant a kahu, add pī kai detail, frame the annual tradition as the
center's own, and do not coin a Hawaiian ceremony name.

## 2. Lauhala weaving — Mrs. Santos (ch7 l.1760–1793; ch9 l.2489–2493;
##    ch12 l.3324, 3331–3335)

**The skeptic's case.** "One elderly woman single-handedly weaves
enough floor matting for a hall holding three hundred people — 'six
hundred feet stand on three weeks of her work' — from leaves she picked
three weeks ago? Lauhala preparation alone takes longer than your whole
book. You've turned a master art into a montage."

**What survives.** The craft depiction itself is largely right and
lovingly done. Lauhala weaving uses leaves of the hala tree (Pandanus
tectorius); preparation is real drudgery: trimming the base, stripping
the thorny edges and midrib, wiping each leaf clean, softening/
flattening (traditionally rolled), and coiling into **kūkaʻa** bundles
that rest anywhere from a week to months before weaving. Sources:
OluKai's lauhala overview
(https://olukai.com/blogs/news/lauhala-weaving-origins-techniques-and-uses),
step-by-step preparation guides
(https://ukuleles.com/weaving-lauhala/getting-ready-to-weave-plait/preparing-lauhala-for-weaving/),
Keanahala community weaving
(https://www.puuhonua-society.org/keanahala). [ESTABLISHED]
The porch tableau — buckets of leaves, drying racks of pale strips,
hands weaving without looking — is authentic and specific. Ceremony
mats as a standing commission for a community event: plausible; moena
(mats) are the traditional floor covering and remain prestige ceremony
pieces. [LIKELY]

Two real problems:
1. **Timeline/scale.** "The mats take three weeks" (l.1786) cannot
   cover gathering-to-floor for a whole hall. Prep spans weeks to
   months before weaving even starts.
2. **Gathering rationale.** "before the sun gets to them" (l.1774–75)
   is invented. Weavers gather *fallen or browned dry leaves*, not
   dew-fresh green ones; dawn gathering makes sense (cool hours, first
   pick of newly fallen lau), but the stated reason is wrong. Also note
   hala grows coastal/lowland — her gathering should sit on the lower
   stretch of the trail, which the text permits.

**Fixes (both preserve the locked canon decision — she still weaves
the mats, still gathers at dawn, still explains every sighting).**
- l.1774–1776, Kekoa's line: replace "She gathers the leaves up the
  trail at dawn — / before the sun gets to them." with:
  "She gathers the fallen leaves along the lower trail at dawn — /
  she says the best ones go fast, and the afternoon rain / spoils the
  rest."
- l.1786: replace "The mats take three weeks." with "The new mats take
  three weeks. The leaves took a year." — one line converts the error
  into craft knowledge (this year's kūkaʻa were prepped from last
  season's gathering; the three weeks is the weaving push).
- ch12 l.3334–3335: "three weeks of her work" → "sixty years of her
  work" (the hall's mats accumulated across decades of ceremonies —
  it also lands harder, and matches her "Sixty years" line at l.3405).

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: adjust** (three line-level fixes above; no
structural change). Update the canon line in `concept/premise.md` when
adopted: "weeks of weaving atop a year of leaf preparation."

## 3. The koʻi (adze) — trail symbol and stolen artifact (ch5
##    l.1136–1157; ch10 l.2831; ch12 l.3347)

**The skeptic's case.** "A koʻi in a drawer is fine. A koʻi as a
petroglyph is you inventing rock art — tool motifs are not a Hawaiian
petroglyph type — and 'blade on a handle' identified from a university
guide makes your invention wear a real institution's name."

**What survives.** The artifact is fully plausible: the koʻi (stone
adze, basalt blade lashed to a wooden haft) was the indispensable
pre-contact tool — canoe carving, woodwork, even shaping other stone —
and koʻi are standard holdings in Hawaiʻi museum collections (Bishop
Museum holds many); the Mauna Kea quarry Keanakākoʻi is one of the
largest adze quarries in the world. Sources: NPS "Hawaiian Tools"
(https://www.nps.gov/hale/learn/historyculture/hawaiian-tools.htm),
Maui Magazine "Carved in Stone"
(https://www.mauimagazine.net/carved-in-stone/), Kealopiko on adze
making (https://kealopiko.shorthandstories.com/olopu/index.html).
[ESTABLISHED] Naming: the word is right; spell it **koʻi** per the
orthography decision in Flag 5. A cultural-center workroom cabinet is
exactly where such a piece would live. [ESTABLISHED]

As a petroglyph motif: documented Hawaiian motifs run to human figures,
canoes and sails, paddlers, dogs, fishhooks appear occasionally, and
circles/cupules; a koʻi glyph is not a documented type. The whole
trail (see item 4) is already a fictional construct, so this rides on
the same license — but it must not be attributed to real scholarship.
[FICTION-OK with item 4's source fix]

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep** (artifact, theft, and name), with the
orthography normalization and with the source-attribution fix in
item 4 covering the glyph side.

## 4. Petroglyphs / kiʻi pōhaku (ch1 l.137–164; ch3; ch5 l.1112–1147;
##    ch6 l.1374–1411; ch10 l.2816–2833; ch12 l.3417–3427)

**The skeptic's case — and it is the strongest one in the book.**
"Your local kid calls our kiʻi pōhaku 'graffiti' that 'nobody pays
attention to anymore.' Your heroine strokes the carvings with her
fingers. You attribute invented symbol meanings to the University of
Hawaiʻi. Your kids treat a sacred site as a treasure map, stack rocks
in a cave, and your climax happens in a lava-tube chamber — which any
Hawaiian reader will half-expect to be a burial cave, because that is
what looted Hawaiian caves overwhelmingly were."

**What survives, piece by piece.**

*Terminology.* Kiʻi pōhaku is the Hawaiian term for petroglyphs and the
book never uses it — a missed educational beat the series formula
exists for. Real interpretive framing: carvings may record travels,
events, genealogy, boundaries, sacred places — and meanings are often
*not known*. Sources: Images of Old Hawaiʻi
(https://imagesofoldhawaii.com/na-kii-pohaku/), Bradshaw Foundation
(https://www.bradshawfoundation.com/news/index.php?id=Petroglyphs-in-Hawai-i),
Cox & Stasack, *Hawaiian Petroglyphs* (Bishop Museum). [ESTABLISHED]
Fix: give Kekoa or Mrs. Kalani the term once, e.g. ch2 tour: "Kiʻi
pōhaku, we call them. Images in stone."

*Touching.* Site etiquette is explicit: do not touch, trace, chalk,
wet, or walk on petroglyphs (skin oils and abrasion damage them)
(https://www.to-hawaii.com/culture/petroglyphs.php; NPS Puʻuloa
guidance). The book violates this twice and models it for 8–12s:
- l.154: "Amanda traced her finger along one of the deeper grooves."
  Fix: cut the touch and give the correction to Kekoa — it
  characterizes him and teaches the reader: "'Eyes, not fingers,'
  Kekoa said. 'Skin wears them away. My grandmother's rule.'" Amanda
  sketches instead — which is already her thing.
- l.658–659: the measuring tape "pinned... against the rock." Fix:
  "held his end of the tape a careful inch off the rock" — keeps the
  fair-play measurement, models the rule.
  Photographing and sketching are the *approved* behaviors; the twins'
  core method survives untouched. [ESTABLISHED]

*"Graffiti" / "nobody pays attention" (l.139–150).* The self-correction
("way more important") does real work, and kid-voice slang is
defensible; the worse line is "Nobody pays much attention to them
anymore," which is both plot-necessary and community-flattening. Fix:
"Most people walk right past them. My grandmother says the rocks can
wait; they've got more patience than people." Keeps the neglected-clue
setup, removes the claim that the community doesn't care. Keep or
soften "graffiti" at the author's discretion — with the correction
retained either way.

*The invented decoding attributed to UH (l.1119–1123).* "University of
Hawaii digital archive. Petroglyph field survey. ...this spiral form
marks a sacred place. And these straight lines under a symbol show
direction of travel." The directional-lines system is invented, and
real surveys stress that meanings are largely unknown. Putting the
invention in a real institution's mouth is the one outright
fact-attribution failure in the manuscript. Fix (minimal, preserves
fair-play): fictionalize the source and hedge the certainty —
"a digital archive of old island field surveys" + Andrew: "The survey
says nobody knows most of the meanings for sure. But the one team that
mapped this district guessed the spiral marks a sacred place, and the
lines show direction of travel." Bonus authenticity: spirals are
genuinely *rare* in Hawaiian rock art — "a symbol almost never found in
Hawaiʻi" (Waikoloa field reporting:
https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2025/08/08/ancient-stone-carvings-uncovered-on-hawaiian-beach,
https://www.popsci.com/science/hawaii-beach-petroglyphs/) — so one
added line ("Spirals are almost never found in Hawaiʻi. This hillside
is special") turns a weakness into a clue for why Dr. Lee is obsessed
and why the site matters. The chamber's "canoes with sails, rows of
paddlers" (l.2823–2825) are documented real motifs — keep, they're the
most accurate carvings in the book. [ESTABLISHED]

*Rock-stacking breadcrumbs (ch6 l.1407–1411, 1494–1503, 1537–1539,
1565–1567).* Genuine problem. DLNR and cultural practitioners actively
discourage recreational rock stacking; stacks mimic ahu, and at sacred
sites the practice reads as desecration — and Amanda builds hers
*inside* a place the text has just labeled sacred. Kids imitate
protagonists. Sources: HAWAIʻI Magazine on why stacking isn't pono
(https://www.hawaiimagazine.com/why-rock-stacking-at-hanakapiai-beach-isnt-considered-pono-right/),
NPS on rock structures
(https://home.nps.gov/puho/learn/management/coralgraffiti.htm). Fix
that improves the craft: Kekoa already bands flashlights with
reflective tape (l.2666–2668) — let Amanda carry a strip of his tape
and a pocketful of her own markers (tape-flagged clothespins, or
tape squares on her notebook's torn corners), placed at junctions
*and collected on the way out* ("leave the mountain the way you found
it" — one line, and it rhymes with her litter-collection beat at
l.3206–3213). The "big, medium, small" ritual can survive as the order
she clips them. Payoff lines in ch6 update mechanically.

*The lava-tube chamber and the burial-cave shadow (ch6, ch10, ch11).*
This is the hard sensitivity. Hawaiian lava tubes were preferred burial
places; iwi kūpuna (ancestral remains) and moepū (funerary possessions)
were extensively looted from caves, and that looting is an open wound —
protected today under NAGPRA and HRS 6E, with entry to caves without
permission strongly discouraged precisely because they may hold iwi.
Sources: OHA on iwi kūpuna repatriation
(https://www.oha.org/iwi-repatriation/), Land Use Commission summary of
burial rights
(https://luc.hawaii.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/5.-Iwi-Ku%CC%84puna-Summary_June-2022.pdf),
SHPD Hawaiian burials
(https://dlnr.hawaii.gov/shpd/branches/ibc/hawaiian-burials/),
National Geographic on Hawaiʻi's lava tubes
(https://www.nationalgeographic.com/magazine/article/lava-tubes-hawaii-caves).
[ESTABLISHED]

The manuscript, examined closely, *already avoids the worst*: the
chamber contains carvings only — no remains, no funerary goods; the
staged artifacts are modern plants by the villain; and ch12's ending
("the elders are deciding how the chamber gets protected — studied
properly, with the community deciding what happens," l.3424–3427) is
genuinely the right disposition, matching real consultation practice.
What's missing is a *signal to the knowing reader* that the author saw
the burial-cave association and steered clear of it, plus an
acknowledgment that kids entering an unmapped sacred cave uninvited is
itself not pono. Fixes:
- Characterize the chamber as a *carved gathering place* — Mrs. Kalani
  in ch11 or ch12: "A place of records. Voyages, names, seasons —
  carved where they would keep. Not a burial place; those we would
  never speak of like this, and never enter." One line does triple
  duty: closes the burial-cave reading, teaches the real distinction,
  and voices the kapu.
- ch4 l.822–827, Kekoa's "sacred sites... hid important treasures":
  change "hid important treasures" to "kept things that mattered —
  and some places you don't go into at all. Ever." Removes the
  treasure-cave trope and plants the boundary the twins later cross
  under emergency logic.
- Give the trespass a consequence-note: Mrs. Kalani's ch11/12 material
  already includes gratitude; add half a line — "Next time, you knock
  first. Even on a mountain." — so entry-without-permission is named,
  gently, in-genre.
- Do NOT add bones, moepū, or "undisturbed dust of the dead" anywhere;
  the chamber must stay a records-place.

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: adjust.** Keep the trail, the chamber, and the
fair-play decoding — they are the book. Apply: kiʻi pōhaku term;
no-touch fixes (two lines); "nobody pays attention" rewrite;
fictionalized survey source + meanings-unknown hedge + spiral-rarity
line; breadcrumb swap; chamber "place of records, not burial" line;
treasure-cave line fix.

## 5. Honu — green sea turtles (ch5 l.1059–1077; ch12 l.3468–3470)

**The skeptic's case.** "Your turtle scene is cute, but 'don't touch'
is the floor, not the rule. The rule is distance, and your book will
send ten thousand kids paddling toward honu."

**What survives.** Almost everything. Honu (Hawaiian green sea turtles)
are protected under the Endangered Species Act and state law; touching,
chasing, or harassing them is illegal; NOAA/DLNR guidance is to keep
**at least 10 feet (3 m), on land or in water**, and let the animal
control the encounter. Sources: NOAA Fisheries viewing guidelines
(https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/pacific-islands/marine-life-viewing-guidelines/viewing-marine-wildlife-hawaii),
DLNR reminder (https://dlnr.hawaii.gov/blog/2014/06/06/nr14-068/).
[ESTABLISHED] In the scene, the *turtle* approaches Andrew while he
lies still — legally and ethically clean (passive encounter), and
Kekoa's "Don't touch — it's the law, and also she'll judge you"
(l.1064–1066) is correct and perfectly voiced. The ch12 reprise
(surfacing beyond the break) is fine as written.

**Fix.** One clause so kids model the full rule — extend Kekoa's line:
"Don't touch, don't follow — give her ten feet. It's the law, and also
she'll judge you." Andrew holding still while she sinks away already
demonstrates compliance.

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep** (one-clause adjust above).

## 6. Geography, logistics, and the weather clock

**The skeptic's case.** "Your Maui has a beach town with petroglyph
fields, walkable lava tubes, and green mountains all within a kid's
lunch break — that town doesn't exist. And your uncle warns that 'the
rainy season's coming' in June, which is exactly backwards."

**What survives.**
- **Kahului shipping (ch8 l.2074–2075).** Accurate and well-chosen.
  Kahului Harbor is Maui's only commercial deep-draft harbor; container
  freight moves through it via Matson, Pasha, and Young Brothers — a
  container "out of Kahului" is precisely how stolen goods would leave
  Maui by sea. Sources: Hawaii Business overview
  (https://www.hawaiibusiness.com/hawaii-shipping-air-transportation-industry-continues-to-grow-pasha-matson-young-brothers/),
  Young Brothers (https://htbyb.com/company-overview/). [ESTABLISHED]
  Keep unchanged.
- **Trail/lava-tube/petroglyph geography.** Maui genuinely has both:
  the Olowalu petroglyphs (Puʻu Kilea, ~70 kiʻi pōhaku on a basalt face
  near the coast, West Maui:
  https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/olowalu-petroglyphs) and
  walkable lava tubes (Hana Lava Tube / Kaʻeleku:
  https://ww2.aaa.com/tripcanvas/attraction/hana-lava-tube-kaeleku-caverns-VA16976).
  They are, however, on opposite ends of the island; the book's town is
  a composite. [FICTION-OK] Because real Maui petroglyph and cave sites
  suffer real vandalism and looting, the composite is actually the
  *responsible* choice — it keeps readers from treating the book as a
  site map. Keep the town unnamed; see Flag 6 for the author's note.
- **Storm season (ch3 l.696–717; ch5 l.1092–1098; ch10 l.2566–2569).**
  The book runs June–August (three-month stay ending "at the end of the
  summer"). Hawaiʻi's wet season is roughly November–March; summer is
  the dry season, and thunderstorms are infrequent statewide (~20–30
  days/year, more in winter). Sources: WRCC Hawaiʻi climate narrative
  (https://wrcc.dri.edu/Climate/narrative_hi.php), Climates to Travel
  (https://www.climatestotravel.com/climate/hawaii). [ESTABLISHED]
  Three findings:
  1. Uncle Jacob's "The rainy season's coming. They don't have much
     time left" (l.2566–2569) is backwards. Fix: "You've only got two
     months left on that island. Best hiking weather of the year is
     right now." Same persuasive function, true.
  2. The ch3 lightning storm is plot-critical (the flash reveals the
     upper symbols) and can stay — rare is not impossible — but buy it
     authenticity with one line from Kekoa later: "First lightning
     I've seen all year. The mountain wanted you to look up." (Or
     plainer, per taste; the rarity acknowledgment is the point.)
  3. The ch5 storm needs no lightning; keep it a fast trade-shower
     squall (text already reads that way — no change needed beyond not
     adding thunder). "Storms show up out of nowhere here" (l.698) is
     fair for brief localized showers; fine as is.
- Small authentic touches verified in passing: shave ice (correctly
  not "shaved"), lilikoʻi = passion fruit, musubi, mango bread, lanai,
  ti plants, plumeria, mock-orange hedge, gecko chirps — all sound.
  Optional upgrade: ch4 l.946 "coconut pie" → "haupia pie" (the beloved
  local coconut dessert; free authenticity).

**RULING RECOMMENDATION: keep-with-adjustments** (Jacob's line fix
mandatory; lightning-rarity line recommended; Kahului untouched).

## 7. Representation sweep

**The skeptic's case.** "Two white kids from Seattle land on Maui,
out-read the locals' own carvings in eighteen days, get the lei and
the applause, and fly off to Tokyo. The local boy runs errands, the
kupuna dispenses aloha-wisdom, and the one working-class neighbor is
the red herring."

**What survives — mostly the book, honestly.** The manuscript has
real, load-bearing mitigations:
- **Mrs. Kalani** is the anti-mystical-elder: she runs the tracker, the
  backup tape ("old faithful"), the police mobilization — the *proof*
  is hers by locked canon, and her "too old for technology" beat
  (l.3123–3127) knowingly spikes the trope. Her aloha speech
  (l.3582–3597) is accurate in content (aloha as
  hello/goodbye/love/connection) and is earned by relationship, not
  offered to tourists. Keep. Two continuity wrinkles for the
  continuity-keeper, not culture: she "works at" the center (ch1),
  has "a hospital shift" (ch8 l.1906–07), and "runs a gift shop"
  (ch9 l.2459–60) — pick a job description.
- **Kekoa** subverts the beach-boy frame (drone instructor, surf
  teacher, tech mentor, and the one who runs the mountain). He is,
  structurally, the culture-explainer to visiting protagonists — the
  series formula's inherent risk — but he has independent stakes and
  the best jokes. One genuine failure: **ch12 honors only the twins**
  (l.3369–3390: lei and bracelets for Amanda and Andrew; Kekoa is
  filming). He ran for help and talked the rescue up the mountain.
  Fix: Mrs. Kalani pulls him up too — "and my grandson, who ran" — a
  third lei. This is the single highest-value representation fix in
  the pass. The locked climax canon (win is the twins', proof is
  hers) is untouched.
- **Mrs. Santos** is the pass's quiet triumph: red herring dissolved
  into master weaver, honored on her own terms ("The mats know it.
  That's enough"). Her surname reads local (Filipino/Portuguese
  heritage families are core to Hawaiʻi, and the lauhala-weaving
  community includes weavers of many ancestries); leave her heritage
  unspecified. Keep.
- **Messenger line (ch12 l.3384–3388).** "In old Hawaii, young people
  were often the ones who carried important messages between
  villages" — close but soft. The real institution: **kūkini**, elite
  trained runner-messengers serving the aliʻi, selected young for
  speed (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kukini,
  https://hilo.hawaii.edu/rec/HistoryoftheKukini.php). Fix: "In old
  Hawaiʻi, the kūkini — the swift runners — carried messages between
  villages. They were chosen young, for sharp eyes and fast feet."
  More accurate *and* it names Kekoa's contribution if the lei fix
  lands.
- **Dr. Lee's theory (ch7 l.1669–1679; ch11 l.3011–3018, 3110–3114).**
  Accuracy-adjacent representation issue: "the old texts insist the
  crossing was impossible" and "a migration the university swears
  never happened" risk implying that Hawaiian voyaging achievement is
  scientifically fringe. It is the opposite: Hōkūleʻa's 1976
  instrument-free Hawaiʻi–Tahiti voyages settled the intentional-
  voyaging question and launched a cultural renaissance
  (https://archive.hokulea.com/found.html,
  https://archive.hokulea.com/holokai/1976/ben_finney.html).
  [ESTABLISHED] Fix: narrow his claim to something genuinely contested
  — e.g. ch11 l.3016–3018: "everyone insists this district was settled
  three centuries later — but the carvings prove voyagers were here
  first" — and keep Kekoa's ch5 "master navigators" line (l.1037–1040),
  which already states the truth. Dr. Lee stays wrong about methods,
  not about Hawaiians.
- **Language policy.** The manuscript does not italicize Hawaiian
  words — keep it that way; Hawaiian is an official language of the
  State of Hawaiʻi and current local practice (UH style guide) is
  roman type (https://www.hawaii.edu/style-guide/,
  https://www.himb.hawaii.edu/hawaiian-language/). Diacritics are
  inconsistent: "ko'i"/"ki'i" (straight apostrophes), "lilikoi" (no
  ʻokina), "lanai," "Hawaii." See Flag 5 for the series-wide decision.
  No Pidgin is attempted anywhere — correct call for this author;
  faking Hawaiʻi Creole English badly is worse than standard-English
  dialogue. Do not add "brah"/"da kine" garnish.
- **Poi beat (ch12 l.3494–3497).** "approached like scientists and
  finished like locals, mostly" — a gentle brush with the poi-is-weird
  trope, self-aware enough to keep. Optional warm-up: Kekoa demos
  two-finger poi first.
- **The twins-solve-it structure.** Genre-standard for MG and canon-
