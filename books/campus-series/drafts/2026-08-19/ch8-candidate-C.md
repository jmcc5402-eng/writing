# Chapter 8 — One Name On It

Cal came to Delmar's on a Wednesday wanting two things,
a brisket plate and no conversation about the House,
and Delmar's was the one table in town where the House
was the only conversation on the menu.
He knew that going in. He was hungry.
A man can hold two impossible beliefs before lunch
and still signal his turns.

The lot was half full and the smoke stood over the block,
hickory on top, vinegar and black pepper underneath,
and he heard her before his boots hit the gravel.
Not words. Just a laugh, carrying off the screen porch,
over two box fans and Tick Moran talking on the counter radio,
and the whole porch leaning toward it like a stove in January.

Ratchet sat up in the passenger seat and looked at him.

"Don't," Cal said.

She didn't. She was a professional.

There was a version of lunch where he ate in the truck.
He gave it the four seconds it deserved.
Then he got out, and Ratchet dropped down after him,
and they took the steps together, on purpose,
past a porch full of people who had all suddenly found
their plates fascinating.

Inside, the register had a QR code taped up
where the menu used to hang, and under it a hand-lettered sign
offering oat milk, in small print, under protest.
The clipboard sat where it had sat all month,
a pen tied to it, pages of names deep.
Cal read the top line without meaning to,
then made himself stop meaning to.

"Brisket plate," Ray said from the register. Not a question.

"And a quiet table."

"One of those we smoke fresh daily."

His crew had the two inside tables by the window fan,
and every one of them was looking somewhere else with great skill,
which is how a man finds out he's been the subject of a table.
His second tipped her chin at the screen door, meaning
*you see what's out there,* and went back to her beans.

He saw what was out there.

He carried his tray out anyway and took the rail chair
at the end table, the one facing up Millrow, toward the House.
He always took that chair. He'd have told you it was for the fan.
Nobody ever asked, which was the chair's other advantage.
Ratchet arranged herself underneath it with a sigh
and went off duty.

Marisol was three tables down, being introduced
to somebody's visiting cousins,
and she came out of the introduction owning both their names
and the name of their dog.
By the time she'd worked the length of the porch,
strangers were turned toward her like chairs
she'd personally rearranged.
He did not watch her do it,
except in the sense that he missed none of it.

She pulled out the chair across from him
like it had her name on it.

"Afternoon, university."

"Ma'am."

"You're aware you still owe me hardware."

"It's on my bench," Cal said. "Accruing interest."

"So there's a bench." She sat. "Progress."

"You eating," he said, "or campaigning?"

"I ate at eleven. Now I'm visiting my constituents."

"Half your constituents came for the ribs."

"So did I. Then the ribs came with a view of a wrecking ball."

She had never once said any of it quietly,
and the porch, which had been pretending to eat,
gave up pretending.

"You want to see something?" She was up before he answered,
in and out through the screen door, and came back
with the clipboard from beside the register.
Her hands were quick and certain on it,
thumb riding the page edges like a bank teller's,
the tied pen swinging.
She was flushed with the argument and openly enjoying it,
and it was a good look, and he was not looking at it.
"Pages, Cal. Grandmothers. Line cooks.
People wait for this pen at lunch rush. Ray tied it down twice."

"Once," Ray said through the window.
"Second knot was decorative."

She set the clipboard down in front of Cal with both hands.

He didn't pick it up.

"That clipboard's got one name on it, ma'am. Mine."

The porch went quiet enough to hear the smoker tick.

"Every roof on that campus leaks onto my desk," he said,
and he said it at full volume,
because she'd set the volume and he could match it.
"Every furnace. Every payroll hour.
Everybody eating lunch behind that screen door.
Paper doesn't decide anything.
Paper lands on a desk, and the desk is mine,
and when this is over I still have to be able
to look at this whole town across a counter.
So collect your pages. I'm the one who has to be right."

"No. You're the one holding the pen," Marisol said,
"and your pen is aimed at the town's living room,
and you're standing there telling me about roofs.
Nobody signs a guest book for a boiler, Cal.
They sign because that House is theirs,
and you cannot budget your way around the word theirs."

"That House has ten good years left in the porch," Cal said.
"Fifteen if somebody loved it.
That's not sentiment. That's lumber.
And I don't know if the rest of it can be saved,
and I am the only person anywhere near this fight
who will say I don't know out loud,
so you might consider what that's worth."

"Worth exactly one signature. On the right line."

"It's a budget, not a bedtime story."

"Everything's a budget with you.
You'd depreciate a sunset.
You'd inspect a hug for load-bearing walls."

The laugh got out of him before he could stop it,
which annoyed him, which she saw, which made it worse.

Behind the screen door, a fork stopped halfway.

Because the crew wasn't laughing.
Through the mesh, her guest book didn't look like a guest book.
To twelve people whose winters ran on the same thin budget
this fight kept pulling at, it looked like a layoff notice
with flowers on it,
and they had just watched their director
laugh at the woman carrying it.

Then the screen door swung out hard,
Sonny Dillard backing through it with a loaded to-go tray,
and the door came back and clipped the tray,
and the top box went airborne.

Verna Poteat, eighty-three, put up one hand without rising
and fielded it flat. Level. Not a bean lost.
She set it back on Sonny's tray and patted it once.

"Verna," Sonny said, reverent.

"I know," said Verna.

"Be on my side," Marisol said. "I want you on my side."

"Honey, I'm on the side with the show." Verna went back to her tea.
"Anyway, I may be spoken for on Thursdays. It's early days.
I haven't decided what I know."

Earlene had turned her chair all the way around by then,
fanning herself with a paper menu, festival seating,
though every minute or so her eyes cut to a pickup
parked across the road and came back.

"Earlene," Cal said. "You watching us or the road?"

"This is reconnaissance."

"Which one of us?"

"That's classified," Earlene said, and fanned faster.

"November," Marisol said, volume restored.
"The trustees meet in November.
That's the whole calendar this town gets,
and you know the date better than I do."

The screen door had come back off Sonny crooked
and was banging out of rhythm, top hinge screw sitting proud,
and Cal was up and at it with the screwdriver blade
of his pocketknife before he'd decided to be.

"Are you fixing the door in the middle of my argument?"

"Keep going. I hear better when my hands are busy."

"You're aware that fixing things angrily is still charm,
in county terms."

"Then quit watching my hands and make your case."

She made it. Full volume, to him and to the porch both,
the town's one living room and the people whose whole year
ran through it, and she was better than good,
and the porch was hers by the end of it.

He drove the screw home, folded the knife, turned around,
and she was closer than he'd left her.

Neither of them said anything.
It went on one beat past the length
either of them would have admitted to.

The register bell rang.

"To-go for Fountain," Ray hollered through the window.
"While it's hot."

The moment paid its check and left.

Marisol sat back down and put her palms flat on the table.

"Ground rules," she said, at a volume for the table
instead of the county.
"I won't make it personal.
The building, the board, the budget, your November,
all of it is fair game, said as loud as I like.
You, yourself? Never on the table.
I don't fight that way."

"Then I won't pretend it isn't personal," Cal said.
"It's lights and groceries
and somebody's grandmother's porch.
Anybody who sells you this as paperwork
is lying to one of us. Probably both."

"Deal."

She put her hand across the table and he took it.
One pump, a good strong grip,
and she let go a half second before he did.

"This isn't settled," he said.

"Cal." She was already standing, collecting her clipboard,
smiling at him with the whole porch watching her do it.
"It isn't even loud yet."

The second laugh got clean away from him.

"That's twice," his second said, behind the screen door,
in the voice people keep for scores.

"Somebody ought to sell tickets," Verna advised her tea.

Marisol's phone was already out and up.
Her thumb did its two quick taps,
saving the porch to wherever she kept things,
and she never once looked at the screen.
Then she went back to her constituents,
and the porch reshuffled itself around her.

Ray came out with a wrapped plate
and traded it for Cal's cold one without being asked.

"Talk's smoke," Ray said. "Yours is at least hickory."

Cal ate the second plate in the chair facing up Millrow,
and it was better than the first one would have been,
and he knew exactly why,
and had no intention of saying so, even to the dog.

Nobody had won. Nobody had given an inch,
and nobody was going to.
The whole porch had watched the two of them
plant their flags on the same square foot of Ashford
and grin about it,
and the whole town would have it retold by supper.

He'd gotten the plate.
The quiet he'd lost, fair and square, at full volume,
in front of his entire crew,
and the fact he carried back to campus
and could not put down anywhere
was that he hadn't wanted it to stop.

Ratchet rode back with her head out the window,
and whatever she knew, she kept it.
