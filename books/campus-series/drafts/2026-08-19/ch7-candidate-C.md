---

The Checkerboard at four on a Tuesday belonged to the pie case,
the regulars, and anyone who needed a booth
more than they were prepared to admit.
Marisol had the one by the window.
The phone lay face-down beside the pie fork,
and she had turned it over exactly twice.

Once when it buzzed, and it was the pharmacy.
Once when it didn't buzz at all, which was somehow worse,
and she'd flipped it, read nothing,
and set it back down like she was returning evidence.

Twenty minutes north, her son was under the stadium glow,
a walk-on number on a practice field,
twenty minutes and a whole life away.
Her daughter had been at Bellamy since August
and had texted twice in two days.
Once at eleven p.m. to request money,
an amount with a suspicious quantity of nines in it,
and once to say *can you send my charger,*
from a child who had packed four.
Marisol had paid the nines and mailed a charger
she suspected was ransom
for a phone call that would come when it came.

The first fall with both of them gone,
and the parents' group was full of women in the same water.
She'd pinned a thread for it Monday,
*first-flight parents, my loves, this way,*
and watched four hundred strangers
walk each other through it.
It had helped everybody except,
in any way she could personally locate,
her.

Earlene Tatum slid into the other side of the booth
without invitation, ceremony, or her reading glasses,
set down her coffee, and looked at the phone.

"Twice," Earlene said.
"I've been watching from the register.
Last week you'd have worn a hole in it. That's progress."

"You're keeping score," Marisol said.

"Somebody has to."
Earlene raised two fingers toward the counter without turning.
"Two pies. The buttermilk and the chocolate.
Both forks to this table."

"Earlene. I came in for coffee."

"You came in to sit somewhere that isn't your house
and not be fussed over.
That's available, but it comes with pie."
Earlene settled in with nowhere better to be,
which was untrue, which was the kindness.
"I've buried two mayors, so listen.
The first fall is the worst."

"Okay," Marisol said.

"The second fall is also the worst," Earlene said.
"So you may as well eat."

The pies arrived.
Marisol laughed, a real one, sudden,
half a beat from being something else entirely,
and picked up a fork,
and her eyes went hot for exactly one second.
Earlene studied the parking lot with tremendous interest
until it passed.

"He's twenty minutes away," Marisol said.
"That's the stupid part.
I could hit his practice field with a decent throw."

"Twenty minutes is the longest distance there is,"
Earlene said. "Eat your pie."

She ate her pie.
And between bites, because she was done not-looking,
she turned the phone over on purpose
and went to see what the board had grown overnight.

New citizens.
*Concerned4Ashford,* three days old, no photo, no history,
wanted everyone to know the House was
"honestly a firetrap at this point, and past saving."
*AshfordSafetyMom,* two days old, friends with nobody,
agreed with her in all caps.
And folded into the messages, polite as a church bulletin,
a note from a name Marisol had never seen:
*As moderator, you'll want to keep the group neutral
on this issue. Just thinking of you.*
Two more sat behind it, differently worded, same fold.

Her thumb did the screenshots before she'd decided anything.
One, two, three, timestamped, filed,
the small tidy motion of a habit with calluses on it.
Sunday the logs would go out with the coffee,
same as every Sunday, rain or shine or nothing to report.

Then she put her fork down and read it all again, slower.

Real people arrived mad about a thing
with fourteen years of casserole photos behind them.
These accounts had been born mad,
all of them this week, all of them fluent,
all of them saying *firetrap*
like they'd rehearsed in the same room.
And the polite ones weren't asking her to disagree.
They were asking her to be quiet.

You didn't organize to shout down an opinion.
You organized to clear a road.
The whole town had read the same caption she had,
back when it made the rounds.
The House had until November,
and November was waiting on one signature.
Not a vote. Not a committee.
Not the weather of public opinion.
One man's pen.

She knew the man.
She had watched him laugh once,
head back, the whole chest in it,
unguarded as a screen door in summer,
a man briefly twenty years younger.
She found the old post without exactly deciding to,
and his name in it,
and looked at the name a good while too long,
long enough that the diner went quiet around the looking.

"Your chocolate's about to weep," Earlene said,
and pushed the second pie into her line of sight.

Marisol blinked, set the phone face-down,
and reached into her tote instead.
A spiral notebook, ninety-nine cents of college rule,
bought in a fit of optimism at the drugstore.
She clicked her pen.

"What's this now," Earlene said.

"People," Marisol said, and opened to the first page.
"The House's people.
Everybody with a story in that building,
on the record, in their own words,
before anybody signs anything.
I've got my Saturdays back.
This is where they're going."

Earlene chewed on that along with the buttermilk.
"Start with Ray," she said. "Then Verna.
And spell Tatum right."

*Ray. Earlene. Verna.*

"You're page one," Marisol said.

"Course I am," said Earlene,
and took up her fork again like the matter was settled,
because it was.

---

That night, the porch collected women.

Aisha Cole came up the walk at seven,
still in the day's work clothes,
carrying a steel shaker that was sweating harder than she was.
"I have been on my feet since four-thirty," she announced.
"I have brought espresso martinis
to a porch cooler full of domestics,
because I contain multitudes.
And if anybody says the word *firetrap* to me tonight,
I will prescribe something."

"Hello to you too," Marisol said.

Birdie Pham took the steps with a coffee can of zinnias
riding one hip, orange and shameless,
and set them on the porch rail,
where they immediately outranked everything.
"From the truck," Birdie said.
"They don't keep. Neither do I."

Marisol went in for glasses.
Through the kitchen doorway the fridge stood
where it always stood,
a June date circled on the calendar page under a magnet,
a note about somebody else's life.
She took down three jelly jars and came back out.

Aisha poured. The first sip went around,
and the day's news went with it, work, the truck,
the tent count for Saturday,
and then Aisha set her jar down with the click of a woman
arriving at her actual grievance.

"That coach up there," Aisha said,
"fights every call I make. Every one.
I say sit, he hears play.
I say Monday, he hears Saturday.
Today he stood in my doorway
and argued with me about my own signature."

Birdie looked at Marisol.
Marisol looked at Birdie.

"Don't," Aisha said.

"Nobody said anything," Birdie said.

"You had faces."

"We were born with these," Marisol said.
"How tall is he?"

"I am not doing this," Aisha said,
and refilled her own jar,
and did not say how tall he was,
which was, the porch agreed without a word, an answer.

"Speaking of the market," Marisol said,
because mercy demanded a fresh victim,
"how's the peach man?"

Birdie adjusted one zinnia by a critical half inch.
"Peaches are done for the season."

"That's not what she asked," Aisha said.

"There's a late variety," Birdie told the flowers,
and did not deny one single thing,
which took a discipline they all respected.

The second round went around slower.
Down the street a sprinkler came on.
The cicadas changed keys.
Marisol turned her jar by the rim
and told them about the two pies, and the new accounts,
and the polite strangers who were just thinking of her.

"Firetrap," Aisha said, dangerously.

"You said you'd prescribe something."

"I'm deciding what."

"Here's mine," Marisol said,
and it came out steadier
than it had sounded in her head all afternoon.
"The fight isn't opinion.
Opinions don't hatch fully grown on a Tuesday.
The whole thing runs through one man's pen,
and while everybody's out yelling at the weather,
I'll be doing something older than the internet.
The House's people. On the record. In their own words.
Ray, Earlene, Verna, and everybody after them,
every Saturday I've got, until it's a book
nobody can call a firetrap."

Birdie stopped fussing with the zinnias.
Aisha looked at her over the rim of the jar.

"Say it again," Aisha said.

"Why?"

"Because now we've heard it," Birdie said.
"Twice makes it true."

So she said it again, shorter, decided,
and Aisha nodded once, slowly,
like a signature of her own.

"And the facilities man," Aisha said. "The pen himself.
The one still holding your wrench hostage."

"The town has one story," Marisol said,
"and it's that one."

"It's a good story."

"It's not about him."
Marisol turned her jar a quarter-turn.
"His name is the name that matters.
That's all I know about it."
Which was true,
except that she also knew what laughing did
to the whole frame of the man,
and that she could call up his hands
with a clarity she had never applied for.

"You've gone somewhere," Aisha said.

Marisol realized she was smiling at a point
somewhere past Aisha's ear, and stopped.

"Mm," said Birdie, into her jar.

The dark came up through the yard
and the porch light took over.
The zinnias went from orange to ember.
The shaker gave up its last inch.
And Birdie, who had been quietly building toward something
for one full jar, put her drink down.

"So my cousin in Birmingham," Birdie said.

Aisha sat up like church had started.
"Tell it proper."

"I intend to."
Birdie put her feet up on the cooler.
"My cousin in Birmingham. July.
Her dock, her lake,
and the man who'd spent three weekends
rebuilding her pontoon motor.
And before you ask, yes. Single, both of them.
Grown. Free as birds."

"What does he look like," Marisol said.

"She says, and I quote,
'like the reason the church put in air conditioning.'"

"Go on," Aisha said.

Birdie went on.
She did not hurry.
She gave them the heat coming off the dock boards,
and the smell of the lake,
cut grass and gasoline and sunscreen going warm,
and the cooler with three drinks left in it,
none of them what anybody wanted.
She gave them the dragonflies, supervising.
She gave them the man wiping his hands on a rag, slow,
watching her cousin the whole time he did it.

"Birdie," Aisha said. "The point."

"The point is being approached correctly."
Birdie took a sip on purpose.
She gave them the rag going over the rail.
She gave them the man saying her cousin's name once,
quiet, like a question he already liked the answer to.
She gave them her cousin
walking the whole length of that dock,
and then she gave them the temperature again,
in case anyone had forgotten the temperature.

"*Birdie,*" Aisha said.

Marisol had stopped breathing entirely.

Birdie gave them one more dragonfly.
And then, at last, leaning in,
"and she swears his shirt came off over his head
and hit those dock boards,
and the second it landed she—"

The truck horn went off at the curb.
One long blast, self-inflicted, into the night,
Birdie's flower truck announcing nothing to no one.
The porch came apart.
Aisha put both hands over her heart
like she'd taken the bullet personally.
Marisol's groan brought a neighbor's dog into the discussion.

"She does that when the humidity's up," Birdie said,
unmoved, and went down the steps
to slap the horn quiet.
"Short in the relay."

"Birdie. *Birdie.* She what," Aisha called.

"That's all I know," Birdie called back,
a lie so enormous the zinnias leaned away from it.

"Your cousin," Marisol said,
when Birdie had climbed back up.

"My cousin," Birdie agreed.

"And she's doing well?"

"Thriving," said Birdie, and picked up her jar,
and no power assembled on that porch could move her.

---

They broke up the porch a little before eleven.
Aisha left the shaker on the rail,
"as collateral," she said, "against next time,"
and that was how Marisol learned there was a next time.
Birdie's truck grumbled off,
its horn's dignity an open question.
Marisol walked Aisha as far as her turn
and started back alone
up a street of porch lights and cut grass.

The phone buzzed against her hip at the first driveway.

There was a version of her that would have had it out
of her pocket before the buzz finished.
She knew that woman.
She had been that woman as recently as spring.

It buzzed again at the third driveway,
patient as a salesman.

She walked.
Behind her, her own porch light.
Inside, on the kitchen table,
a notebook with three names in it and room for a town.
The fight ran through one man's pen. Fine.
As of four o'clock this afternoon, she owned a pen too.

She let it ride to the corner.
Under the streetlight she took the phone out
on her own schedule, unhurried,
a woman fed, witnessed, and on the record,
and whoever it was got her whole attention,
exactly when she was ready to give it.
