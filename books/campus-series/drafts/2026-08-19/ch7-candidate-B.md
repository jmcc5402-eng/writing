The light in the Checkerboard had gone long and gold,
the kind that came in low across the square now
and lay down the whole length of the pie case,
and Marisol sat in the last of it
with a fork she hadn't used
and a phone face-down by the napkin dispenser.

She had turned it over twice.
Once at four. Once at four forty.
Nothing both times, which was correct, which was healthy,
which was what raising people to leave you on schedule
felt like when it went exactly right.

Her boy was twenty minutes away,
across town under the practice glow,
a number on a roster, a walk-on with a grown man's jaw
and a life she received now in summaries.
Twenty minutes by car. A whole life by every other measure.
Her daughter had left for Bellamy in August
and taken the last of the noise with her.
So Marisol had come downtown on purpose,
to sit with the first fall somewhere the first fall could get pie,
and the one thing she did not want with it was the face.
The face said poor thing. The face came with a head tilt.
The next person who made the face at her
was going to watch her order a third slice
and eat it slowly, maintaining eye contact.

Earlene Tatum slid into the booth uninvited,
which from Earlene wasn't company. It was jurisdiction.

"Two," she said to the counter, holding up fingers.
"One chess, one chocolate."
Then she looked at the phone lying face-down by the pie fork.
"How many times."

"Twice," said Marisol.

"Progress," Earlene said.
"Last month you'd have worn that thing like a pager.
Twice is a woman getting well."

The pies arrived together,
and Earlene dealt out the forks with the authority
of a woman who had buried two mayors
and rated them both honestly at the graveside.

"First fall is the worst," she said.
"I'll tell you that for free.
And the second fall is also the worst.
There's no system. Eat."

"Which one was worse for you?" said Marisol.

"All of them," Earlene said.
"Each one the worst in its own personal way, like mayors.
Chess first. Chocolate's for after you say the true thing."

Marisol ate a bite of chess pie,
which tasted like church basements
and every good potluck of her life,
and said the true thing.

"He's twenty minutes away and I can't text him first.
I made that rule myself. I'd like to report the rule."

"Rule's sound," Earlene said. "Hate it anyway.
That's allowed. That's the whole procedure."

Marisol flipped the phone over, which didn't count,
because this was work.

The board had grown things overnight.
An account nine days old, a sunset for a face,
was calling the House a firetrap in three separate threads,
gravely, regretfully, the word *firetrap* every time,
a man hammering the same nail.
A second account, six days old, agreed with the first
in the first one's own vocabulary.
And in her messages sat three polite strangers,
all strangers, all polite, *so much respect for this group,*
hoping she'd keep things *neutral* on the House question,
all three inside the same hour of the same evening.

Twelve thousand members teach a moderator
what actual people sound like.
Actual people argue sideways. They misspell.
They bring up their cousin and their knee surgery
and leave mad about something two threads over.
These read like they'd been ordered off a menu.

She screenshotted all of it
before she let herself have a feeling about it,
and filed the screenshots where screenshots went,
into the folder that emptied itself into an export
every Sunday morning, next to the coffee.
Old habit. Load-bearing.
Then she answered a locked-out mother
about the parent portal, because the real board
still needed running while somebody salted it.
*Pinned post, my loves. Password page is the second link.*

Opinion, she thought, sitting very still over the chocolate.
This wasn't opinion. Opinion has relatives and typos.
This was built, and built things are paid for,
and everything built here leaned on the same one door.
The whole town had read that November caption
the same morning she had,
and the town had been doing the arithmetic out loud since.
The House stood or fell on one signature,
and everybody knew whose.
One man's pen.

She had watched that man laugh.
Head back, the whole thing, till he had to put a hand out
and lean on the nearest object willing to hold him.
She had seen those hands ride a plane down a length of pine,
easy, listening through the wood,
and she was not going to pretend she hadn't filed that too.
The pen and the laugh did not sit in the same pocket easily.
That was not her problem to reconcile tonight.
Tonight her problem was simpler.
If the fight was going to run through one man's pen,
then somebody ought to be holding the other pen.

She dug the spiral notebook out of her bag,
the fat college-ruled one she kept for lists,
and opened it past the lists to a clean page.
Across the top she wrote *The House's people,*
and under it, in her group-admin hand, three names.

*Ray. Earlene. Verna.*

Earlene read it upside down without apology.

"Good list," she said. "Short. Get more pages."

"It's page one," said Marisol.

Her phone buzzed against the table. Aisha.
*Your porch. Tonight. Don't cook. I have seen the board
and I am not spending my one free evening on the board.*

"Go on home," Earlene said, collecting both plates,
neither of which she had finished, both of which she took.
"You've got company coming and I've got eggs in the truck."

In the car, Marisol set the notebook on the passenger seat,
where a person would sit,
and then, for no reason she was prepared to state,
she opened the glove box.
The wrench still wasn't in it.
It was on a workbench across town,
being owed to her at an interest rate nobody had set,
and she looked at the empty spot where it used to live
a good deal longer than an empty spot deserves.

Two blasts of a horn broke it up.
The flower truck rolled past with its buckets swaying,
and Birdie leaned across the cab,
pointed at her like a summons, and mouthed one word.
*Tonight.*

Marisol closed the glove box and drove home.

---

The dark came earlier now, and softer,
and the porch light had a real job again for the first time
since spring.

Dr. Aisha Cole came up the walk
while the sky still held a last rim of light,
carrying a cocktail shaker already sweating cold,
climbing the steps with the stride of a woman
off shift and done apologizing for it.

"Espresso martinis," she said. "Made correctly, at my house,
because I have met your blender.
And before you start, I know what's on that board,
and I did not bring these to discuss it."

Birdie Pham arrived on foot two minutes behind her,
zinnias in a coffee can, the can still wearing its label.
She set them on the porch rail,
where they burned orange under the bulb,
and took the porch swing
as if it had been assigned to her years ago.

Marisol went inside for real glasses,
because they were not drinking Aisha's work out of plastic.
On the front of the fridge hung the calendar,
flipped ahead to June, one date circled twice in blue pen.
She took the glasses down from the shelf and went back out.

Aisha poured with a level eye and handed them around.

"I'm not going to talk about the board," Marisol said.

"Good," said Aisha.

"I'm going to talk about this."
She took the spiral notebook off the rail
and turned it so they could see page one.
"The House's people. All of them, in their own words,
with their names on it, on the record.
What that place was to them, said where it counts.
I've got my Saturdays and I'm putting every one of them
behind it, as many as it takes."

The porch considered that.

"Say it again," Aisha said.

"Why?"

"Because now there are witnesses.
That's what porches are for. Say it again."

Marisol said it again.
It sounded less like an idea the second time
and more like a schedule.

"The truck's yours whenever you want it," Birdie said.
"People will tell a flower truck anything."

"And wear the blue dress when you go asking," said Aisha.
"People sign things for that dress. I've seen it happen."

Somewhere in the second round, Aisha got onto the coach.

"There is a coach on that campus," she said,
"who has appealed every call I have made this season.
Appealed. As if I'm a referee.
As if medicine comes with a rebuttal window.
He called my office twice on Friday. Twice."

"Is he handsome?" said Birdie.

"He is a nuisance," said Aisha.

"That's not a no," said Marisol.

"It's a clinical finding," said Aisha,
and topped off her own glass first,
which everyone saw and nobody mentioned.

"Speaking of findings," said Marisol, "how's the peach man?"

Birdie became suddenly and completely interested
in rotating the coffee can a quarter turn,
as if zinnias had a correct facing.

"Curb Market's open Saturday," she said, to the zinnias.

"That's not a no either," said Aisha.

"It's not an anything," said Birdie,
and it certainly wasn't a no.

"And the wrench?" said Aisha. "Any word on the wrench?"

"No," said Marisol, smiling at a point
somewhere past Birdie's ear. "No word on the wrench."

"Mm," said Aisha, and was merciful, and let it lie.

The streetlights were all the light left on the square side
of the sky when Aisha set down her glass
and squared up like a woman with an agenda item.

"Since nobody here has anything to confess," she said,
"I have one. My college roommate has a cousin
down in Birmingham."

"Real cousin?" said Birdie.

"Church treasurer," said Aisha, which wasn't the question.
"Fifty-one. Widowed, decorous, sings alto.
She kept her husband's two horses after,
couldn't bring herself to sell them,
which means every six weeks she needs a farrier."

"A what?" said Birdie.

"Shoes horses," said Aisha. "Forearms. You understand."

"Tell it proper," said Marisol, settling in.
"What does he look like?"

"Gray at the temples," said Aisha. "Leather apron.
Forearms, as established.
Smells like smoke and clean iron,
and he talks to the horses in a voice she says
she felt through the fence rail.
So the cousin develops an interest in hoof care
she has never once had in her life.
Every six weeks, lemonade down to the barn. Two glasses.
Six visits of lemonade and lay-ministry small talk.
And on the seventh, she walks down there,
and he takes the glass, and drinks the whole thing
looking straight at her, and sets it on the post,
and reaches back and pulls that apron over his head
by the neck strap, one hand,
and the apron hits the gravel,
and under it he is wearing absolutely no—"

The phone on the rail lit up.

Every head on that porch turned.
On the screen, at one minute past eleven,
sat a payment request from her boy.
Eighteen dollars and change.
Memo: *laundry. also wings. love you.*

The porch groaned like bleachers.

"Go back," said Birdie. "Finish it. He was wearing
absolutely no *what*?"

"The moment has passed," said Aisha,
pouring out the last of the shaker with ceremony.
"Birmingham protects its own."

"Give me the cousin's number, then," said Birdie.

"She's a very private person," said Aisha.
"Church treasurer."

Marisol, for her part, had already screenshotted the request,
memo and all, before she paid it,
which she then also did, immediately, in full, like a fool,
smiling at the screen with her whole face.
It was the first word out of him all day,
and it was an invoice, and she'd have paid double.

"Look at her," said Aisha.

"I know," said Birdie.

"Eighteen dollars," said Marisol. "For wings. My son."
Nobody on the porch believed she was complaining,
least of all Marisol.

---

When the shaker was empty
and the coffee can had been granted a permanent post
on the rail, Marisol walked them to the end of the block,
because it seemed to her a first wine night
ought to close with an escort.

"Saturday, then," said Aisha, and it was not a question.

"Truck's ready when you are," said Birdie.

The walk back was hers alone,
porch lights strung down the street,
the air gone dry at the edges,
carrying that first held-breath smell
the year gets when it starts packing.

Halfway down the block, the phone buzzed against her hip.
A year ago she'd have had it out
before the second buzz.
She let it ride.

It buzzed again, patient, unhurried, whoever it was.
She let it ride to the corner,
and at the corner, under the streetlight,
she took it out and turned it over in her own good time,
a woman with a page-one notebook, two witnesses,
and a Saturday already spoken for.
