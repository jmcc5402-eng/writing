# Chapter 5 — The Third Step

On Sundays, Ashford put itself on silent.

Even the parents' group kept the sabbath, more or less.
Twelve thousand people dropped to a murmur of casserole photos,
and one mother asked about parking anyway, on principle.
Marisol answered her from the porch steps, *pinned post, my love,*
put the phone in her pocket, and went walking,
because a year of Sundays had taught her the arithmetic.
Her house was loudest on the day the town went quietest.
The cure for a house like that was a town like this one.

She walked downhill with no particular route in mind,
which was a lie she renewed weekly.
Every walk she took anymore bent toward Millrow Street,
past the church lot with the potluck sign-up sheet on the door
and the app version taped up under it, unloved,
down into the long shade of the live oaks,
where the light came through in loose change on the sidewalk
and the cicadas held one note and meant it.
The heat had late summer's manners now,
resting on her arms instead of standing on her neck.

She heard the House before she let herself look at it.
Not the House itself. The House had stood back behind its two oaks
for a hundred years without once raising its voice.
What she heard was a long, patient shearing,
a hush with an edge on it, stroke after stroke,
coming through the open front door.
On the bottom porch step sat the mum,
big as a dryer drum, claimed by nobody, known by everybody.

She had no business following that sound.
She followed it up the walk.

The front hall was cool and smelled of fresh-cut wood.
A hundred years of Sundays had been walked into the floorboards,
and the stairwell light came down in one wide piece
and lay across the shoulders of Cal Sutter,
who knelt on the third step of the staircase with a wood plane,
on a Sunday, making that sound.
She gave the shoulders the moment they had, frankly, earned.

"The door was open," she said.

"It's rude to the hinges, shutting it all day," he said,
without looking up. "Mind the shavings."

"I heard it from the street." She stayed near the door,
minding the shavings.
"It sounded like somebody ironing a tree."

"That's close to the actual physics," he said, and kept planing.

She got about four more feet into the hall
before the wall stopped her like a hand on the shoulder.

Photographs climbed the staircase wall the whole way up,
dozens of them, small and framed and level as a promise,
and every one of them was the same photograph.
Different frames. Different dresses, different haircuts,
different wars keeping time in the background.
A couple on a staircase, and the staircase was this staircase,
and every couple, all hundred years of them,
stood on the same third step.

"Oh," said Marisol, with feeling.

"Mm," said Cal.

"They're all standing in the same spot."

"Third step," he said. "Every time."

"Who are they?" She came closer to the bottom frames.
"Tell me. I want all of them."

He named them without looking up.

"Third frame, that's Verna and her Mr. Poteat.
Magnolia Court sign was a week old when he asked her.
She'll give you the whole day if you let her.
Eat first. It runs three hours with intermission."

"A week old," Marisol said.
"You can date a proposal by signage."

"Around here you can date most things by signage."

"And them? With the — are those keys?"

"The Phams. He came straight off a shift at the diner to ask her.
Kept the keys on his belt so she'd know what she was getting."

"Efficient," she said, meaning it as high praise.

"Highest kind there is."

"Who takes the picture?"

"Whoever's standing in the hall when it happens."

"And if nobody's standing in the hall?"

"Somebody always is."

He tipped his chin at a frame without lifting his eyes.
"Soldier there had two weeks before he shipped out.
They took them." The plane paused, one second, and went on.
"All fourteen days."

Marisol looked at the girl in that frame for a while,
at the grip she had on the soldier's sleeve,
and put the ache down gently where she could find it again.

"You know every frame on this wall."

"Most of the town's up there," he said. "Saves on introductions."

He went on up the wall for her, a name to every frame,
a hundred years of yes in the same patient voice,
until one photo near the top, plain frame, high corner,
that he named right past without breaking stride,
the same level voice on both sides of it.

Marisol had spent two years and change
learning what a level voice can cost.
She didn't ask.

"So what happened to this one?" she said instead,
coming to the foot of the stairs.
"The step. It's gone pale."

"Knees happened to it."
He sat back on his heels and ran a thumb across the tread,
and she watched the forearm that came with the thumb,
which had spent the whole summer outdoors and wore it well,
and made no apology to herself for watching.
"A hundred years of men, one knee, same spot.
Wood gives up about an eighth of an inch a generation.
I keep it level, so the next fella kneels true
and doesn't blame the house for his balance."

"You've done the math."

"Somebody had to."

"On a Sunday."

"Job security," he said.

The plane made one more pass.
The lie lay there in the shavings between them,
and neither of them picked it up.

He looked up at her then, properly, for the first time,
and she looked back and did not deploy anything clever.
His hair had taken on more sun since the welcome tent,
the gray at his temples doing quiet advance work,
and the look held one beat past where looks let go.

Laughter came up the walk outside and broke it.

Through the open door, past the oaks, two of the town went by,
a straw hat and a yellow sundress, slow, and getting slower.
The hat said something that folded the sundress in half,
and the sundress came up laughing and took the hat's arm,
and the two of them tacked on up the hill and out of the doorframe,
in no more hurry than the light.
Marisol never did see who they were.
The doorway wasn't telling.

"Careful of that doorway," Cal said, back at his tread.

"Does it do that?"

"Frames things."

She let him keep the last word, mostly because it was good,
and drifted to the hall table to give her eyes somewhere neutral.

The guest book lay open there, fat with signatures,
a ballpoint on a ribbon beside it.
She turned back through the pages, idle, being polite to a book,
and her own handwriting came up off the paper at her.

Her own name, in her own hand,
steadier on the page than she had been anywhere that day,
with *drop-off day* written in the column for occasions
and a little flourish on the y she had no memory of making.

That day had been eleven buildings long.
It had been sunglasses and move-in bins
and a folding cart with one shot wheel,
lemonade somebody put in one hand and a pen in the other,
and she had signed where the pen was pointed
and gone back out into the heat to carry things.
She had stood in this hall before, was what the book was saying.
The House had had her name a whole year before the town did.

Her phone was out before she decided anything about it.
That was a settled reflex now, the kids off in their own semesters,
the group always hungry, every good minute photographed on arrival.
She took the picture, the staircase wall, all those couples,
and her thumb found the family thread on its own,
and then she put the phone away with the picture still inside,
unsent, where it would keep.

Some things you send. Some things you stand on.

"Can I stand on it?" she said. "Your step. Or is it still wet?"

"It's wood," Cal said, "not paint."

"You'd be surprised what I don't know about wood."

"I would not," he said, and gathered the plane
and stood down off the staircase to let her by.

She went up two steps and stood on the third,
where the wood was palest, smooth as river stone underfoot,
and set her hand on the rail where the finish was thinnest,
polished for her in advance by a hundred years of hands.

From the third step you could see the whole front hall,
the wide light, the open door, the oaks holding the street,
and Marisol understood that a hundred years of people
had stood exactly here, looking exactly there,
in the best minute of their lives so far.

She stood on it one whole minute, and counted it honest.
The hall did not hurry her.
Outside, the cicadas kept the note going.
If anyone had asked, she would have said
she was inspecting the work, which was, for the record, level.

Nobody asked.
Cal had found something to do with the dustpan and the shavings
that took, to the second, exactly that long.

"Thank you for the names," she said at the door,
smiling at a point somewhere past his ear.

"Wasn't me. The wall keeps them. I just dust it."

"The step is good work."

"The step is a hundred years of other people's good work."
He was already kneeling on it again, plane in hand.
"I keep it honest."

"And the hinges?"

"Hinges are a Tuesday problem."

"It's rude to them, you know," she said, going out.
"Shutting doors."

"So I hear," said Cal, and the long, patient sound of the plane
started up behind her and followed her partway down the walk.

She went out through the gate and under the oaks,
into the loose-change light and the cicadas' one long note.
Her pocket buzzed once at the gate, the group wanting
something small. She would answer it from her own porch.

She had twelve thousand people in that pocket
who would have loved every word of this,
the wall, the step, the man kneeling on it on his day off,
and a picture already waiting in her phone.

She walked home the long way and told nobody at all.
The secret kept pace beside her the whole way,
good company, the best she'd had in a year,
and that, like most things lately, she had decided on purpose,
and in her own favor.
