"—and the second one is also the worst," Earlene said,
setting down the fork, "so you may as well eat."

Marisol had not ordered pie.
Earlene Tatum had slid into the booth uninvited
somewhere between Marisol's first coffee and her second,
flagged the counter without raising her voice,
and ordered two, chess and peach, one fork,
with the full authority of a woman who has buried two mayors.

"If this is sympathy," Marisol said, "I'd rather have the check."

"It isn't. One pie is sympathy." Earlene squared the plates.
"Two is a prescription."

The Checkerboard was in its mid-morning voice,
griddle down to a murmur,
coffee going strong into a second shift.
The Liars' Table had adjourned hours ago,
but its six a.m. opinions were still drying on the air.
The specials card by the napkin box offered oat milk,
in small print, under protest.

The phone lay face-down by the pie fork,
where it had lain all morning.
She had turned it over twice since seven.
Once for the parents' group,
once for an eleven p.m. Venmo request
that had aged overnight into a good story.
Neither time for the thing she was not checking it for.

"Twice," Earlene said, nodding at it.
"I've been counting since I sat down.
Last month you'd have worn a groove in that table.
That's progress, sugar, and I'm scoring it."

"You're scoring my grief."

"Somebody has to. You're doing fine."

Her boy was twenty minutes up the road,
a walk-on number under the stadium glow,
twenty minutes and a whole life away.
The Bellamy parents' GroupMe melted down about the dining hall
on schedule, reliably,
which was how she knew her daughter was eating.
The first fall with both of them gone had a sound,
and the sound was a phone not buzzing on a diner table.

She ate the pie. Both pies, in the end.
It helped, which she would have denied under oath.

Earlene stayed for one cup,
reported on eggs and dahlias in the tone
other people used for weather,
gave the phone a last approving glance,
and slid back out the same as she'd come, unannounced,
the town's business seen to.

Marisol turned the phone over a third time, on her own terms,
and opened the board.

Since Friday, the group had grown strangers.

Three new accounts, no faces, members since Friday,
every one of them suddenly passionate about "that firetrap."
The same word in three mouths.
The same odd hyphen in the same place, twice.
One of them had replied to the other two, agreeably,
at one in the morning,
which was not an hour at which twelve thousand parents
agreed on anything.

And in her messages, two strangers being polite at her.
*Some of us feel the group should stay neutral on town matters.
Just a thought!*
First names she could not attach to a single kid,
in a group where she knew the regulars by their kids.

She screenshotted all of it without deciding to.
The reflex did not sort;
it had screenshotted move-in day and the pinned thank-yous too.
The folder already existed.
It went out every Sunday with the coffee,
and this Sunday it would go out heavier.

She deleted nobody.
She pinned the parking thread back to the top where it belonged —
*my loves, the gravel lot is still free and still gravel* —
and left the strangers sitting where she could see them.

Then she did what the whole town had been doing since Friday
and read the caption again.
November.
And in the middle of it the one name,
the name the whole thing waited on.

She was still looking at the name when the refill came around,
and she had to move the phone to make room for the cup,
and that broke it.

Nobody pays to argue with a town's opinion.
Opinion was free; the town made its own fresh daily.
You paid when opinion was not the thing in your way.
She looked at the three Friday accounts,
and the two polite strangers,
and the one name under the November date,
and understood the shape of it whole.
The fight was not about what anybody thought.
The fight ran through one man's pen.

And she had watched the man laugh.
Two Saturdays ago, at the market crossing,
head back, the whole laugh,
at something she had not been close enough to hear,
and she had changed course on the sidewalk
to be nearer the sound.
That was now a complication.
She filed it as one,
and did not let it be more than that
at a diner table on a Tuesday.

There was a spiral notebook in her tote, college-ruled,
bought in August out of pure muscle memory
in an aisle full of mothers,
before she remembered there was nobody left to need it.
She uncapped a pen and gave the notebook a job.

*The House's people,* she wrote at the top of page one.
Under it, three names to start.
Ray. Earlene. Verna.

The peach pie was gone. The chess pie was evidence.
She paid for both,
tipped like a woman celebrating something,
and took the notebook home.

The cork was halfway out and losing graciously
by the time Aisha got to the part about the coach.

"Every call," she said.
"Every single call I make, here he comes across the grass
with the visor in his hand
like I personally canceled football."

Nobody had called it anything.
Dr. Aisha Cole had texted *porch?* at four o'clock,
Birdie Pham had answered with a thumbs-up
and a photograph of zinnias,
and by dark the porch had a cooler on it,
canned espresso martinis lying in the ice
alongside the domestics like exchange students,
and a coffee can of zinnias holding the rail,
doing their best, which was considerable.

"What kind of call," Birdie said, from inside her wine.

"A medical ruling," Aisha said,
with the dignity of her entire profession,
"the specifics of which are private."

"That's the third time he's come up since the cooler opened,"
Birdie said, to the zinnias.

"Because the man is a plague.
You don't bring up a plague, you report it."

Marisol got the cork the rest of the way out
and filled Birdie's glass. "What's his name?"

"Absolutely not," Aisha said,
and held up her can for a toast she had not earned.

Marisol and Birdie held a short conversation about it
with their eyebrows.

"I can see you both," Aisha said.
"Fine. How's the peach man, Birdie."

"The Curb Market has a great many vendors," Birdie said.

"He holds a flat back for her," Aisha told Marisol.
"Under the table like contraband.
Best peaches in the county, reserved like a hotel room."

"Peaches bruise," said Birdie, with enormous dignity,
"and that is the whole of it."

It was not a denial,
and everyone on the porch heard that it wasn't,
including Birdie,
who smiled into her glass and watered nothing down.

"And you," Aisha said, turning on Marisol.
"You look disgusting, by the way. Glowing. On a Tuesday.
Meanwhile your board is on fire, or claiming to be.
I have no patience for it. Grown strangers inventing fires.
What are you going to do about it?"

Marisol went in for the notebook.
The kitchen light was on over the sink.
On the fridge, the calendar hung open to the wrong month,
June, one date circled twice in the same blue pen.
She took the notebook off the counter
and let the screen door bang behind her on the way out.

She set it on the crate between the glasses
and opened it to page one.

"The House's people," she said.
"Everybody who ever got something from that place,
on the record, out loud, names attached.
Ray. Earlene. Verna. That's page one.
I've got my Saturdays,
and I run parking for twelve thousand people,
so I believe I can run this."

"Say it again," Aisha said.

"Why?"

"Because now there are witnesses,
and you don't get to shrink it later.
Wording matters. Say it the same."

She said it the same.
Birdie reached over and squared the notebook
to the edge of the crate,
which from Birdie was a notarized seal.

"And the pen," Aisha said. "The name on that caption.
That's your wrench man."

"Cal Sutter," Marisol said, "is the complication."

"You like him," Birdie said. It was not a question.
Birdie dealt in facts, same as Earlene,
and said fewer of them out loud,
which made the ones she said count double.

"I've watched him laugh," Marisol said,
"which is not the same thing at all,"
and smiled at a point somewhere past Birdie's ear.

"Mm," said Aisha, into her can.

She had seen his hands on a plane once,
taking one long curl off a board's edge,
patient, certain, nothing wasted,
and had lost the thread of a conversation
she herself was having at the time.
The name the whole thing waited on had those hands.
That was the complication, entire,
and nobody on the porch needed it spelled.

"Which reminds me," Aisha said, "of my cousin."

"Which cousin," Marisol said.

"Birmingham."

Birdie set down her glass and folded her hands like church.
"Tell it proper."

"It was July," Aisha said. "You remember July.
Her air conditioner died on the hottest Sunday of the year,
and the warranty company sent a man out same day.
Big. Quiet. Sunburned across the back of the neck.
Forearms from carrying condenser units up stairs for a living."

"What was she wearing," Birdie said. "Proper means proper."

"The sundress with the broken zipper,
because it was a hundred and four
and she had given up on society.
So the man fixes the unit in forty minutes
and says maybe ten words the whole time.
Then the cold air comes back on.
All at once. Every vent in the house at the same time.
And my cousin stood in the middle of her hallway
in that sundress in that first cold air
and made a sound she says the man felt in his knees."

Marisol had stopped pretending to drink her wine.

"He asks can he check the airflow in the back rooms.
She says he can check the airflow anywhere he likes.
And she swears to me, hand on heart,
that when his shirt came off over his head
and hit that hallway floor, the whole house went—"
and the biggest moth in the county,
drunk all evening on the porch light,
chose that exact syllable
to fly directly into Aisha's open mouth.

What followed was not dignified.
Aisha came out of her chair
spitting and swatting in two registers,
the chair went over,
Birdie screamed and then laughed
until the coffee can was in danger,
and the moth blundered off into the dark,
the only soul present who would ever know
how the house went.

"Finish it," Birdie said, when she could speak.
"The house went WHAT."

"The moment's gone," Aisha said,
with the ruins of her dignity,
holding her can up to the porch light
to check it for passengers.
"Ask the moth."

The groan that went up ran the length of the porch
and a fair way down the block.

"There is no cousin," Marisol said.

"There is a cousin," Aisha said,
"and she is thriving,
and her electric bill has never been lower,
because that man came back to check the airflow
every Sunday in August."

"On warranty?" Birdie said.

"Honey, everything's covered."

---

They went down the front walk at half past eleven,
still relitigating the existence of the cousin.
Birdie had left the truck around the corner past the hydrant,
and Aisha was walking,
on the theory that the cooler counted as cardio.
Marisol went with them as far as the corner
because the night was good
and she was in no hurry to hand it back.

The phone buzzed in her pocket at the bottom of the walk.
She let it ride.
It buzzed once more at the hedge
and then thought better of itself,
and she kept walking, one witness on either side,
all the way to the corner,
where the flower truck coughed twice and caught
and Aisha hoisted the cooler in salute
and took her cardio up the hill.

Marisol stood under the streetlight
until the taillights turned.
Then she walked back alone to her own porch,
where the light was on and the glasses were still out
and the zinnias held the rail,
and page one lay open on the crate,
right where she had said it out loud.
