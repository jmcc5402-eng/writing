# Chapter 6 — Table Nine

Cal had a plan for the gala. Three hands, then home.
Boyd's, because November made Boyd's hand necessary.
Tick Moran's, because a hand Tick didn't get to shake
got discussed on the radio.
Ray's, because Ray was carving brisket at the back of the ballroom,
and a man wanted to be remembered at the carving station.
Twenty minutes, all in. He parked the truck nose-out.

The ballroom at the event center had been his problem since noon.
His crew had hung the drape, taped the cable runs,
and leveled the lectern with a folded shim.
He knew the room like his own pockets.
He also knew, without deciding to, where Hartwell House stood.
North-northwest. Past the coat check, then a mile of dark.
He knew it from every chair in the building, the same as exits.
He left that fact alone and went to find the seating chart.

The chart had him at table nine.
That was wrong. He'd sent his reply late and asked for the back.
Facilities sat by the kitchen doors, when it sat at all.
He read the chart twice.
Table nine stood center of the room, in the good light,
and somebody had made him a place card. Heavy stock, tidy capitals,
CAL SUTTER, waiting at a plate like it had always been there.

"Ray," he said, at the carving station. One hand shaken,
one item off the list. "Who does the seating?"

"Committee," Ray said. He didn't look up from the brisket.

"Which committee?"

"There's always a committee." Ray laid a slice on Cal's plate,
which closed the matter as far as Ray was concerned.

Table nine had a jar of Birdie Pham's zinnias in the middle
and faces Cal knew around the rim.
Earlene Tatum, in earrings that meant business.
Verna Poteat, holding the drink menu at arm's length.
Sonny Dillard, scrubbed pink, in the sport coat he wore
to weddings and inspections.
And Marisol.

The dress was dark red, and it had opinions,
and every one of them was correct.
Cal looked at the zinnias.

"Cal Sutter," Marisol said, reading his card off the table
before he'd touched his chair. "Table nine drew the handyman.
Sit down. Everything here is broken."

"Nothing here is broken," Cal said. "I checked at noon."

"The tea's unsweet," Verna said, "and my chair rocks."

Cal was under the table before his jacket hit the chairback.
A glide had backed itself out of the chair's foot.
He gave it a half-turn and came up
to find the whole table watching.

"Well," Earlene said. "Table nine wins."

"He does this everywhere," Marisol told the table.
"He fixed my tent in August. Uninvited."

"Invited by the tent," Cal said, and sat down,
which put the dress at his left elbow for the duration.
He made a private note to look at the lectern a lot.

Dinner came out in waves, and the table went to work on it.
Sonny asked Verna if she had any rooms left for parents' weekend.

"I've got a waiting list for my waiting list," Verna said.
"Booked two falls deep. I could charge triple and they'd thank me.
I charge what I charge."

"Which is why they send you flowers," Marisol said.

Earlene came back from the bar with something dark
in a cocktail glass, foam riding on top.
"They've got espresso martinis up there," she announced.
"Right in the cooler next to the domestics.
It's coffee that's been somewhere. Verna, you want one?"

"I want to watch you have one," Verna said.

"It's got coffee in it," Earlene said. "It's practical."

"So's a mule," Verna said. "I don't ride those at night either."

Marisol ran the table without appearing to run anything.
Verna's tea got sweet somewhere between courses.
Sonny got asked one question about the new tanker truck
and was happy for ten straight minutes.
Earlene got the first cut of cake without asking for it.
Nobody saw a thing get done, and everything got done.
Cal saw. He was trying to look at anything else,
which is how a man ends up cataloging.

She talked with her hands.
Quick hands, ringless, sure of their airspace.
He filed them under problems.
Her laugh started low and climbed.
He heard it four times before the speeches
and knew the count, which was also a problem.
For cover he read the front of the program twice.
Order of speakers. Menu. A word of welcome in serif type.
He never got as far as the back page.

Around them the room ran on town time.
Odell Fountain worked the aisle between tables,
shaking hands he'd barbered over for forty years.
Birdie Pham stood in the service doorway in her good shirt,
counting her zinnias jar by jar, satisfied with every one.

"Delmar's has a clipboard by the register now," Earlene reported,
in the tone she used for fronts moving in.
"Save the Hartwell House. Page and a half of names."

"Page and a half," Marisol said. "I'm name three."

"It's a house," Cal said. Because apparently he was going to do this.

Marisol turned in her chair. The dress came with her.
"It is not a house. You stood in that front hall.
You saw the photo wall. Half this ballroom is up on it."

"I saw the sill under the photo wall," Cal said.
"You could plant beans in it. Gutters are gone.
The glazing's loose. The porch has dropped an inch since spring,
and the roof has two honest winters left in it."

"You're describing lumber. I'm describing a building."

"We're not talking about the same building," Cal said.

"No," she said, delighted, "we are not."

Neither of them was winning. Neither of them stopped.
He topped off her tea in the middle of disagreeing with her
and didn't notice he'd done it. She let him.
He was enjoying himself. He'd deal with that later.

"You know those gutters personally," she said, slowing down,
narrowing in. "You said 'glazing.' Out loud. At a party.
That's a lot of inventory for a man who's only counting boards."

"Eleven," Cal said.

"Eleven what?"

"Windows on the front elevation. Original glass in nine of them."
He put his fork down. "That's all I'm saying about it."

"That is the most romantic thing said at this table all night,"
Marisol told the centerpiece.

"It's arithmetic," Cal said.

"Careful, Sutter. You're getting interesting."

"Mm," Earlene said, into her martini.

"I keep thinking about that photo wall," Marisol said,
quieter now, just to him.
"Everybody in town is up there twice. Young, and then not."
She turned her glass a quarter-turn and left it there.

Cal looked at the zinnias again. They didn't help.
He cut her a piece of the cake instead, and she took it,
and neither of them said anything else about front halls.

His twenty minutes had died somewhere before the salads.
He hadn't noticed the funeral.
He hung his jacket over the chairback and stayed.

At eight Tick Moran took the lectern and turned the radio voice on.
"Folks, I talk for a living," Tick said,
"so believe me when I tell you the next man
needs no introduction, which has never once stopped me
from giving one. Rex Boyd."

Boyd came up in a jacket that cost what jackets cost
when they're pretending they don't.
He didn't stand behind the lectern. He stood beside it.

"I've been rich a while," Boyd said, in the same warm register
he'd have used on a jury or a grandbaby.
"But every good thing I've ever had
started on a Saturday in this town."

He talked for six minutes and never raised his voice.
He thanked mothers by their first names.
"You can buy most things," Boyd said. "I've checked.
You cannot buy a hometown."
He got Verna to dab one eye, which Cal would not have bet on.
Then he opened one hand toward the town outside the walls.

"You'll see drawings soon enough. Drawings are for architects.
Tonight I just want to give the thing its name.
We're going to build this town a front door,
and we're going to call it the Gateway."

The room came up out of its chairs.
"Pending trustee approval at the November board meeting,"
Boyd added, palms up, warm as the rest of it,
fine print delivered like a blessing.

Cal applauded the exact amount courtesy required.
The weight under his chair stayed put.
A survey, ordered. A signature, wanted, before the trustees met.
Twelve names on a winter layoff list
that Boyd's money would quietly erase.
He ran the arithmetic again. It came out the same as always.
He looked north-northwest at nothing, then made himself stop.

The printed programs lay by every plate, Boyd's name in serif
on the front. The back page carried the fine print.
Sponsors, committees, and near the bottom,
"digital engagement: [TK: sock-vendor name] Media LLC,"
whose welcome line read "ya'll come."
Nobody at table nine turned the program over.
Nobody in the ballroom did.

Marisol's phone had lain face down all evening, patient.
She flipped it during the applause and smiled at her lap.
Some thank-you from the parents' group, by the look of it.
She read it twice. Then her thumbs made a quick two-button pinch,
and the nice thing got saved, filed away like a receipt.
He'd watched her do that twice already tonight.
Even the happy ones got saved. Especially those.

Somewhere in the after-speech noise she looked over at him,
and he was already looking, which he would have denied,
and neither of them quit on schedule.
It went a beat long. Then another beat.

"Cal, honey," Verna said, "are you going to eat that cake
or guard it?"

"Guard it," Cal said, and ate it.

The room loosened after that. Jackets found chairbacks.
Down at the bar, two strangers were hitting it off,
a young woman from the Foundation's table
and a fellow from the donor office, thirty-some apiece,
each holding a drink they had clearly swapped on purpose,
standing a half-step closer than the bar required.
Nobody was watching them. That was probably for the best.

The gala thinned by degrees. Sonny drove Verna home.
Earlene made one last pass of the room and left with more news
than she'd arrived with, which was the point of Earlene.

"Table nine," Marisol said, standing, gathering her wrap.
"Same time next gala?"

"I'll check with the committee," Cal said.

"You do that." And the dress left, with all its opinions,
and took her along.

Cal walked the room once, because it was his room.
He squared the lectern. He kicked a cable flat.
At table nine the place cards still stood at their plates,
tidy capitals, every name where somebody had decided it should be.
He picked up his own. He could not have testified to a reason.
It went into his jacket pocket, and he went out to the truck.

The lot had mostly emptied. The truck sat nose-out,
the whole night rigged for a quick exit he'd never used.
North-northwest, past the fence line, the House stood in the dark
where it always stood. He didn't look. He knew anyway.

The card weighed nothing at all in his pocket.
He noticed it the whole way home.
