Cal owned one jacket that buttoned all the way up,
and he was in it, which meant a funeral or a fundraiser.
Tonight had valet parking, so.

The plan was three handshakes.
He'd built the route in the truck on the drive over.
Through the main doors of the function room at [TK: venue],
down the right-hand wall, hand one, hand two, hand three,
out through the kitchen corridor before anybody reached a podium.
Twenty minutes. He'd done longer jobs standing up in worse shoes.

The place-card table killed the route at the door.

The cards stood in alphabet rows on a white cloth,
tented card stock in a serious font,
and SUTTER, CAL had a nine on it in gold ink.
He read it twice.
Facilities sat back by the kitchen at these things,
close to the breakers and the mop sink,
and he'd have bet the truck on a chair he could leave from
sideways. Nine was front and center. Donor country.
Close enough to the podium to count coat buttons.

Cards got shuffled. Somebody's assistant had had a long week.
He picked his up like a work order he intended to dispute,
and went in.

Hand one happened at the silent auction,
over a golf weekend he had no plans to bid on.
Hand two found him at the bar and cost him one club soda.
He was forty feet from hand three and gaining
when Birdie Pham crossed his bow with an armload of zinnias
taller than she was and said, "Left or right, Cal. Pick fast."

"Left," Cal said, and picked it.

By the time the flowers had gone by, the room had turned over.
Ray was calling folks to tables in the voice he used at Delmar's
for order up, and the current took Cal to table nine
whether he'd finished arguing with it or not.

Earlene Tatum was already seated across the table,
purse in her lap like a copilot.

"Cal Sutter," Earlene said, in the tone she used for fronts
moving in. "They've got you up front this year."

"Appears so," Cal said.

Odell Fountain sat down next, in a bow tie with corners on it,
and Verna Poteat took a chair at the next table over
and turned it a few degrees so as to miss nothing.
Then the room did the thing rooms do,
a half-step drop in every conversation at once,
and Cal looked up along with everybody else.

Marisol crossed the floor in a dark red dress that had opinions,
and the room moved as one body to second them.
She knew it, too. She walked like the noise belonged to her,
thanked a waiter by name, touched Verna's shoulder in passing,
and pulled out the chair next to Cal's
before he'd decided whether standing up was a statement.

"Table nine," Marisol said, reading his card, and then him.
"Cal Sutter, in a jacket that buttons."

"It's the same jacket for funerals," Cal said.
"Try not to read into it."

"Too late. I've read into it. There's a whole book club."
She sat, and her perfume arrived a half second behind her,
and he fixed his eyes on the zinnias
like a man renewing a long commitment to flowers.

From chair four at table nine, the House sat off his right
shoulder, west and a little north, on the far side of the wall
and the parking lot and the dark.
He checked without deciding to. He always checked.
He'd checked at funerals.
He eased his chair an inch around when he sat down,
and could have told you exactly why, and didn't.

Ray's crew ran the buffet like a shift change,
and the argument got to the House by way of the cornbread.

"I keep thinking about that front hall," Marisol said.
Not to the table. Just to him, and quieter.
"That airplane on the wall. Somebody sanded that by hand
and hung it where you'd see it coming down the stairs.
There are photographs going up that staircase
like a family climbed it one year at a time."

"There's also a step that moved under your foot," Cal said.

"It creaked."

"It moved. Different word. Different bill."

"See, there it is." She turned in her chair to face him,
and her hands came into it, quick, sure hands
that drew the whole house in the air between them,
roofline, porch, hall.
"I say photographs, you say bill.
We stood in the same building for twenty minutes
and toured two different properties."

"Mine has a roof that runs six figures
before you find out what's under it."

"Mine has a plane hanging on fishing line."

"Fishing line isn't load-bearing."

"Nothing in your world gets to just be loved, does it," she said.
"It all has to hold weight."

"Everything holds weight," Cal said. "That's the job."

She narrowed her eyes at him.
It was not an unfriendly narrowing.

"You're enjoying this," Marisol said.

"I'm eating my dinner."

"You are having the time of your life
arguing with me about a staircase."

"It's good cornbread," Cal said,
and she laughed, and there it was, the laugh,
up from the chest and unconcerned with witnesses,
heads turning two tables out.
He looked at his plate and put the laugh on the list.

He had a system for problems.
Name it, size it, price it, get it scheduled.
Her hands had gone on the list somewhere around the salad,
the quick draw of them, a house built in the air.
The laugh went on at the cornbread.
The dress had been on since the door.
Nothing on the list would take a price or a crew,
and he kept the list anyway.
A man likes his systems, even the ones that are failing.

And she ran the table. That was the other thing.
Nothing you could point at.
Verna's coffee got warmed because Marisol caught a waiter's eye
without breaking her own sentence.
Odell got asked the one question about barbering
that opened him up like a hymnal.
A couple stranded with full plates and no chairs
got waved in and introduced around
before their napkins were down.
Cal ran crews for a living. He knew conducting when he saw it,
and he respected the work,
and wished briefly that she would stop being also this.

Tick Moran took the podium in his 1340 voice.
"You all know I talk for a living," Tick said,
"so I'll be brief, and not one of you believes me."
He was, roughly, and then the lights came down a notch,
and Rex Boyd didn't take the podium so much as arrive at it.

Expensively casual. Jacket open. No notes.
The room leaned in, because his volume asked it to.
He never raised his voice, that man.
One warm register for weather, for money, for everything.

"I'm not going to show you drawings tonight," Boyd said.
"Drawings come later. Drawings are for engineers,
and I love engineers, but tonight isn't theirs.
Tonight is about the first thing a family sees
when they come to us on a Saturday.
Everything good that ever happened to me in this town,
somebody was holding a door open at the front end of it.
That's all a gateway is, friends. A door held open.
So that's the name. The Gateway.
Pending trustee approval at the November board meeting,
and my lawyers made me say that part out loud."

The room laughed, and then stood up in pieces to applaud.
Under the applause, Cal did the other math,
the math he'd carried in and kept under his chair all evening.
Boyd's gift plugged the winter shortfall.
No gift, and the shortfall had twelve names on it,
and Cal could recite all twelve by shift.
He clapped when clapping was called for.

Then he turned his program over to see how much was left.
The back page thanked the caterer and the florist,
listed the sponsors in descending type,
and closed, in small print, with
"digital engagement: [TK: sock-vendor name] Media LLC,"
whose welcome line underneath read "ya'll come."
Cal's eyes went across it and kept going.
Two speakers left. He set the program down.

Beside him, Marisol's lap had begun to glow,
starting about midway through the word Gateway.
"Twelve thousand parents," she said out of the side of her mouth,
"just heard the word expansion inside of one minute."
She thumbed the screen and screenshotted the top of the pile.
Then, while she was in there, she screenshotted the photo
she'd taken earlier of the centerpiece and her tented name card,
which was not news, and proved nothing, and she kept it anyway.
Cal watched her do it and said nothing.
He had his own systems for keeping things.

Between the speeches and dessert,
Cal crossed the room and got hand three done
at the sponsors' table,
which left him eight feet from the kitchen corridor
with the route home lit up like a runway.
He stood there a second.
Twenty minutes, the plan had said.
The plan was two hours old, and nobody had died.
He went back to table nine and sat down.

Over at the bar, where the espresso martinis stood in a row
beside the iced-down domestics,
two strangers in their early thirties had been talking
since before the speeches
and had arrived at the stage of ignoring the room entirely,
her laughing with her whole neck,
him gesturing with a cocktail stirrer
as if it held his entire case.
Nobody introduced them. Nobody needed to.

Dessert came, and Odell got into a story
that required both hands and Verna's corroboration,
and Marisol had her chin in her palm with the laugh
still on her face, and she caught Cal watching.

He knew the count on a look. Everybody knows the count.
One beat is noticing. Two is manners. Three is a conversation.
They went to four.
Neither of them called it,
and the room went quiet around the edges,
or he stopped hearing it, one of the two,
and then the coffee station caught fire.

Not a big fire. A sterno can tipped against a stack
of cocktail napkins, and a foot of flame stood up
off the table, and the near third of the room
made the sound a room makes.
Sonny Dillard came out of his chair like the fire lieutenant
he was, and Sonny was fast.

Verna Poteat was faster.

She was two steps away, mid-refill, eighty-three years old,
and she had the lid off the nearest hotel pan
and upside down over the flame
before Sonny's napkin hit his seat.
She smothered it flat, flipped the sterno lid shut
with a butter knife, checked the table skirt
with the back of her hand, and finished pouring her coffee.

"You run a motel long enough," Verna said to the room,
"you learn where the lid is."

The room gave her a bigger hand than the Gateway got,
which Cal privately scored as correct.
Sonny sat back down slow.
"Miss Verna, the department takes volunteers."

"Baby, I have put out worse with less and no applause."

"Your town," Marisol said, under the clapping.

"Yours too now," Cal said, before the sentence
could check in with him first.

She looked at him a beat.
"That was nearly a nice thing, Cal Sutter."

"It was about the fire code," Cal said.

The gala came apart the polite way after that,
goodbyes in rings, valet tickets,
Birdie's zinnias going home under arms one jar at a time.
Marisol got claimed by three tables' worth of farewells
on her way out and ran those too,
without seeming to run anything.
At the coat check she turned, found him across the room,
and held up her program like a toast.

"Nobody died," she called, "and only the one fire."

"High marks," Cal said.

"Next time I want to see the fork skills. I saw promise."

Then the doorway traffic carried her out,
and the room was just a room again, only louder about it.

Ray's crew was already stripping tables.
At nine, the cloth was half gone,
cards and crumbs raked toward a bus tub,
and Cal picked his card out of the wreckage.
SUTTER, CAL. The nine in gold.
Two hours he had never agreed to, and here was the receipt.
He put it in his breast pocket and squared it with two fingers,
which was not a thing a man does with trash.

He drove the long way home.
The House sat dark off the road, porch line against the sky,
exactly where it had been all night,
off his right shoulder, west and a little north of chair four.
He had known that for two solid hours. He slowed down anyway.
Some things you check by hand.
