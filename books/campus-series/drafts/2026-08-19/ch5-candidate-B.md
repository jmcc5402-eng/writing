Sundays, Ashford put itself away.
Flags came off the porches, coolers drained into flowerbeds,
and by afternoon the town had the swept, blinking look
of a kitchen after a big dinner.
Even the cicadas sounded like they had taken a chair.

Marisol walked on Sundays now.
It was that or stay home and referee the quiet,
and the quiet had been winning since August,
when her daughter left for Bellamy
and took the last of the noise with her in two duffel bags.
So she walked. No route, no reason she would have said out loud,
and if her feet kept choosing Millrow Street,
that was between her and her feet.

Delmar's was smoking something low and patient
even on the Lord's day.
Through the screen door she could see the clipboard
beside the register, the one that had been filling up
since the lawn picture went phone to phone two Saturdays back,
and a man bent over it with his cap off,
printing his name like it was a deposition.
She kept walking.
Uphill, the square falling away behind her, campus not yet arrived,
and there between two oaks stood the Hartwell House,
back off the street, minding a hundred years of its own business.

The mum was still on the bottom step,
big as a dryer drum, unclaimed and unexplained,
which in Ashford was a whole language.

From inside the house came a sound she didn't know
and then, one heartbeat later, completely did.
Shhhk. A pause. Shhhk.
Long strokes, wood giving up thin ribbons of itself,
patient as the smoke down the hill.

The front door stood open.
Later she would examine, at length, the fact
that she never once considered not going in.

The hall was cool and dim and smelled of wax and old sunlight,
and the dust hung in the doorway light
like the air was in no hurry either.
At the foot of the staircase a work light was clamped
to the banister, and in the pool it threw,
Cal Sutter was kneeling on the third step with a wood plane.
Green polo, on a Sunday, like the job was a skin condition.
The light sat across his shoulders while he worked,
and the shoulders were carrying it fine.

He didn't startle.
He finished the stroke, long and level,
and the shaving came off in one ribbon
and curled down onto a drop cloth
that had clearly done this before.

"Door was open," Marisol said.

"Door's always open," Cal said. "Town's porch."

She came as far as the newel post and leaned on it,
and gave herself one honest second on the forearms,
brown to the elbow, running the plane out flat and slow,
nothing wasted, nothing hurried.
Competence, she had decided at forty-three,
was the last special effect that still worked on her.
It was working.

"You're aware it's Sunday," Marisol said.

"That's why I'm here on a Sunday," Cal said.

She let that sit, because it wanted to,
and looked up instead, and that was when the wall got her.

The staircase wall was photographs, frame after frame,
floor to landing, and every one of them was the same photograph.
Different dresses. Different haircuts. Different wars.
A couple on this staircase, always this staircase,
and always, she saw, following it up,
the same step. Third from the bottom.
The one under his knee.

"Who are they?" Marisol said.

"Which ones?" Cal said.

"Start anywhere. Start at my shoulder."

"That's Verna and her Mr. Poteat," Cal said,
without looking up from the wood.
"Back when the Magnolia Court's sign was new."

Marisol looked.
The bride had a grip on her groom's arm that promised
the motel would run on time for the next sixty years.
"And two up. The one with the belt."

"The Phams," Cal said. "Diner keys still on his belt.
Some men can't go off duty even to get married."

"Sounds familiar," Marisol said, and looked at the polo.

"It's a comfortable shirt," Cal said, and kept planing,
and the corner of his mouth did a thing
it clearly hadn't cleared with the rest of him.

"And the uniform," she said, higher up the wall.

"Soldier and his girl.
The war gave them two weeks, and they took them."
The plane kept its rhythm right through that one,
but Marisol found she had laid her hand flat on the rail.

He went on up the wall from memory, voice easy,
and right near the top he named right past one,
no hitch in the plane, none in his voice,
just a photograph's worth of silence,
and landed on "and that's your hundred years."
Marisol, who missed nothing for a living, let it go by.

"So the House has a type," she said. "Staircases."

"The House has a step," Cal said.

"The one you're kneeling on."

"Wore pale." He ran a thumb along the fresh wood.
"Hundred years of men coming through that door
with a ring in one pocket and a speech they'd forget,
and going down on one knee right about here.
Same spot every time. Knees took the finish down
a quarter inch past its brothers."

"So you're sanding away the evidence."

"Keeping it level." Another stroke.
"Tread wears uneven, someday a knee goes wrong
at the worst possible moment of a man's life.
Kept level, this step's got another hundred years in it.
The oaks out front will bury us all."

"Is this even yours to keep?" Marisol said. "Job-wise.
I thought your key ring stopped at the campus line."

Cal squared the plane on the next pass.
"Job security," he said.

It fooled neither of them.
She let that one go by too.
It was getting to be a habit in this hall,
and somewhere in her phone was a picture of a tidy green lawn
where everything she was standing in used to be.
Neither of them mentioned it.
The whole visit was the two of them not mentioning it.

He looked up at her then, finally, from the third step,
and she looked back,
and the hall did the thing old halls do with a silence,
which is hold it out to you and see what you'll do with it.
Neither of them did anything with it.
It went one beat past comfortable, and then another,
and had just started on a third when the town walked past the door.

Out beyond the doorframe, on the sidewalk past the oaks,
a man went by carrying a covered dish with the potholders still on,
walking fast to stay level with a woman
who could have taken that dish back at any point and didn't.

"I told the app I had the beans," the man said.

"You can tell that app whatever you like," the woman said.
"The app is not who you have to convince."

They went out of the frame still negotiating,
her laugh trailing after them like a ribbon off a plane,
and whoever they were, they never once looked in,
and the House watched them go like it had seen the rerun.

By the time Marisol looked back down,
Cal was working again, head bent,
and the tips of his ears had developed an opinion.

She crossed to the hall table to give them both a minute.
The guest book lay open there, fat with years,
a different hand every few lines.
You could tell the Augusts by the parents,
and the parents by the exclamation points.
One entry just said "found the laundry duffel, crisis over,"
and Marisol was still smiling at that one
when she turned a page and her own handwriting
walked up out of a stranger's afternoon and stopped her cold.

Her loops. Her crossed T's.
*Proudest tired woman in the state.*

Drop-off day, a year ago August.
The campus a river of move-in bins,
somebody's folding chairs, lemonade going warm on a table,
a cool dim hall she had registered only as next on the list.
She had signed where a smiling woman pointed
and gone back out into the heat to carry a duffel,
and never once put it together
that the cool dim hall was this hall.
She had stood inside the Hartwell House
before she could have found Millrow on a map,
and the House had kept her handwriting the whole time,
the whole quiet year, without saying anything.

Her thumbs had the phone out and the photo taken
before the rest of her was consulted.
Years of running twelve thousand parents
had trained her hands to save first and feel later.
She looked at the picture of her own writing
and could not think of one person on earth to send it to.
The twelve thousand would have hearted it by breakfast.
She left them out of it, and put the phone away.

Behind her the planing had stopped.
Cal was standing now, down on the hall floor,
folding the drop cloth over one arm,
shavings still clinging to his knuckles,
the plane held easy at his side like it weighed nothing,
which in his hand it appeared to.

"Can I?" Marisol said, and didn't say what.

He stepped back and made room.

She climbed to the third step and stood on it.

That was all. A woman standing on a stair.
The door was a tall rectangle of afternoon
at the end of the hall, oaks moving in it,
and she stood where a hundred years of the town's knees
had landed, and let the minute run.
It ran long.
It ran past the point where a polite visitor
would have laughed and stepped down,
and past the point where a polite host
would have handed her a reason to.
The cicadas came in faint from the yard.
Below her, Cal held the plane and did not fill the silence,
which she understood by now was him at his most fluent.
She thought about her daughter's room and didn't cry.
She thought about a green lawn in twelve thousand phones
and didn't say so, and he didn't say so,
and the House stood around the two of them
and said nothing the loudest of anybody.

Then she stepped down, and the hall went back to ordinary,
except that it didn't, quite.

"Thank you for the tour," Marisol said.

"Wasn't a tour," Cal said. "You walked in.
Wall was already up."

"Then thank you for the wall."

"Take that up with the century," he said,
and bent to unclamp the work light,
which was as close to *you're welcome*
as she suspected the man came.

"See you around, Cal Sutter," she said,
smiling at a point somewhere past his ear,
and went out through the tall rectangle of afternoon,
down the steps past the mum
that everybody knew and nobody claimed,
and out between the oaks to Millrow Street.

At the bottom of the hill the screen door at Delmar's banged,
and somebody called somebody's name,
and Sunday went on being Sunday.

Marisol walked home the long way, downhill through the swept town,
and told no one.
Not the twelve thousand, who would have loved it.
Not Earlene, who half knew everything already.
She let herself in, and the house was quiet,
and for once, the whole way to dark,
the quiet was just quiet.
