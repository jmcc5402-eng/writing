Cal Sutter came down Millrow at noon on Wednesday
wanting exactly two things out of the world,
a chopped plate and no conversation about the House,
and he'd have laid money on getting at least the plate.

Delmar's announced itself same as always,
hickory smoke standing in a fat column over the cinderblock,
vinegar and black pepper riding underneath it,
the screen porch already talking with its mouth full.
Two motor-pool trucks sat in the gravel,
which meant the crew had beaten him to lunch,
which meant witnesses.
And on the porch, moving table to table with a tea pitcher
nobody had issued her, was Marisol.

He sat a second with the engine ticking.
He could get it to go.
Ratchet stood up on the bench seat, nose to the glass,
and she had opinions.

"That makes two of us," Cal said, and got out.

He went up the steps and in through the porch,
touching his hat brim once at the room in general.
He did not watch her pour,
and caught the whole operation anyway,
the pitcher never quite still,
her free hand landing on a stranger's shoulder
as if it had known that shoulder for years,
a table of visitors turning into regulars under her hands.
He made it to the register without incident,
if you didn't count the route,
which ran a foot closer to the tea pitcher
than the geometry strictly required.

Ray had the register, one ear on the room,
tearing butcher paper off the roll.
The menu was a QR code now,
taped up where the menu used to hang,
and under it a hand-lettered card allowed
that they had oat milk, in small print, under protest.

"Chopped plate," Cal said. "Tea."

"Ratchet out there?"

"She is."

Ray dropped a burnt end into a paper boat without being asked
and set it on the tray.
That was most of what Ray said before one o'clock.

And there at Ray's elbow, beside the register, sat the clipboard.

Cal had heard about it. Everybody had heard about it.
A clipboard and a pen on a string,
and under the clip a stack of pages gone thick as a hymnal,
names in every kind of hand,
blue ink, pencil, one that looked like crayon.
He put one finger on the stack and riffled it, once,
like a man counting money he wasn't going to get to keep.

The pitcher arrived at his elbow before he heard her.

"Careful," Marisol said. "People sign that."

He should have taken his tray to the porch.
He knew it while he was turning around.
He turned around anyway,
and said it loud enough for the porch to quit chewing.

"That clipboard's got one name on it, ma'am. Mine."

The screen door had a clear view of the register,
and behind it his whole crew stopped pretending to eat,
his second nearest the door, fork down from the first volley.

"Funny," Marisol said.
She set the pitcher on the counter
and squared the stack with two taps, unhurried.
"I count about three hundred."

"Three hundred asking. One answering.
Come November it's the answering name they'll want,
and it won't be anybody's on that stack."

"Then it's a petition to one man," she said,
pleasant as pie, "and I'd have printed that across the top,
but it says it better on a porch. Shall we?"

She picked the whole rig up off the counter,
string, pen, pages, and all,
and carried it out through the screen door like a covered dish.

Ray hit the register bell at nobody. "Sonny. Ribs."

Sonny Dillard collected his ribs
and did not hurry back to the rail, on account of the show.

Cal followed her out with the tray.
Ratchet met him at the top step
and escorted the burnt-end boat into the shade
like it owed her money.
The empty chair at Marisol's table had its back to the street.
Cal turned it a quarter before he sat,
so Millrow lay out in front of him,
and the House end of Millrow where it always was.
Nobody remarked on it. Nobody ever had,
which suited him,
because he'd have had to invent something about the light.

She laid the clipboard on the butcher paper between them
and turned it to face him, which he noted, and didn't touch.
A spiral notebook came out of her bag
and sat under her phone, closed,
her hand resting on it the whole time
without her once appearing to know it was there.

"Here's my whole case," she said.
"Your pen is aimed at the town's living room.
That's it. That's the speech.
I could dress it up,
but the porch furniture would just agree with me."

"A living room's a fine thing," Cal said.
"So is a payroll.
Only one of the two keeps the lights on in the other,
and it isn't the sofa."

Somebody at the rail said *mm*, like church.

Neither of them had lowered their voice since the register,
and neither pretended to now.
Down the sill somebody had the radio going low,
Tick Moran talking swap meets on the AM station,
and the porch turned its volume down to hear this instead.

"Ask this porch what that House is to it," she said.
"Better, don't. You already know.
That's why I'm not worried about you."

"You should be. I'm good at my job."

"I know," she said. "It's your worst quality."

He was raising his tea and had to set it back down.
Not a laugh. Close. The porch saw it anyway.

"You've stood on that porch, Cal.
Look me in the eye and tell me it isn't worth saving."

"Roof like that's got ten years in it," he said.
"Fifteen if somebody loved it.
Loving it is the line item, ma'am.
I've read the budget. Love's not in there."

"You know that roof by heart."

"I know every roof by heart. It's the job."

That sat between them one beat longer than it needed to.
He looked at her and she looked back,
and neither of them was smiling
and neither of them was mad,
and the porch got very interested in its plates.

The register bell dinged.

"To go," Ray called through the screen. "Earlene."

Earlene did not get up.
Earlene was two tables down with a lemonade,
her chair swung a good forty degrees off her own table,
aimed at something across the road
she had so far declined to identify.

"Your order's up," Verna said.

"It'll keep. I'm conducting reconnaissance."

"On what?" Cal said.

"That's need-to-know," Earlene said, and sipped.

"Somebody's been oiling my gate at Magnolia Court,"
Verna offered, to the table at large.
"Squeaked for years. Now it swings shut sweet as you please,
and I have never once bought oil."

"Gates don't oil themselves," Earlene said.

"No," said Verna, pleased with the entire world.
"They surely don't.
I've been courted quieter, but not by better."

Marisol's phone went off against the table, face up,
the parents' group doing what it did.
Somebody's freshman needed a charger overnighted.
Somebody else's freshman was fine
but had sounded weird on the phone.
She answered both with one thumb, mid-sentence,
without dropping her argument or the smile,
twelve thousand people run off a porch table
between two swallows of tea.
Cal made himself study the smoker across the lot,
which was a good smoker,
the frozen-margarita machine slushing along beside it
off its generator,
and heard himself lose track of the smoker entirely.

"Twelve thousand parents," he said,
"and you're organizing my town on your lunch hour."

"Somebody left it lying around."

He laughed.

It got out before he'd approved it, a short one,
but it carried,
and behind the screen door a fork stopped in the air.

He didn't have to look to know what the crew saw.
Twelve people back there ate off the same budget
this fight kept circling,
and to them the stack under that clip
was a layoff notice with flowers on it.
He knew the winter math under all of it
closer than any soul on Millrow,
and he kept every bit of that off his face,
and the porch he was sitting on
sat inside the argument too,
which nobody said, and everybody knew.

The pen hung off the clip on a string knotted short,
a granny knot, doubled,
so that every signer since page one had stooped to it.
His hands were at it before he'd voted on the matter.
He picked the knot loose with a thumbnail
while she made her next point,
paid the string out to its whole length,
and hitched it back to the clip
so the next name wouldn't have to bend.
Marisol watched him do it
and had the grace not to say a word until he'd finished.

"You realize you just improved my petition."

"I made it hang straight," he said. "It's still wrong."

"Ground rules, then,"
she said, "since it appears
we're going to keep doing this in public."

"We already did. Table nine."

"Table nine was a scrimmage.
Here's mine. I don't make it personal. Ever.
Not you, not your crew, not your yard, not your dog."

"Ratchet appreciates that."

"Merits only.
I will fight you with names and porches
and everything I can carry,
and none of it will ever be aimed at you."

"Then here's mine," Cal said.
He set his fork down first.
"I won't pretend it isn't personal.
I'll give you numbers till frost,
because numbers are what I've got.
But I'm not going to sit on this porch
and tell this town it's arithmetic and nothing else.
It isn't. I know it isn't."

"Deal."

She put her hand out across the butcher paper.
He took it.
Her grip was warm and not soft,
a hand that had hauled its share of folding tables,
and he let go of it at the correct time,
which took some doing.
Somebody at the rail whistled, low,
and did not apologize.

"You cost me a quiet lunch," Cal said.

"Put it on my tab."

"You don't have a tab."

"Sure I do. You're holding my wrench."

"It's the wrong wrench," he said, and laughed,
the second one, longer than the first,
and this time he heard the crew hear it.

Her phone was still in her hand from the charger business.
Her thumbs made the small two-button pinch
that saves whatever's on a screen.
He didn't ask what she was keeping. She didn't say.

She stood, collected the pitcher,
tucked the clipboard under her arm like a Sunday purse,
and went back to watering the porch,
and the argument was over
because both of them had said all of it
and neither of them had moved an inch.

Behind the screen the crew went back to their plates
in the manner of a jury
that would be deliberating at the shop for a week.
Whatever else they'd counted,
their director had argued with the enemy
for the length of a chopped plate, full volume,
and laughed twice.
Cal knew the tally. He'd have counted it the same.

His tea had gone pale and the ice had long since quit,
and the plate was cold at the edges,
and the plate was the thing he'd come for.
Nobody had been wrong on that porch all lunch,
himself included,
and nobody had yielded, herself included,
and he'd walked in wanting quiet
and would have paid real money
to keep the loud going another hour.
He finished the brisket cold, facing up Millrow.

He bussed his own tray, because he always had,
and stopped at the register on the way out.
The clipboard was back by the till, pages squared,
the pen hanging straight on its whole length of string,
one name still missing off it.

"Good lunch?" Ray said, meaning nothing by it,
meaning everything by it.

"Loud."

Ray took the tray. "Talk's smoke."

"Not this talk," Cal said.

He put his hat on and went out past the porch,
past the pitcher making its rounds,
touching the brim exactly once more,
and climbed in the truck where Ratchet was waiting,
and did not look in the mirror
until Delmar's had gone small behind him,
and then he looked.
