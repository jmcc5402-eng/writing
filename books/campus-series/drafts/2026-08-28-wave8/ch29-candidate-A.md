# Chapter 29 — Asking for a Friend

POV: Marisol Pruett, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate: A. Variance card: D4.

---

By Wednesday of rivalry week the county had its colors out,
flags stiff on every porch in the November cold,
game-day grills wheeled to the curb three days early on faith alone,
and out at the House the scaffolding stood dressed in tarps
that filled and emptied on the wind, so that from the gate
the old place looked like it was breathing.

Inside was all ladders.
Cal's crew, twelve of them and contracted clean through March,
had the hall smelling of sawdust and stripper,
spindles numbered and laid out on canvas like evidence,
winter plans taped up where a window used to have a view.
Marisol came out at midmorning carrying the clipboard.

The second guest book. Seven thousand names, near enough,
in every ink the county owned, the clip worn silver
from a season of thumbs. Its work was done.
Everybody knew that. Nobody had wanted to say it out loud.

The case was already on the mantel, and the case was the surprise.

The woman on Cal's crew had built it, the quiet one,
tarps and demolition since October;
Marisol had never heard her put four words in a row.
She had made it from walnut offcuts of the stair job,
corners mitered so tight you couldn't have slid a receipt through,
and she'd cut the glass herself that morning
on a plank between two sawhorses, one score, one clean snap,
while Ronnie stood holding his coffee like a man at a card trick.

"Where'd you learn glass?" DeShawn asked.

"Grandmother framed pictures forty years," she said.
"You pick things up."

"You've been letting me nail plywood all month," Ronnie said.

"I don't do plywood cheap," she said, and leveled the case
with two taps of one finger, and that was the end of the interview.

Marisol laid the clipboard on the little walnut rail made to hold it,
and the woman set the glass over it,
and nobody said one word you could have put on a plaque.
Ronnie took his cap off and looked around
like a man who suspected a speech was expected of him,
found no help anywhere, and put the cap back on.

"It's a clipboard," Travis said.

Marisol leaned in and read the first page through the glass,
the launch-day names, August handwriting.
She knew every third name on that page by heart now.
There had been a Saturday, once, when she had known none of them.

"Spindles won't strip themselves," Cal said, mild as milk,
and the crew closed back over the hall like water,
and the guest book began its retirement
with everybody's backs already turned.

Ratchet heard the flower truck before anybody else did
and trotted down to the gate to supervise, tail swinging;
she inspected every bucket as Birdie Pham lowered the tailgate,
mums and pansies bound for the porch rail, and approved them all.

Eli had been out front since eight, allegedly about the lawn.
He came around the truck while Birdie was still setting buckets down,
nothing in his hands at all,
and stood where she would have to look at him.

"Birdie," he said, loud enough for the scaffolding.
"Would you come to the game with me Saturday."

No grass anywhere in the sentence. No weather. Nothing to duck behind.
The hall went quiet all at once, ladders and all.

Birdie set down the bucket she was holding and looked at him
for about as long as he could stand.
"Well," she said. "Yes. I was starting to think I'd have to ask myself."

The crew came apart. DeShawn whooped from the porch roof,
somebody drummed a ladder, and Ronnie hollered a thing about October
and money changing hands that Marisol chose not to hear officially.

"I'd have you home by dark," Eli said, mostly to the gravel.

"You will not," Birdie said, and handed him the pansies to carry.

He followed her up the walk looking six inches taller,
and Ratchet escorted the both of them, gravely, the whole distance.

---

The Courier came out Thursday, and it ran the thing on page two,
below the fold, no photograph, plain as a feed bill.
The Millrow phase of the tower development was discontinued.
The project would proceed at its sites elsewhere.
Restoration of the House would continue through the winter,
the contractor's crew engaged through March.
One clause noted a zoning calendar two counties over
said to be filling up for spring.

Marisol read it twice at her kitchen table.
"Elsewhere," she said to the empty kitchen, and let that be all of it.
Then she cut the paragraph out with the kitchen scissors
and put it under a magnet on the fridge,
one shelf up from the calendar where June sat circled,
and went to find her phone, which had a voicemail waiting.

Her son had called before her coffee finished.
"Mom, hey. The travel list posts tonight. Coach says I'm close.
Rivalry week here is completely unhinged. Love you, Mom, gotta go."
The phone had kept recording from his pocket a moment longer,
footsteps and wind and a door.
Her daughter's text came up from Bellamy an hour later,
a row of hearts and GOOD LUCK TONIGHT in capital letters,
deputizing Marisol to relay it, which she did, verbatim,
hearts and all.

The chair by the window stayed empty and the app stayed closed
and she was not counting.
The quiet in this house had been hard once, back in September.
It was just quiet now.
She finished her coffee standing up at the fridge,
reading the crew's whole winter, contracted, in black and white,
stuck up there like a good report card.

---

Kat drove in Friday morning with her out-of-county plates
and parked in front of the house, engine ticking.
Cal was on the porch with his coffee, due at campus half an hour ago
by any clock that applied to other people.
When Kat's car door shut he stood up off the rail,
took the pen out of his shirt pocket, her pen,
the one that had signed the true report at the podium,
and set it in her hand without one word being asked for.
His knuckles were still summer-brown in November.
She noticed, and let herself.

"Change orders can wait till Monday," he said.
"I'll see you at the Checkerboard."
And he went down the walk and gave her the porch.

Kat came up it with the bag she had carried since August.
"You're sure," she said, hand on the zipper.

"I'm early," Marisol said. "Early is better than sure. Open it."

The zipper sounded louder than it was.
Inside, the papers were tabbed with little arrows,
every arrow set straight, three months of waiting in perfect order.
Kat's voice changed into her working voice.
"Everywhere there's an arrow. Initial page twelve, both boxes.
Leave the last date blank. The last one's mine."

"You've had this ready since August."

"I've had this ready since August," Kat said.
"You were always going to tell me when."

Marisol put the papers on the porch rail and signed the house away.

Not her house. Half hers, half his, two states back,
unsold for two and a half years and unslept-in longer.
She could not buy out his half. She had known the number since August,
known it to the dollar, and she was through pretending
that a different summer was coming in which she could.
The decree gave her until June to say so. That was the part she refused.
Not the selling. The being made to.
Ashford was hers now, and things that are yours have prices,
and she would rather walk up to the register herself, in November,
than be marched to it in June.
Somebody else's kids would go up past the pencil marks
in that one doorway, an inch at a birthday,
and she hoped they were loud about it.

She signed at every arrow. The paper weighed what paper weighs.
She kept her chin level and nothing landed on the page,
and only at the last line did her breath go thin,
for exactly the length of her own name.

Kat stood with her hands folded on the rail and waited,
a professional to the end of it, until the pen was down.
Then she crossed the porch and hugged her, hard, no realtor left in it.
"Okay," she said into Marisol's hair.
"That's the business done. The rest of me has been wanting
to do this since August."

"I'll file Monday morning," she added, when she let go, realtor again.
"You'll have offers by Thanksgiving. It's a good house."

"It is," Marisol said. "It always was."

Inside, while Kat repacked the folder,
Marisol took the calendar down off the fridge,
found June, and tore the page out whole,
folding the circled date to the inside where it could mind its own
business, and dropped it in the trash under the sink.

"What was June?" Kat asked from the doorway.

"A deadline," Marisol said. "It missed me."
She hung the calendar back on its nail, lighter by a month.
"Come to the Checkerboard tonight. The whole county
thinks it knows my story. You should hear it.
It's gorgeous. Not one word of it is true."

---

Friday night the Checkerboard was standing room only,
rivalry eve, the Phams' griddle going flat out,
and the plywood stage the family put up on game weekends
had Earlene Tatum on it with the microphone,
running the trivia, the raffle, and loosely the town.
"Next round's for pie," she was saying as the door shut.
"No phones. No husbands helping."

Marisol sat hip to hip with Cal in the back booth,
his coat still carrying the cold of the square in its folds,
their hands together on the tabletop in plain view.
Kat had the seat across and most of a slice of pie.

Verna had the parents' board open at the Liars' Table
and was reading the thread aloud like scripture,
because somebody had posted a photograph of the booth already
and the thread had taken off without one fact aboard.

"They met at a pie auction," Verna read out, over her glasses.
"He bid on her pie and would not be outbid. Everybody knows this."

"Nobody knows this," Marisol said. "It never happened."

"He built her a porch swing to court her," Verna read on.
"She's an auditor from the capital, sent down to save the House,
which she did single-handed, and the famous pen
has been in his family a hundred years."

"Drugstore," Cal said, to nobody who would believe him.

Marisol took the pen out of her coat
and slid it back into his shirt pocket where it lived now.
"Heirloom," she said. "You heard Verna."

Aisha held her phone up from the counter.
"They've got you two secretly married since Labor Day."

"One faction has the wedding in June," Kat said, scrolling.
"Outdoor. There are said to be photographs."

"Busy month, June," Marisol said.

Marisol's thumb found her own phone, face down by the pie.
Fourteen weeks of correcting the record lived in that thumb.
She left the phone exactly where it was.

"Married since September," she said. "Imagine the cake we owe people."

Somewhere in the middle of the raffle Kat stood up,
either to stretch or because the story of the season
had gone around wrong one more time than she could stand,
and said, to the room at large,
"The end of the margarita story, by the way,
is that I paid for the margaritas."

"Course you did, honey," Earlene said into the microphone.

The Checkerboard went up like a bleacher section,
and the margarita story ended there, four months old, paid in full.

They left while the raffle was still going.
Outside, the square stood under its lamps in the cold,
flags overhead, the tower ringing the hour into it.

Weeks ago the county had invented a kiss on this square,
sworn to it, produced witnesses.
Now Cal put his hand along her jaw, taking his time about it,
and they finally held the kiss the town had already testified to,
in the very spot the town had picked,
slow because there was no reason on earth to be anywhere else.

Somebody rapped the diner glass. The door swung open
and Earlene's voice spilled out over the microphone.
"Folks, if you're just now joining the raffle,
there is a development on the square. It's the kiss.
The one from the board. They are doing it right now."

Marisol heard every word of it and stayed exactly where she was.
The county had watched her all fall and gotten every chapter wrong.
This one they could have.

By the time Cal got the truck door open for her,
Verna had posted the kiss to the board,
where according to the thread it had happened weeks ago,
so the record ruled it a reenactment and moved on to the wedding.
Marisol left her phone in her pocket and let the county tell it.
Cal drove her home.
