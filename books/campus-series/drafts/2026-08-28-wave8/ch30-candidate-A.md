# Chapter 30 — Last Home Game (working title)

POV: Cal Sutter, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate: A. Variance card: D6.

---

The RV city had gone up in the dark again, same as it had in August,
and by seven its noise lay over the east side of town,
generators and charcoal smoke and one cowbell somebody would regret.
Cal stood at the mouth of the motor pool with the bay door up,
both hands around a cup of shop coffee, breath showing,
and for the first Saturday in fourteen weeks
had nowhere he was needed in any of it.

Half a lifetime of home Saturdays, and he could read this one by ear.
The rattle of the drumline, still ragged, finding its rows.
A public-address test, one syllable, swallowed whole.
He could have told you the hour of the morning blind.

The shop behind him was at rest, and none of it was neglect.
Mowers winterized and racked, carts charged and lined up straight,
the House's change orders squared away in their binder
and her pen riding his shirt pocket, on call.
Twelve names ran down the winter board, contracted through March,
every Monday between now and spring spoken for.
Above the workbench, where another man might pin a calendar,
a seating card said Table Nine,
hung square on four thumbtacks, built to outlast the building.

Ratchet lay in her stenciled spot by the fuel pump,
chin on her paws, one ear cocked at the noise downhill,
unimpressed by all of it.

"Big day," Cal told her. "Try to pace yourself."

She thumped her tail twice against the concrete and stayed put.
He stood a while longer in the doorway of his own quiet shop
and let himself feel the size of the day.
A Saturday with nothing broken in it had scared him once.
This one wasn't empty. It was only waiting.
He rinsed the cup, whistled her into the truck,
and drove up the hill against the grain of the whole town.

The carillon was letting go of nine when he came up the porch steps
with a small wooden box under his arm and Ratchet at his heel.
Inside, the hall stood in its tarps and ladders,
scaffolding to the ceiling, dust hanging in the low morning light,
and the old crack ran down the east wall under its little panes of glass.

Nineteen slides of microscope glass, cemented one below the next,
each dated in a small level hand that was not his,
and then no more dates.
He had read them a thousand times. He did not read them now.

The earliest ones had gone amber at the edges, their dates
faded down to pencil ghosts, and under every one of them
the crack had kept still, all those years, provable at a glance.
Glass couldn't fib. It held or it broke. It had held.

He laid the kit out on a sawhorse plank.
One clean slide. The tin of cement. A rag,
and a carpenter's pencil sharpened flat at the shop that morning.
He buttered the glass edges thin with his knife,
set the twentieth slide across the crack at the bottom of the run,
pressed two fingers to it, and held.

The cement needed thirty seconds. He gave it more.
Down the hill a horn section found its feet, fell apart,
found them again.
The tarps ticked in a draft he would chase down on Monday.
His fingers stayed on the glass long past any use they were doing,
and he let them stay.

He had kept the paper log for years and never dated glass.
The log was upkeep. The glass was watching.
The watching had been hers, and then for a long time
it had belonged to what he'd lost,
and he had left it that way on purpose.
Now, with two fingers flat on the twentieth slide,
he let himself say the plain of it, silently, once.
The watch was his.
And it wasn't only made of grief anymore.
He was watching for the wall to hold
because of what was coming to this house now,
and not only for the sake of what was gone.
Crew on Monday. Copper in the walls by Christmas.
The years he had, apparently, decided to have.

He took up the pencil and dated the glass in his own hand,
block letters, too heavy on the first stroke, steadied by the second.
The first date on that wall that wasn't hers.
The pen stayed in his pocket. The pen was for change orders.
It was done then, and there was no reason on earth
to keep standing there, and he kept standing there.
Long enough for the light to move a plank's width along the floor.
Long enough that Ratchet came in from the porch
and leaned her whole weight against his shin.
He looked at the nineteen dates and the one,
wiped the knife clean, and packed the box.

By early afternoon the stadium had filled, and the roar came up the hill
in weather, rising and gone and rising again, no more remarkable to him
than rain on a roof he trusted.
Half the crew's trucks sat in the House's side yard
on a Saturday nobody had put on the board.

"Didn't contract you for Saturdays," Cal said from the hall door.

"Didn't contract you either," DeShawn said from the top of the tall
ladder, pulling new wire down through a hundred years of wall.
"And here you stand."

Ronnie fed him slack from below. "He can't help it.
Man aims the truck at home and the truck comes here on its own."

Cal laughed.

"You following the game at all?" Ronnie said, chin toward the radio
on the ladder shelf.

"I can hear it fine from here."

"Boss, that is the saddest sentence anybody ever said on a rivalry
Saturday."

"Let him alone," DeShawn said. "Man's finally got a Saturday he
likes."

"You want us Monday too?" Ronnie said.

"Monday and every Monday till March."

The crew's second, a woman who could out-pull any two of the others,
came through with a coil over her shoulder, took one look at Cal
standing idle, handed him a roll of tape,
and pointed her chin at a junction box.
He went where he was pointed. It was her wall today.

"How's it coming?" he asked her, and got a thumbs-up
around a mouthful of wire nuts.

"Rewiring before anything pretty," DeShawn said, to nobody,
in Cal's own cadence, handing down a length of the old cloth wire
like a dead snake.

Travis, stripping cable by the window, said, "Wire's wire."

Cal walked the downstairs once before he left them to it,
hand trailing the new runs stapled clean along the studs.
Good bones had never been the House's problem.
The wiring had been the problem,
and at last, in the walls and elsewhere, it was getting fixed.

"Go home before the traffic," he said on his way out.
"That's the boss talking."

"Which one of us is leaving last?" DeShawn said,
and Cal had no answer he cared to give.

He spent the last of the afternoon at the plywood table in the hall,
walking the change orders forward with her pen,
initialing what the winter would need.
The carillon let five o'clock go into a sky already going dark,
and below, lot by lot, the RV city folded itself up.
Awnings cranked in, flags came down,
taillights strung out toward the highway.
The last roar of the season had come down Millrow an hour before,
worn as familiar as a hymn,
and what was left was the after-noise of a town heading home happy.

He had worked that lot at dawn half his life,
hauling somebody's dead battery back to life before kickoff,
and he watched it empty now with no itch to be down in it.
Everything he wanted rescued was already up this hill.

The mums in the rail boxes had gone rusty at the edges,
and nobody had found the heart to pull them.
Marisol was on the porch when he came out of the hall,
coat collar up, hair down,
watching the fairgrounds empty with her hands in her pockets.
He went back in and came out with two mugs
and put one in her hands without being asked.
She wrapped both hands around it and drank.

"Crew was here today," she said. "I saw the trucks going home."

"Nobody asked them in. They came anyway."

"Of course they did." She drank again. "It's done, by the way.
The old house. Signed, sealed, somebody else's gutters."

"Any regrets?"

"I checked this morning." She settled her shoulder against the post.
"Turns out no."

"Fourteen weeks," he said.

"You keeping count?"

"I keep records. You've seen the shop."

"You eat anything today?" she said.

"I had coffee."

"Cal."

"It was a large coffee."

"Your crew fixed the porch light," she said.
"It doesn't flicker anymore. I almost miss it."

"I can loosen it."

"Don't you dare."

The cold had put color in her face, and it suited her.
A last generator quit somewhere down the hill,
and the quiet after it had the whole winter inside it,
and neither of them reached to fill it.

He set his mug on the rail by the mums and held out his hand.
"Come inside a minute. I want to show you something."
She set her mug beside his and took the hand.

"Cal Sutter, it is pitch dark in here," she said, and came anyway.

"Cords," he said.

"I know this house in the dark."

He walked her past the mantel and the guest book under its glass,
between the ladders and the sawhorses, to the foot of the stairs.
The third step was worn pale in the middle,
a century of other people's nerve taken one person at a time.
He stepped up onto it and drew her up beside him,
and there was room, and it held level under the both of them.

"I had a—there was going to be more of this," he said.
"I've been leveling a sentence in my head since August
and it never came out square."
His thumb crossed her knuckles and settled.
"I love you. That's the part that held."

The smile arrived before he could do anything about it,
the whole thing, the door swung wide on the lit room,
and this time he left it open.
It was hers. There was nobody else it was for,
and he let her see all of that too.

He had seen what she did next exactly once before,
thrown wide to a whole dance floor with the town watching.
This one crossed about ten inches and had no town in it.
It was turned all the way down to him alone,
and it landed with more in it than the loud one ever carried.

"I love you too," she said,
and there was not one clumsy thing about it anywhere.
"You've been leveling that since August?"

"Since about the second Saturday."

"Good," she said. She looked up the stairs into the dark,
then back at him. "In August I wanted this year over.
I'd have signed it away early if anybody was buying.
Somewhere between the smoke and this step
I stopped wanting time to move at all."

He kissed her on the third step, taking his time about it,
her free hand coming up cold and sure along his jaw.
When they came apart she stayed against him,
her head under his chin, facing the open door and the evening in it.

Down the hill the last taillights reached the highway.
The porch light lay in across the hall floor
that would be sanded new in the spring.
They stood on the step while the evening went fully dark,
her hand in his, the twentieth slide drying on the east wall,
and around the two of them, tarped and braced and wired for the years
ahead, the House held them both.
