# Chapter 30 — The Third Step

POV: Cal Sutter, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate C. Variance card: D3 (short-declarative session).

---

Rivalry Saturday came in cold and clear.
Frost on the windshields. Breath hanging in the bay lights.
Down the hill the RV city was already awake,
breakfast smoke drifting low over the fairgrounds,
the hum of it steady, a furnace with a town on top.
Last home game of the fall.
The game was weather. It had always been weather.
Cal let it be, and unlocked the shop.

Ratchet had beaten him in.
She lay in her parking spot,
the one with her name stenciled on the concrete,
and thumped her tail twice without lifting her head.

"Morning," he said.

She sighed and resettled.

The motor pool was at rest.
Trucks fueled and nosed in straight. Floor swept.
Salt loaded for a winter that hadn't started yet.
The board on the wall was full edge to edge,
twelve names contracted through March,
every week between now and then spoken for.
He read it name by name anyway.
DeShawn. Ronnie. Travis. The rest.
A whole crew's winter under one roof.
The table-nine seating card was pinned above the workbench,
where he would see it every day.
He saw it.

He stood in the open bay door with his coffee
and listened to the town fill up below.
For a lot of years a quiet Saturday had a hole in the middle.
He checked for the hole, the old habit, honest about it.
It wasn't there.
The quiet was just quiet, and it was big,
and there was room in it.
Ratchet came over and leaned against his leg.
He stood there and let the morning be what it was.

The carillon rang eight.
He mixed a small batch of cement, stiff, no bigger than an egg.
He wrapped a square of glass in a clean shop rag
and laid it flat on the seat of the truck.
Then he whistled Ratchet up into the cab.

---

The House stood in its scaffolding with the tarps rolled back.
Cold inside. Plaster and sawdust and old wood.
Ratchet took the porch. Cal took the hall.

Morning light lay on the east wall.
The crack ran where it had always run.
Nineteen small panes of glass crossed it, cemented down,
each one dated in a hand he knew.
Her dates climbed the crack and stopped.

Dated glass doesn't argue.
If a wall moves, the glass breaks, and the date says when.
Nineteen panes said this wall had never moved an inch.

He brushed the dust from a span of plaster
below the lowest of her panes,
blew on it, and wiped it clean with the rag.
The crack there was the same hairline it had been in spring.
The log said so too. He had checked anyway.

He took the pencil from behind his ear.
Her pen stayed where it lived, in his shirt pocket.
The pen was for change orders. This was not one.

He wrote the date on the twentieth slide.
Small. Square. His own hand.
His hand had never dated glass before this morning.
His entries lived in the paper log, filed under upkeep.

He buttered the cement thin across the back of the pane.
He set it over the crack, low, just past the end of her line,
and pressed it with two fingers and held it while it took.

The wall was his to watch now.
He had known it a long time without putting it anywhere.
He said it to himself now, alone, with two fingers on the glass.
The watching was his. And it was not made of grief only.
Not anymore. Somewhere across this fall
it had taken on more rooms than that.

He held the pane while the cement went off,
and the noise came up the hill through the open windows,
horns and smoke and the same game the town had always played,
and none of it hurried him.

He wiped his thumb on the rag and stepped back.
Twenty dates. Hers first. His last. The line going on.
Next year's pane would want a spot a little lower.
He caught himself picking it out, and let himself.

He gave it a minute in the cold hall.
Then he went out to meet the crew.

---

The crew worked a half day, on account of the game.
By midmorning the hall was ladders end to end.

DeShawn was up in the ceiling, feeding new wire down the chase.
Ronnie stood at the panel in a headlamp.
Travis held the ladder and supplied advice.
The second electrician, a woman who spent about six words a shift,
moved more wire than any of them without spending one.

The old wire came out brittle, its cloth gone to powder.
The new copper went in bright.
Good bones, this house. Bad wiring, getting seen to at last.
Cal had one doctrine on the subject, and the crew could recite it.

"How's the chase?" he said.

"Clean," DeShawn said, from inside the ceiling.
"Whoever framed this place wanted us to win.
Before anything pretty, boss."

"Before anything pretty," Cal said.

"Panel's dressed," Ronnie said. "You want the honors, or can I?"

"Go ahead."

Ronnie threw the breaker.
The hall lights came on all at once and held. No flicker.
Nobody said anything for a second. Then Ronnie did.

"First thing I'm doing in March,
I'm flipping every switch in this house at the same time.
Just to feel something."

Cal laughed.

"Wire's wire," Travis said.

The second electrician came down her ladder
and handed Cal the old hall switch,
dead brass, worn smooth as a river stone.

"Keep it?" she said. Two of her six words.

"Mantel," Cal said, and set it there.

They broke at noon.

"We're standing room by the band," Ronnie said, packing out.
"Come find us."

"I've got a wall drying," Cal said.

"Lights'll be on Monday when you get back, boss," DeShawn said.
"Try not to admire them all weekend."

"No promises."

They went down Millrow toward the stadium
and were gone into the crowd inside a block.
The second electrician left her ladder set for Monday.

Cal signed a change order against the porch rail
with the pen from his shirt pocket
and put it back where it lived.
On his way out he laid his hand flat on the hall plaster.
Good bones. The wiring finally getting fixed.
The House's, and not only the House's.
He had noticed that this fall without saying so out loud.

---

The game happened without him. That suited him fine.
The roar came down the hill all afternoon,
swelling and settling, swelling and settling.
He did small work under it.
He swapped a cracked porch board for a straight one,
planed the edge by hand, swept up after himself.
Small work. The right size for the day.
Ratchet supervised from the porch.

The carillon rang five into a going-down sun.
The stadium let out.
The last roar of the fall came down Millrow,
familiar as a hymn the whole town knew without the book.
Then the fairgrounds began to empty.
The RV city came down lot by lot,
slide-outs coming in, rows going to gaps,
taillights stringing out toward the interstate.

The mums on the porch rail had gone rusty at the edges,
right on schedule.
The cold came up out of the ground at sundown.
The streetlights came on down Millrow one at a time.

Marisol came up the walk with her hands pulled into her sleeves.
She climbed the steps and stood beside him at the rail,
and for a while they watched the lots go dark.
He went in and came back with two mugs
and handed her one, hot, without being asked.
She wrapped both hands around it.

"Tarps come down all right?" she said.

"Twice. Wind took one. Travis took the other."

"Personally?"

"On principle."

"Did you eat anything today?"

"There was a sandwich involved."

"Half of one is what I heard."

"You've got sources on my sandwich?"

"I have sources on everything in this town."

She smiled into the steam and looked down the hill.

"How was the shop this morning?"

"Quiet. Ratchet held her spot. The board's full through March."

"Twelve names," she said. "One winter. I like the arithmetic."

"So do I," he said.

The lots kept emptying below them.

"Lot by lot," she said. "It's a card trick run backwards."

"It'll deal again next fall."

"I know it will. That's my favorite part."
She turned the mug in her hands.
"I signed the last of it yesterday. The house back there is theirs
now. Two states of my life, and I kept waiting for the floor to
move."

"Did it?"

"Never did," she said. "Turns out I was already standing where I
live."

He looked at her while she watched the town.
Hair come loose on one side. Steam standing off the mug in the cold.
The sentence was ready. It had been ready since August.
It was not going to come out any better for waiting.

He took the mug from her hands and set it on the rail by the mums.
She let him. She watched him do it.

"Come inside a minute," he said.

She didn't ask what for.
He took her hand and walked her in through the scaffolding,
down the hall under the new lights,
past the mantel and the guest book under its glass,
to the foot of the staircase.

"The lights work," she said.

"Since noon. Ronnie did the honors."

"Did he make a speech?"

"He's saving it for March."

The third step was worn pale in the middle.
A century of people gathering their nerve had done the wearing.
People took that step one at a time. They always had.

He stepped up onto it and drew her up beside him.
There was room. There had been room the whole time.
Nobody knelt. They stood on it together, her hand in his,
the new light steady down the hall behind them.

"I had a sentence," he said.
"I've been leveling it since August.
It kept coming out crooked, so you're getting the crooked one.
I love you."

He smiled then. All the way. The third time, and he knew it.
He felt it go wide, a door swung open on a lit room,
and he left it open, for her, with no town anywhere around
to take a share of it.

She had smiled at a whole town once, from a dance floor.
He had stood in that crowd and caught what everybody caught.
This one crossed no distance at all.
It arrived, and it was aimed, and it stayed.

"I love you," she said.
There was not one clumsy thing about it.

Then, quieter, "In August I wanted the year to be over.
I wanted it gone.
Somewhere between the smoke and this step
I stopped wanting time to go anywhere at all."
She looked at him on the worn pale wood.
"It can stand right here."

He kissed her.
Cold hands, warm mouth, one step holding two.

Down the hill the last of the RV city rolled out toward the
interstate.
The new lights burned steady in the hall.
The House stood around the two of them,
braced and mended and wired for whatever came next,
and held them both.
