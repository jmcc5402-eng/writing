# Chapter 30 — Last Home Game

POV: Cal, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate: B. Variance card: D2 (hands and objects).

---

The RV city went up in the dark one last time before winter,
and by seven the cold had a thousand generator breaths in it
and the first drumline notes were coming up the hill
with frost still on them.
Rivalry Saturday. Last home game of the fall.
Cal Sutter stood in the open bay of the motor pool
with shop coffee warming both hands and let the noise arrive.
The game was the weather here,
and today it could run itself.
Down the hill the flags were going up over the awnings,
and the first charcoal of the morning was lit somewhere out of
sight, thin November smoke, more stubborn than the August
kind. A horn section tuned against the cold, gave it up,
and tried again.

The shop behind him was at rest.
Bays swept, carts fueled and staged for the lot crews,
the compressor bled down for the weekend.
The winter was spoken for, twelve names contracted through
March, and the change-order folder rode in the truck
where he could reach it without looking.
Above the workbench, pinned square where he would see it
every morning, a small seating card said *Table Nine*
in careful calligraphy, gone soft at the corners
from four months of shop air.

Ratchet lay in her stenciled parking spot between the bays,
chin on her paws.

"Not going to the game either," he said.

Her tail swept the concrete once.

"Big day, though." He tipped the last of the coffee out on the
gravel and rinsed the mug at the shop sink.
"Not the football part."

She watched him the whole way to the truck, and held her spot,
which was her whole job, and she was excellent at it.

A quiet Saturday used to have a hole in the middle of it.
He stood a second with his hand on the truck door and checked,
old habit, feeling for the hole.
There was just the day, full to the edges.

---

The House took the morning sun on its east side,
tarps still, scaffolding pearled with frost.

The slide rode flat in his shirt pocket, behind Marisol's pen.
He had cut it square in the shop on Thursday,
microscope glass, a wafer of it, light as a fingernail,
and he took it out now with two fingers
and held it up against the sky to check it clean.

The old crack ran down the east wall under the scaffold,
and along it, in two staggered rows, sat the nineteen panes,
each one cemented down and dated in a hand that was not his,
the earliest dates gone faint as breath on glass.
Not one pane had ever cracked.
The wall had never moved, and the glass had spent years
proving it in her handwriting, and then the dates had stopped,
and his own dates had gone into the paper log ever since,
page after page, pencil on paper, never on glass.

He had set glass all his life, storm sash and cabinet panes,
quick work, the kind his hands did while his head was
somewhere else. His head was nowhere else this morning.

He mixed a thumbnail of cement on a scrap of shingle.
He buttered the rim of the glass with the tip of the putty
knife, set the twentieth pane across the crack at the end of
the second row, and held it down with two fingers while it
grabbed.

Then the grease pencil from behind his ear.
He wrote the date small, up in the corner,
where she had always put hers.
His figures came out squarer than any others on that wall.
Blunter. A builder's hand.
The first date on the east wall that wasn't hers.

His eyes stung once.
He blinked it away and checked that the date was clean.

Then he stayed crouched there a minute, palm flat on the cold
plaster below the rows, and let the admission come all the way
in, the one he had been circling since the vote carried.
The watch was his now.
And it wasn't made of grief alone anymore, not even mostly.
He wasn't guarding what the wall had already held.
He was keeping watch over what it was going to hold next.

He said nothing to the wall.
He wiped the putty knife on his thigh, packed the pencil away,
and went to unlock the House for the crew.

---

By noon the carillon rang the hour under the first full roar
from the stadium, and the hall was ladders and drop cloths and
old wire coming out of the walls in brittle lengths.

DeShawn fed a cloth-wrapped run down through a ceiling plate.
"Look at this. Insulation's coming off like pie crust."

"Don't say pie," Ronnie said from the foot of the ladder.
"I skipped breakfast for this."

"My mom makes chili every Rivalry Saturday," Travis said,
stapling new runs along a stud bay.

"Hall circuit's next," DeShawn said. "Send up the fish tape."

"Save the plaster where you can," Cal said. "It's original."

"Boss, everything in here is original," Ronnie said.
"That's the fight."

His second came down the hall with a clipboard and a chewed
length of dead wire, stripped to bright copper at one end.
"Squirrels," she said.

"That squirrel's been gone fifty years," DeShawn said,
"and it's still winning."

Cal laughed, a short true one that went up through the open
joists, and DeShawn grinned down through the ceiling like a
man who had won something himself.

"Change order," his second said, turning the clipboard around.
"Second-floor runs to the landing, if the chase behaves."

"Same run as the hall?"

"Same run."

He clicked Marisol's pen out of his shirt pocket, signed,
clicked it home, and pressed the pocket flat with two fingers
without thinking about it.

New wire before anything pretty.
That had been an argument once.
It hadn't been one in a long time; by now it was just the
order the work went in, and everybody on the crew could recite
it, and nobody needed to.

All afternoon Millrow carried the game up to them in swells
and hushes. A big one came and hung there.

"Touchdown," Ronnie said.

"Fumble," said Travis.

"That's seventy thousand people finding out something at the
same time," DeShawn said, "is what that is."

The swell broke up into band noise, and the crew went back to
pulling wire, happy to be wrong in company.
Sometime past four DeShawn lifted the clipboard out of Cal's
hands.

"Go on, boss. We've got it till dark, and you've got somewhere
to be."

He went.
A year ago he would have found one more thing to check on the
way out.
He didn't even slow down.

---

The carillon rang five into the first real dusk, and by then
the RV city was folding itself up lot by lot, awnings cranking
in, taillights threading out toward the county roads.
The stadium's last roar of the fall came down Millrow worn
smooth as a hymn the whole town knew by heart, and then
Millrow began to empty in the dark.

The mums on the porch rail had gone rusty in their pots.
Marisol was on the porch when he came up the walk,
coat collar up, hair loose, watching the lots empty out,
and the cold had put color in her face.
Across the fairgrounds a few last awnings came down, and brake
lights flared and settled all the way out to the county road,
the whole visiting city going home for the year.

He filled two mugs from the steel thermos on the tailgate,
carried them up the steps, and handed her one, the white one,
hot, without being asked.
She took it in both hands and held it under her chin.
Her hands looked right around a mug.
He had thought so for months, and it kept on being true.

"How far did they get?" she asked.

"The landing. There'll be light on the stairs by Tuesday."

"Light on the stairs." She blew across the mug. "Listen to
that."

"Ratchet come up with you?"

"Holding the shop."

"That dog works more Saturdays than the county does."

They stood with the steam going up between them.

"The listing went live this morning," she said. "There's a
sign in my old yard by now. Two states from here."

"How does that sit?"

"I keep poking at it to see if it hurts." She turned the mug a
slow half turn in her hands. "It doesn't. It's a good house.
It's just not where I live."

"Loud one today," he said, with a nod down the hill.

"You could hear the fourth quarter from the square. The whole
place emptied out by halftime. Everybody wanted the ending in
person." She smiled down at the last taillights. "I didn't
even ask who won."

Below them the generators shut down one by one, and the dusk
came on with charcoal in it, and neither of them hurried it.

He set his mug on the rail beside the rusty mums and held out
his hand.

She looked at the hand, then at him.
She set her mug next to his, the two of them steaming together
in the cold, and took it.

He walked her in through the hall, past the quiet ladders,
past the guest book under its glass on the mantel catching the
last of the light, to the foot of the staircase.
He hadn't turned on the work lights.
He didn't need them for this hall.

"Cal, it's dark in here."

"I know where I'm going."

The staircase rose away into the black of the second floor,
and the third step held its pale worn middle even now,
a hundred years of other people's nerve rubbed right into the
grain.
He stepped up onto it and drew her up beside him by the hand,
and the step took them both.
There was not much room. There was enough.

"Marisol." He had built a preamble for this. Weeks of one.
It was gone. "I love you. I've been leveling that sentence
since August, and that's as straight as it ever got."

And the smile came, all of it, wide, the door swung open on
the lit room with no closing anywhere in it, for her alone.

She looked at him from a hand's width away.
He had watched her hand a smile to a whole town once, from a
dance floor, and had kept his little piece of it like
everybody else in the county.
This one came up slower and came up entire, and it had one
address.

"I love you too," she said, without one clumsy thing about it.

Her thumb moved across the backs of his knuckles.
"In August I wanted the year to be over," she said.
"Somewhere between the smoke and this step I stopped wanting
time to move at all."

He kissed her on the third step, her hands coming up cold to
his jaw and warming there.

They stayed a long while, two on the one pale step, her hand
in his. Out on the rail the two mugs cooled side by side, and
down the hill the last taillights threaded away toward the
county roads, and the House stood around the two of them,
tarps and scaffold, new wire and old glass, and held them
both.
