# Chapter 29 — Asking for a Friend

POV: Marisol Pruett, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate: C. Variance card: D5.

---

On Wednesday the House smelled like pine shavings and cold canvas,
and everybody's breath showed in the front hall.

The scaffolding had gone up along the west face,
tarps lashed over the bones of it,
and Cal's crew moved through the rooms in gloves and flannel,
twelve strong, carrying lumber and winter plans,
arguing in the easy register of people whose work ran through March.
Frost sat on the porch rail past midmorning.
Nobody said a word about it. There was too much to do.

The glass case appeared on the mantel around noon.
Ronnie stood beside it wiping his hands on his jeans,
and that was the only warning anybody got.

Cal carried the clipboard in from his truck under one arm,
like lumber, like anything.
The second guest book. Seven thousand names, near enough,
the pages gone soft at the corners from a season of hands.
He held it out to her without a word,
and Marisol understood that she was being asked to do it,
and that if anybody attempted a speech
the whole crew would file into the yard until it was over.

She laid the clipboard in the case and closed the lid.

"Well," said DeShawn.

"Yep," said Cal.

"That's it?" said Travis. "No speech?"

"That was the speech," said Cal.

"Seven thousand," said Ronnie. "You'd think it'd weigh more."

"It weighs plenty," Marisol said.

Travis took his hard hat off,
appeared to have no plan for what came next,
and put it back on.
Upstairs the hammering never paused.
Marisol stood at the mantel with her hands deep in her pockets
and looked a minute longer than she strictly needed to,
at the little clipboard that had carried the whole argument,
retired under glass in a house full of ladders,
and waited until she trusted her voice before she used it.
Nobody made her use it.
The tarps filled and slacked against the frame,
the crew closed back over the room like water,
and the whole ceremony ran four minutes,
which she thought was the correct length.
The House wasn't finished. Just the clipboard was.

Ratchet got them all up from lunch by barking at the gate,
tail going the entire time, no menace in her anywhere.
Birdie Pham's truck rolled in behind the noise,
porch pots riding in the bed, mums in rust and gold,
wrapped against the cold. The winter shift for the porch.

Eli had been at the House since ten
on an errand nobody could afterward reconstruct.
He met the truck at the tailgate,
and the crew did not even pretend to keep working.

"Birdie," he said.

"Eli."

"Saturday. The game. I've got two seats
and I want you in the other one."
He said it at full volume, to her face,
in front of twelve witnesses and a dog.
"That came out louder than I practiced.
I'm asking you to the game."

Birdie looked at him across a bucket of mums
for what felt to the entire yard like a week.

"Took you long enough," she said. "Yes."

The crew reacted like a crew.
Somebody whooped off the scaffold,
DeShawn whistled through his teeth,
Ronnie pumped Eli's hand with both of his,
and the woman running the chop saw hit the trigger twice,
no wood in it, just for the sound.

"Pay up," said DeShawn.

Travis paid.

"Wear the good hat," Birdie said.

"I will," said Eli, like a vow.

Travis said, "I went to the game last year."

Birdie handed her pots across the rail one at a time,
and Eli carried each one like it was full to the brim.

---

Thursday morning she scraped the windshield with a kitchen spatula,
the actual scraper being packed in whatever box scrapers lived in,
and came back inside to a voicemail she'd missed by ninety seconds.

Her son, talking fast against some echoing hallway.
The travel squad posted Friday,
and coach had looked at him twice in walk-through,
which either meant everything or meant nothing,
and the whole campus had lost its mind for rivalry week,
and he had to run. Love you, Mom, gotta go.

She played it twice and kept it.

Her daughter's text from Bellamy landed while the coffee went:
luck for her brother, a row of little flags,
instructions to film everything if he dressed.
Marisol typed back and drank her coffee standing up.
The chair across the table stayed empty,
the app on her phone stayed closed,
and she was not counting.
The quiet in the house had stopped being a diagnosis
sometime back in October. It was just quiet now.

On the calendar by the fridge,
June sat where it had sat all year,
one date circled in pen, seven pages deep.
She looked at it while she rinsed the cup.
Then she dried her hands and called Kat.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Morning. Bring it."

"You're sure," Kat said.

"I've been sure since the vote. I was busy."

"Nine o'clock," Kat said. "I'll bring the good pen."

"I've got a pen," Marisol said.

---

The tower rang five into a sky already gone to lamplight,
and the Checkerboard was steamed to the windows
by the time Marisol slid into the booth.
Cal came straight from the House with sawdust on his cuffs
and took her hand across the table like it was on his list.

"How's my wall?" she said.

"Wrapped for winter," Cal said. "Ladders in the hall through March.
The crew wants a woodstove down there.
I'm pretending to think about it."

Kat came in off the highway twenty minutes later
and set her bag on the booth seat with particular care,
zipper shut.

The Courier lay folded by the napkin dispenser.
Verna had the paragraph close to memorized
and read it out anyway, plain as a zoning notice:
the Millrow phase of the tower project was discontinued,
the towers would proceed elsewhere,
on a schedule some other county would learn about in the spring,
and restoration of the House would continue through the winter,
crew under contract through March.
Boyd's name appeared once, mid-sentence, doing nothing.

"I've read worse obituaries," Verna said,
and that was as far as anybody took it.

By the time the pie came, the parents' board had found them.

Marisol's phone started going on the table, the thread climbing.
Earlene had it up on her own phone first
and read aloud from the stage she carried everywhere.

"'Confirmed sighting, the Checkerboard, holding hands.'
Confirmed," Earlene said. "You two are a public utility.
'For the new families: he restored that whole house for her.'"

"I did not," said Cal, comfortable, not looking up from the pie.

"'They met when he caught her falling off a ladder.'"

"It was a tent," Marisol said, "and I caught it."

"'Engaged since September. Kept it quiet for the vote.'"

"Are we," said Cal.

"'He proposed at the podium Tuesday. That's what the pen was.'"

"That is not what the pen was," said Cal.

"'The pen was his grandmother's.'"

"That one I like," said Kat.

"'They've been secretly married since spring.'"

"We met in August," said Marisol.

"'He kissed her on the square in October. Under the tower.'"

"That never happened," said Cal.

"Yet," said Marisol.

"'Sources say the wedding's at the House in the spring.'"

"The House has no roof on one side," said Cal.

"'Sources say that's romantic.'"

"Leave it up," Aisha called from the counter.
"Best thread we've had all season."

"'She's been running this town since August.
The clipboard was just paperwork.'"

"The clipboard was never just paperwork," Marisol said,
to nobody in particular, and let that be her one correction.

Her thumb hovered over the old register,
the pinned post, the *my loves*,
every gentle correction she knew how to type.
She turned the phone face down instead
and let the thread run wrong at full speed.
Seven thousand of them had signed the clipboard.
They could have this one.

Kat set down her fork.
"The end of the margarita story, by the way,
is that I paid for the margaritas."

"Course you did, honey," said Earlene.

Verna laughed so hard she had to set the coffeepot down
on the Liars' Table and stand there a second,
and the Liars' Table, which had been pretending not to listen
since five o'clock, gave up and laughed with her.

"Tomorrow," Kat said when she stood, bag over her shoulder.

"Coffee's on at eight thirty," said Marisol.

Kat's bag rode back out to her car still zipped.
One more night.

---

Friday morning Kat came up the walk with her breath ahead of her,
and the folder was out of the bag and open
before she reached the porch rail. At last.

"Business first," Kat said. "Coffee after."

The papers went down on the rail,
and the cold of the wood came up through all the pages.
The listing agreement. The house two states back,
half his and half hers,
priced like what it was, which was a house.

"Sign at the flags, initial the yellow tabs," Kat said,
realtor voice on. "Photos and the lockbox are mine.
You never have to see it again unless you want to."

"I know how paperwork works," Marisol said.

"I know you do," Kat said, and turned the folder around,
and stood back with her hands at her sides.

Marisol had run the buy-out number in August,
and run it again in October when her badge came back,
and it had never moved.
She could not buy his half. Not on ten years of shoeboxes,
not this year, not next,
and she was done pretending the arithmetic would change
if she circled it long enough.
So this was the cost of Ashford, itemized:
a house she could still walk blind,
and one doorframe in it with two children penciled up the wood,
year by year, that some stranger would paint over or, worse, keep.
She was not signing because June said so.
June was seven pages away, and if she waited for June,
the decree would sign it for her,
and she would spend the spring as a woman a thing had happened to.
She had done a year of that once. Never again.
She had spent this year deciding things on purpose,
and in her own favor,
and this was the last decision the old life had any claim on.
She was buying this life. It cost that one.

She patted her coat for a pen and came up empty.
Cal was already crossing the yard from his truck,
her pen out of his shirt pocket and held out to her uncapped,
the same one that had signed the true report at the podium
and ridden above his heart since, on call for change orders.
He didn't say anything. Neither did she.

She signed where the flags said sign.
Initialed where the tabs said initial.
Her chin stayed level and the paper stayed dry,
and the whole thing weighed about one page,
which was more than it sounded like.

Kat waited, hands at her sides, professional to the end of the ink.
The second the pen came down on the rail
she wrapped Marisol up, realtor nowhere in it,
just the friend from before Ashford, squeezing hard.

"There she is," Kat said into her hair.

"Sell it to somebody with kids," Marisol said.

"I'll find them," said Kat.

Inside, while Kat packed the folder,
Marisol flipped the kitchen calendar forward
and tore June out by the staples.
She folded the circled date down to nothing
and dropped it in the drawer where the dead batteries went.
The calendar closed back to November like nothing was missing,
because nothing was.

Kat left with the folder riding shotgun, zipped again, lighter.

---

The tower was ringing noon when Marisol walked Cal
back across the square to his truck.
The square had its game-week colors up on every lamppost,
and the noon crowd was out in coats, moving quick between doors.
Since fall the town had been telling a story
about a kiss on this square that had never happened on it.

Marisol stopped Cal at the tailgate,
put her cold hands flat against his open jacket,
and fixed that.

He tasted like coffee.
His thumb came up against her cheek, cold, and warmed there.
She kept her eyes open just long enough
to watch a crosswalk full of people decide not to cross,
then stopped keeping track of anything at all.
Somebody tapped a horn twice, friendly.
The tower was still ringing when she came back to the world,
and she liked it right down to her boot soles,
that it had landed in daylight, on the square,
with the county holding its breath and its phones.

"Change orders Monday," Cal said through the window.

"I'll be there," she said,
and he pulled out toward the House with her pen in his pocket.

Her phone had been going in her coat the whole time.
The thread already had the kiss,
and had it wrong, top to bottom, gloriously.
She left the phone where it was,
put her hands in her pockets,
and walked up the block to get her lunch.
