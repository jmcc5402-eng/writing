# Chapter One — Third-Largest City

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate: C
Variance card: D7

---

The charcoal was lit by eight in a thousand driveways at once,
and the hickory smoke off Delmar's climbed over Ashford
in a column you could have steered by.

Downtown, the bars were filling before their kitchens were,
doors propped, ceiling fans losing,
a bartender stacking plastic cups in ranks
while two silver beards at the rail
relitigated a game older than the ice machine.

Out at the fairgrounds, an RV city had grown overnight,
five hundred awnings deep, flags snapping, generators humming,
and at its gate a delivery driver stood defeated beside his hatchback,
forty pizzas in his arms
and not one address left that meant anything.

Coolers of seltzer sweated on every tailgate
like ice sculptures on a schedule,
and in twelve thousand pockets at once a group chat lit up
over parking, then over ice,
then over whether anyone had eyes on the cake.

Under all of it ran the drumline,
working the same eight counts somewhere inside the stadium's concrete,
and under the drumline ran the cicadas,
and by nine the two of them had stopped competing
and started trading fours.

Two streets off the cooler parade,
a porch of gray heads roared at a screen,
and in the shadow of the doorway behind them
a man had a woman laughing against the frame,
his hand at her jaw, her fist closed in his collar,
the two of them kissing hard enough to drop a beat of the drumline.
Then the screen door swallowed them, and the morning moved on.
Nobody's business. Everybody's town.

At the center of it all the stadium stood open-mouthed and patient,
ninety thousand empty seats warming in the August sun,
and by kickoff Ashford would have tripled,
and for one Saturday it would be
the third-largest city in the state.

And dead in the middle of all of it,
under the one canopy in Ashford that would not stay up,
Marisol had both hands over her head
and a hundred pounds of folding aluminum coming down to meet them.

It was the third time.
The first time, the canopy had waited until she'd alphabetized
three hundred name tags across two folding tables,
then folded a leg and sent G through K sailing.
The second time it had gone for the sheet cake,
and she had caught the strut one-handed,
six inches over the team-colors frosting,
to a round of applause she had accepted with a bow.

She had earned this day.
Both kids were gone to college,
the house had gone loud with quiet,
and she was a year past the divorce, give or take a lawyer.
Twelve thousand parents in her group had spent the week
asking her where to park, what to bring,
and whether the stadium allowed umbrellas,
and she had answered every one of them
with a patience she did not privately possess.
Today was the trade.
One Saturday that belonged to her
and not to the year she had just survived.
She had worn the good sunglasses and the better jeans,
and half the tent had noticed,
and she had noticed them noticing,
and so far that was the best thing about the morning.

The canopy did not care about any of that.
She got her shoulder under the crossbar,
wrench in her free hand,
and was negotiating with the crank
when a green polo entered her peripheral vision at speed.

A man planted one boot on the base plate,
took the frame away from her
without appearing to take anything,
and reset it in two motions.
One, the strut snapped straight.
Two, the crank seated with a click she'd been chasing for an hour.
The canopy stood, taut and ashamed of itself,
and the man plucked the wrench out of her hand,
gave the fitting a quarter turn, and pivoted to leave,
all in the same unbroken economy, a magician clearing a table.

Twice already that morning she'd watched a green polo
materialize somewhere on the fairgrounds,
solve something, and evaporate.
This time she was faster.
She stepped into his path,
and he stopped an arm's length away,
which put them close enough that she had to look up
and he had to decide what to do about his face.

He was weathered gold,
gray arriving at his temples strictly on its own schedule,
with forearms that had clearly won every argument
they'd ever been in
and a mouth holding out hard against the possibility of a smile.

"You're walking off with my wrench," she said.

He looked down at his hand as though it had acted alone.
He turned the wrench over once. "Wrong size."

"It's the size I own."

"That's how the leg keeps winning."
His voice was low and unhurried,
gravel that had been rained on.

"The leg and I had an understanding. You've interfered."

"I noticed the understanding. From the road."

"And you couldn't stand it."

"I couldn't watch a cake die."

"The cake was never in danger," she said,
which was a lie, and he knew it was a lie,
and something happened at the corner of his mouth
that was not a smile but was related to one.

She put out her hand for the wrench.
He didn't give it back.
She kept her eyes on his while she didn't get it back,
one beat past polite, two beats past casual,
long enough that the tent behind her went interested and quiet.
His eyes were doing something now
that interested was too small a word for.
Neither of them won.

Up close he smelled of cut grass and clean sweat
and the inside of a toolbox,
which should not have worked on her, and did.

"I'll bring you the right size," he said,
and slid her wrench into his back pocket.

"I liked the wrong one."

"I know."
And the crowd took him,
green polo and her wrench and all,
gone between one knot of parents and the next.

Marisol stood in the shade of a canopy
that was suddenly, insultingly sound,
and became aware that she was smiling with her whole mouth,
and made herself stop,
and did not entirely manage it.

Birdie Pham was backing her flower truck through the north gate
with one arm out the window and total faith in her mirrors,
and Marisol, there to collect the tent's table arrangements,
was waving her in past a line of coolers on wheels.

The truck sighed to a stop,
buckets of dahlias and sunflowers packed to its ceiling,
and Birdie hopped down already talking,
about the heat, about the traffic,
about how she'd sold two hundred single stems by nine
and game day hadn't even cleared its throat yet.

Eli Fountain came across the service road
carrying two coffees with the concentration of a man defusing them.
The groundskeeper had grass clippings on his boots
and a clean shirt that still had fold lines in it,
and he stopped in front of Birdie
and stood there while his entire opening remark visibly left him.

"The field's at eleven percent moisture," he said.

"Is that good?" Birdie asked.

"It's ideal."

"Well," Birdie said, glowing at him, "congratulations."

"One of these is yours," Eli said,
and then held both coffees slightly closer to his own chest.

"Which one?"

He looked at the cups with real alarm,
and Birdie laughed,
a big unembarrassed laugh that turned three heads at the gate,
and took the left one,
and Eli watched her take it
as if the coffee were achieving something he had only read about.

Marisol took her buckets and left them to it.
Whatever it was,
it was ten percent conversation and ninety percent future,
and it was the nicest thing she'd seen all morning
that hadn't stolen a wrench from her.

Earlene was cutting the sheet cake into a precise grid
when Marisol got back with the flowers,
and she did not look up to deliver her report.

"That's Cal Sutter," Earlene said,
in the voice she used for barometric pressure.
"Facilities director. Forty-seven.
That man has kept this campus standing
longer than most of the paint."

"I didn't ask," Marisol said.

"Sky's blue, too," Earlene said, and kept cutting.
"Nobody asks about that either."

Marisol arranged the dahlias down the center of the tables
and asked nothing further,
which Earlene, to her credit,
allowed to stand for nearly a minute.

"He has your wrench," Earlene observed.

"He does."

"Mm," Earlene said,
in the voice she used for a front coming through.

By the second half the roar was coming over the trees
in long slow waves,
and Marisol could read the game from the tent
without seeing a single down of it.

The name tags were gone from the tables.
The cake was a memory with frosting.
Three hundred families had come through
and been welcomed and pointed toward their seats,
and her phone had gone off all afternoon
with the group chat's small triumphs and smaller disasters,
and she had run all of it with one eye.
The other eye had spent the entire second half
conducting a private census of ninety thousand people,
sorting every gate, every service cart,
every golf cart and catwalk and concourse shadow,
for one green polo.

She found green polos. She found six of them.
Every one was the wrong man,
wrong shoulders, wrong walk, wrong everything,
and every time her chest did the little drop-and-recover anyway,
and every time she told herself it was about the wrench.

The canopy held all day.
She checked it at the final gun, out of spite,
and it stood there being perfect.
She was almost disappointed.

Teardown went fast
with a stadium's worth of hands drifting past.
The tables folded, the coolers lightened,
Earlene left with the cake knife
and a report on tomorrow's humidity,
and by dusk the tent was a bare frame on flattened grass
and the RV city was lighting its ten thousand lanterns
for the night shift of the same party.

Traffic out of Ashford moved
at the speed of ninety thousand goodbyes,
and Marisol sat in it
with the toolbox on the passenger seat
and the day cooling on her skin,
smelling of hickory and cut grass and a little of frosting.
Somewhere in the line of taillights
a truck's playlist bled over into her cab,
something with horns in it,
and the cicadas kept their end up
from the dark on both sides of the road.

At the edge of town the traffic broke open,
and she put all four windows down.
She hadn't done that since the divorce,
some dim idea about arriving places composed,
hair in order, life visibly handled.
August came through the car at sixty
like it had been waiting a year for the invitation.
It wrecked her hair immediately.

The toolbox rattled beside her,
one slot empty where a wrench ought to have been,
and she let it rattle,
because it was the sound of a Saturday
that had belonged, start to finish, to her.
She turned the playlist up and drove home loud,
smelling of smoke, singing over the cicadas,
hair entirely wrecked.
