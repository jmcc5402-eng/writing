# Chapter One — Game Day

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense
Candidate: A
Variance card: D6

---

On the last Saturday in August, Ashford woke at half past four
in the morning and began to triple.

It started at the fairgrounds, where an RV city had grown overnight,
white and humming, laid out in avenues with flags flying over it,
and by dawn it had a skyline.
A delivery driver idled at the edge of it
with four insulated bags and an address that no longer meant anything,
and after a while he gave up, got out,
and walked into the rows on foot, calling somebody's name,
a man lost in a city that had not existed on Thursday.

Cut to Delmar's, where the hickory smoke went up at six,
a gray column over the pits that you could set a watch by.
People did.

Cut to downtown, where the bars had their doors propped open
and their floors already loud, and to the lots,
where charcoal caught by eight in five hundred chimneys at once
and the smoke stood up over the town like a second population.

The drumline started somewhere behind the stadium at nine
and did not stop.
The cicadas, who had held that job all summer,
listened for a minute and then adjusted their pitch and kept playing.

Cut to a porch two streets off the noise,
where a man with silver at his temples
had a woman laughing back against the rail,
her fingers hooked in his collar,
his mouth an inch from hers and closing,
neither of them a day under forty
and neither of them in any hurry at all.
A flag snapped on the porch post.
The morning moved on without them.

By ten the heat was something you wore.
Coolers of seltzer sweated in truck beds,
playlists argued with the drumline and lost gracefully,
and by kickoff the town would have tripled,
the third-largest city in the state assembled out of nothing in a day,
and gone again by Sunday.

And every cut of the morning, if you followed it far enough,
landed in the same place: a white canopy at the stadium's north gate,
three hundred name tags in alphabetized rows,
a sheet cake in team colors the size of a door,
and a woman in the middle of it all
whose phone had produced four hundred messages before seven o'clock.

Marisol ran the parents' group — twelve thousand members,
most of whom seemed to be awake — and she ran the welcome tent,
and today she was running both in a sundress
she had bought specifically
because it was not a sensible choice for manual labor.

She had promised herself one thing at her bathroom mirror that morning,
with the seriousness of a woman signing a lease.
This Saturday was hers.
Not the lawyers', not the year's,
not the two bedrooms upstairs that had gone off to college
and left the house loud with quiet.
Hers.
She had a tent, a cake, a town, and one day,
and she intended to spend all of it.

The canopy had other plans.

It folded the first time at nine-forty,
a slow diagonal lean that Marisol caught with one hand
while signing a volunteer in with the other.
It folded again at ten-fifteen,
and she climbed onto a folding chair in the sundress,
to scattered applause,
and beat the frame bolts tight with the business end of a wrench.
Earlene, laying out name tags,
observed that this was not what a wrench was for.
Marisol observed that Earlene's section of the alphabet
had fallen behind.

The third time it went down like it meant it,
taking a corner of the cake table with it,
and before she could even get to the chair
a man in a green facilities polo stepped out of the crowd,
planted one boot on the base plate,
and reset the whole frame in two motions.
Lift, lock.
The canopy stood like it was ashamed of itself.

He picked her wrench up off the table, turned it over once,
and shook his head at it.
Then he was turning to go,
the entire rescue apparently meant to happen without a word,
except she was faster.

"That's mine," she said, stepping into his path.

He stopped.
Up close he was weathered gold,
gray coming in at the temples like it had been invited,
forearms that did this sort of thing all day.
Forty-five, maybe more, and wearing every year of it well,
which she found frankly rude of him.

"Whoever tightened these bolts," he said,
"used the wrong end of this."

"Whoever tightened those bolts
kept three hundred families out of the sun all morning."

"Until it fell on the cake."

"Grazed the cake." She held out her hand. "Wrench."

He looked at her hand. He looked at her.
And there the morning snagged,
because she was used to being looked at —
she was forty-three
and had stopped apologizing for enjoying it —
but this was a different verb.
He looked at her with a slow, exact attention,
an estimate being made and revised upward,
and she looked right back and let him feel it,
and the silence went one beat past polite
and neither of them fixed it.

It kept going.
Somewhere to her left Earlene said "Well" to nobody in particular.
The drumline came around the corner of the concourse
and passed and faded, an entire song's worth of drums,
and still nobody had the wrench
and nobody had surrendered anything.
A bead of sweat ran down between her shoulder blades.
His jaw did one small thing and put it away.
She decided she could stand there until the season ended
if that was what the moment required.

He broke first, which pleased her,
and did not give her the wrench, which did not.

"I'll bring it back with the right bolts," he said,
and stepped around her,
and the crowd closed over him like water over a dropped key.

"That was my grandmother's wrench," she called after him,
which was a lie,
and "I know," he called back, which was somehow worse.

"Mm," said Earlene, alphabetizing.

"Not one word," Marisol said, and went back to her name tags,
and noticed, in a distant administrative way,
that her pulse had opinions.

The morning got loud after that in the best ways.
She checked in families until the alphabet blurred.
The group chat asked where to park a forty-foot motorhome, twice,
and she answered the fairgrounds, twice, with love.
The cake quietly lost its damaged corner
to a volunteer with a knife and no witnesses.

At eleven, Birdie Pham's flower truck rolled up to the north gate
with the window down and buckets of zinnias riding shotgun,
and the whole tent smelled suddenly of cut stems and cold water.
Birdie sold game-day bouquets off the tailgate
at a pace that suggested she had done this in a previous life
and been champion at it.

Eli Fountain arrived at eleven-twenty carrying two coffees.
Marisol watched him stand beside the truck for a full minute,
holding both cups level, while he assembled his opening line.

"Grass came in good this year," is what he went with.

Birdie wrapped a bouquet and considered this.
"I heard that about the grass."

"I grew it," Eli said, and then visibly wished to be dead.

"Well, it's the best grass I've parked on."
Birdie took one of the coffees as if it had always been hers,
which, Marisol suspected, from now on it always would be.

Marisol turned away
to give them privacy neither of them knew they needed,
and caught Earlene watching too,
and for a second the two of them grinned at each other
over the name tags,
shareholders in something that had just been founded.

"That's Cal Sutter," Earlene said.

Marisol looked up from the M's. "What is?"

"Your wrench thief." Earlene said it in her weather voice,
the one she used for barometric pressure
and other people's business.
"Facilities director.
He's been holding that campus together
longer than most of the buildings.
You want a gate opened, a pipe unburst,
a canopy shamed into standing, that's who shows up.
Then he leaves before anybody can thank him.
Drives the whole town crazy."

"I wasn't going to thank him," Marisol said.
"I was going to frisk him for my wrench."

"Mm," said Earlene, to the barometric pressure.

Kickoff arrived at two-thirty like a landslide.
Ninety thousand people stood up somewhere over her head
and made a sound she felt in the cake table,
and the town on this side of the gates went from flood to river,
everybody suddenly moving the same direction.

The tent's work thinned.
Marisol restocked, broke down boxes,
sent the volunteers off in shifts to see some football,
and did not admit, even under Earlene's raised eyebrow,
what she was doing with the rest of her attention.

What she was doing
was scanning ninety thousand people for one green polo.
It turned out the facilities department owned a great many green polos
and issued them to men who were, on closer inspection,
not Cal Sutter.
She ran her eyes along the concourse
and got a golf cart with a cooler on it.
She checked the gate crew
and got neither the shoulders nor the walk.
Twice she was certain,
and twice the certainty turned around holding a trash bag.

He did not come back with the right bolts.
The canopy, possibly warned, stood perfectly all afternoon.

By the fourth quarter the light had gone long and gold.
When the stadium finally poured itself back out into the town,
the third-largest city in the state began, slowly, to disassemble.
The RV avenues lit their string lights.
Delmar's smoke went from column to haze.
Somebody's playlist out at the fairgrounds
had reached its slow songs.

They broke the tent down at seven.
The name tags were gone to the last letter.
The cake had been reduced to a smear of team colors on a board,
which Earlene pronounced the sign of a good day.
When the canopy came apart in her hands, easy and obedient,
Marisol gave the frame one small kick on principle.

She walked to her car through the cooling lots,
past charcoal going gray and coolers riding home in wagons,
and her phone buzzed with the group chat's long goodnight —
where's the tent next week, who found a set of keys,
three hundred thank-yous with her name in them.

She answered the keys one
and let the rest wait until morning, which was new.

The house would be quiet when she got there.
It had been quiet for weeks now, the loud kind of quiet,
the kind she had been managing room to room
with all the lights on.
Tonight she thought she might just be tired instead,
which was an enormous promotion.

Somewhere out in the dark, in a town still three times its size,
a man she had known for ninety seconds
had her wrench in his pocket.
She found she was smiling about it,
alone, at a stop sign, like a fool,
and made herself stop, and didn't.

She rolled the windows down
for the first time since the divorce
and drove home through the smell of hickory smoke and cut grass,
cicadas doing the drumline's night shift,
warm air moving through the car with its arm out the window.
Her Saturday.
She had spent every last dollar of it,
and it had been hers, all day, start to finish.
