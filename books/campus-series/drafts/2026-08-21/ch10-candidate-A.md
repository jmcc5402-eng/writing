Doss Farm & Feed had been selling the county its Saturdays
since 1946, and this one was moving briskly.
The team was off playing somebody two states over,
so the store had WDSS going on a radio
older than most of the trucks in the lot,
and every man in the aisles was not listening to it
at the top of his concentration.
The place smelled the same as it had for eighty years,
alfalfa pellets and saddle leather,
creosote drifting in off the fence posts in the side lot.
The one modern note stood floor to ceiling by the register,
a whole wall of coolers in team colors, padlocked to the shelf,
with a QR code on a laminated card zip-tied to the lip,
SCAN TO ENTER THE COOLER DRAWING,
and a second card in ballpoint under it,
SEE SONNY IF THAT DON'T WORK.

Cal had come in for twine.

"Jute or poly?" Sonny Dillard said from the counter,
before Cal had asked anything.
Sonny had watched him come up the string aisle
and had the answer shelved and priced before the question landed.

"Jute."

"Jute rots."

"That's what I like about it."

Sonny took that in and filed it wherever he filed things.
"Rose tying," he said. It was not a guess. Sonny didn't guess.
"October wind will test your knots before it tests the canes."
He set the ball of twine on the counter
and squared it to the register out of habit.

"Anybody win the cooler yet?"

"Drawing's at the Christmas open house," Sonny said.
"Card's been up since June. Four entries.
Three of them are my nephew."

The radio said the visitors were big up front,
and the aisles relayed it inward, shelf to shelf,
to men who were not listening.

"They'll run it straight at us all day," Sonny said.
"I heard their line averages three hundred pounds a man."

"You worried?"

"I don't worry on away Saturdays.
Worry burns daylight, and it's a four-hour drive back either way."

The radio sent the pregame to a commercial for a tractor
nobody in the store could afford,
and the man ahead of Cal in line, seed cap, county boots,
sixteen feet of hog wire panel balanced on one shoulder,
used the quiet the whole county was using it for.

"Sonny. What do you make of all this mess about the House?"

"The House?" Sonny rang the hog wire without looking down.
"Good bones, bad wiring. Same as the rest of us."

"Huh," said the seed cap man, and then, in the same breath,
"you got these panels in a twelve-foot?"

"Tuesday," Sonny said, and the store moved on past it,
a creek going over a dropped stone.

Cal paid cash.
Sonny counted the change back out loud, the old way,
and pushed a peppermint across the counter after it,
store policy older than the cooler wall.

"Burn ban's still on," Sonny said, by way of goodbye.
Sonny held a lieutenant's badge with the volunteer department
and issued the burn ban where other men said take care.

"I'm tying roses, Sonny. Not lighting them."

"That's what everybody says right up until the brush pile."

Cal carried his twine and his peppermint out into the sun.
The line about the wiring rode along with him anyway,
uninvited, the whole six blocks to Millrow.

***

Hartwell House kept daylight hours like any business on the street.
Front door standing open to Millrow,
the way it stood open every day and had for a hundred years,
and nobody at all inside.
An off Saturday made the street quiet enough
to hear the Curb Market running six blocks off,
a low tide of talk and tailgates from the square.
The show mum still held the bottom step,
bronze, magnificent, claimed by nobody,
and watered, lately, by somebody who kept that to himself.

The rosebush lived at the east end of the porch,
a climber, old, thorned like it held opinions,
trained up a lattice that had been rebuilt twice
without a bill ever reaching anybody.
Every fall it asked for the same afternoon.
Dead wood out.
The long new canes bent down and tied
before the October wind came shopping.
Cal set his hat on the rail,
laid the shears and the twine ball on the boards,
and gave himself the ten minutes.

That was the arrangement, and it wasn't written down anywhere
either.
Ten minutes, an off Saturday, a rosebush,
and not one soul to explain anything to.
He missed his wife quietly and on a schedule,
which was also how the House was kept,
and neither of those facts was a coincidence.
The bush kept it simple.
Hands full, nothing to say,
grief cut to the size of one rosebush exactly,
and no bigger, so long as he minded his knots.

The shears did the talking for a while.
Dead wood came out in gray lengths and went in a pile on the boards.
The cicadas ran their shift in the oaks.

Then market paper crinkled at the front walk and quit crinkling.

He didn't turn around.
He knew the paper by its sound,
and he knew the stopped footsteps by their owner,
which he could not have sworn to in court
and would have anyway.
He gave her a two-count to decide,
and heard her decide to go back the way she'd come, quietly.

"Hand me the twine," Cal said, to the rosebush.
"If you're staying."

A pause, one cicada long.

"I wasn't sneaking," Marisol said. "I was retreating."

"Same footsteps."

She came up the steps instead.
She'd dressed for the market on purpose,
since she did everything on purpose,
and the porch got the benefit for nothing.
The zinnias rode one arm in their paper,
orange, paper-wrapped, and still loud.
"These are for the hall table.
Birdie swore this bunch was singing, not arguing."

"Set them on the rail a minute."
He bent the longest cane down one-handed,
a cane that had gotten away from the lattice two summers running,
and held it bowed against the wood like it weighed nothing.
"Hold this one. Right there. She'll fight you."

"It has thorns."

"She. And yes."

"You name everything that fights you?"

"Keeps the argument fair."

"The tent still stands, for the record."

"Never doubted the tent."

Her hands came down on the cane between the thorns
on the first try and stayed where he put them,
steady, no production made of it.
He cut a length of twine off the ball with the shears,
looped the cane to the lattice, and drew the knot up
snug and then a half turn past snug.
For a while it was shears and cicadas
and twine coming off the ball a length at a time,
and neither of them spent a word on any of it.

"My wife planted this bush," Cal said,
conversational as a weather report,
"her fellowship year.
Threatened it every fall she had after that.
Stood right where you're standing and told it
she'd have it dug out by Christmas.
Bush never believed her either."

"Either," Marisol said, careful, like a woman
stepping onto ice she'd been assured would hold.

"It blooms late. Every year.
Later than it's got any right to."
He drew the next knot up and thumbed it flat.
"Out of spite, near as I can tell."

The laugh got out of her whole,
before she could check whether laughing was allowed,
here, on this porch, at that sentence.
Then he watched her face run the check anyway,
one beat late, eyes quick to him and away,
and he could have saved her the trouble.
It was the right answer.
It had been the right answer for years,
and nobody ever risked it.

"Is it going to bloom late this year?"

"Buds are set. October, if she holds to form."

"Then she's still winning," Marisol said.

Something Cal had been carrying across the top of his back,
so long he'd quit billing it as weight,
came down an inch and stayed down.
He tied off the last cane and let it.

"Every year," he said.

She stepped back from the lattice and looked at the job,
the bush drawn down neat for winter, not a bloom on it,
the whole thing holding its buds shut like sealed mail.

"How long does this take you?"

"One afternoon," Cal said. "Same one, every year."

"It's a good afternoon for it," she said,
and let that be everything she said about it.
Then she looked at him,
sleeves back, forearms scratched, hat off,
and took her time about it.

"The garden club would pay money for this view," she said.
"I don't mean the roses."

"Watch the thorns," Cal said, which wasn't an answer,
and was the best he had on hand.

"Those still going to the hall table?" he added,
with a nod at the rail.

"They were."
She considered the zinnias, then the bush,
then the long gray porch that hadn't held a flower all fall.
"The rail gets better light.
She can keep an eye on them out here."

He looked at her then, and she looked back,
and neither of them called time on it,
and it ran one beat past every polite length there was,
and then another.
Up the hill the carillon let noon go, unhurried,
twelve of everything,
and the beat broke on the first bell.

"I was never here," Marisol said, going down the steps.
"You were tying roses.
Nobody held anything for anybody."

"Noted," Cal said.

She went down the walk between the oaks,
and she was smiling, he could tell from the set of her shoulders,
and she aimed it at the street, at the market paper in her hand,
at everything on Millrow that wasn't him.
The paper had quit crinkling by the time he made himself
go back to the bush.

He swept the dead wood off the boards
and stacked it in the yard pile, mindful of Sonny's ban.
The shears got wiped and boxed.
What was left of the twine wound back onto its ball,
and the ball went in his shirt pocket,
where it made a shape his hand kept finding.

The zinnias stood on the rail in their market paper,
orange enough to argue with the whole gray porch,
and they weren't his,
and they weren't exactly the House's either.
He filled the watering can at the east spigot anyway,
carried it up one-handed, full,
and gave them a long, unhurried drink, paper and all,
then set the can back where it lived.

The bush held its buds shut and kept its own counsel.
Late bloomer.
Spite, near as he could ever tell,
and he had never once held it against her.
