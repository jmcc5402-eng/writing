# Chapter 11 — The Night the Mule Ran Out of Glasses

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
(Candidate A — variance card D1, blind run, 2026-08-21.

For the last minute and a half of the game,
the Blind Mule made no sound at all.

A bar built for forty was holding a town.
Marisol had quit counting heads at a hundred,
somewhere back before the pool table went under a sheet of plywood
and retired for the evening.
The TV above the bar was up loud
for the first time since last Saturday,
and under it a hundred-some people breathed in shifts,
so the room never once got to exhale all the way.
Somebody's grandmother had the good stool by the taps.
Somebody's dentist stood on a chair
he had twice been asked to come down from, and hadn't.
Peanut Kyzer stood at his own register with a rag over his shoulder,
pouring nothing, because nobody was drinking.
Drinking was for people whose hands weren't busy being folded.

The team was off playing somebody ranked,
somewhere with worse barbecue,
and the Curb Market had folded its tables at noon,
so the night kickoff belonged to the Mule.
Ashford was up three.
The clock was running and also crawling,
and the whole room leaned at the screen
like the screen might need help.

The snap. The kneel.
One held heartbeat while the town double-checked the math.

Then the Mule went off.

The roar came up through the old floorboards
before it came out of anybody's mouth,
spilled-beer wood giving it back doubled,
and the front windows buzzed in their frames,
and the sound rolled out the propped door
and came back with car horns on it from three streets over.
Marisol was hugged by a stranger, then by the stranger's wife,
then lifted clean off her feet by somebody in a game-day shirt
and passed, hand to hand, down the whole length of the bar,
laughing too hard to apologize to a single person she kicked.
She came back to earth at the far end by the taps,
hair half out of its knot, heart going like the drumline,
and the grandmother on the good stool steadied her with one hand
and toasted her with the other.

The first casualty of the celebration was the glassware.

"Glasses!" Peanut bellowed into the noise, and got nothing back.
"I have got a bar built for forty and half of Doss County in it.
Glasses come home to the bar or nobody pours!"

"Your glasses are in good homes," Earlene Tatum said
from the end of the bar,
where she had claimed a stool early enough to deserve it.

"My glasses are hostages."

"Then negotiate," Earlene said. "You've got the beer."

"Pass them forward," Marisol called, getting up on the foot rail
where the room could see her.
"Jars right, mugs left, and nobody keeps a souvenir."

The relay worked half as well as it should have
and twice as well as anyone expected.
The mason jars came out of the back room first,
a flat of them meant for pickled eggs, promoted on the spot.
Then the chili mugs came down off the shelf,
and the coffee mugs after them,
and a ranked team's worth of beer left that bar in crockery
that said things like WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN.

"The trophy case is not glassware," Peanut called down the bar.
"Whoever has got my softball cup, that's a felony."

"It's a nice cup," the dentist said, and drank from it.

"There's a jelly jar in my purse," the grandmother said.
"Ashford born and raised."

Birdie Pham fought her way in from the truck
with a cut-glass vase the size of a fire hydrant,
handed the zinnias out of it to the grandmother to hold,
and set it on the bar with both hands.

"Deposit's on the house," Birdie said.
"Anything happens to that vase,
this whole bar is buying flowers till Christmas."

"What do I even charge for a vase?" Peanut said.

"By the stem. Pour. It's had worse in it than beer."

"What's worse than beer?"

"Pond water and gladiolas."

He filled it, looking hard at nobody,
and the Liars' Table's whole membership drank from it in turns,
two-handed, formal as communion.

The porch cooler arrived a little after, through the front door,
between two men who were plainly losing to it.
Cal Sutter came off the back wall and took it away from them
by its middle, one man, all the way up
onto the plywood over the pool table,
and set it down level
with about the effort of setting down a mug.
Marisol budgeted one second for that and went over budget.
He was out of the green polo for once,
plain shirt, sleeves pushed back,
and the shirt fit him like it had been consulted first.
Then the crowd closed and gave her the room back.

The cooler was full of ice and alcoholic seltzers,
and Peanut, a man doing license math in his head,
charged a flat dollar a can and called it catering.
Cans needed no glasses.
The crisis eased. The noise did not.

The drumline found the square about then.
They never came inside, and nobody needed them to.
The cadence arrived down the street first,
then the streetlight flash off the rims,
and the Mule sang the fight song facing the open door,
the dentist conducting from his chair.

Her phone buzzed against her hip
in the middle of the second verse.
Her son. Nine days of quiet, and now four words,
all capitals, punctuation like a heart monitor.

DID YOU SEE IT!!!

She stood in the loudest room in the county
and read it four times.
Then she sent back a picture of the bar mid-song,
typed *saw it with a hundred of your closest friends,*
and put the phone away
before she could stand there watching for the dots.

Somewhere in there she ended up behind the bar.
Not by plan.
Peanut's second pair of hands had gone home at ten,
back when the night was only a football game,
and dirty jars were stacking along the drainboard
faster than one man could pour.
So Marisol ran the dish sprayer for twenty minutes,
steam to the elbows,
handing jars back wet into the roar.

"You don't work here," Peanut said, going by with the vase refill.

"Tonight everybody works here."

"You want a shirt?"

"I want a raise."

"Aren't you the one runs the parents' group?"
the grandmother called across the taps.
"You're prettier than your little picture."

"That's the steam," Marisol said. "Does wonders. Jar."

The grandmother laughed and surrendered her jar.

The parents' group had been going off since the kneel,
and when her shift at the sprayer ended
she gave it its lap of honor from the end of the bar,
pinned the celebration thread — *my loves, we WON.
Parking threads reopen Monday.* — and was two scrolls deep
in other people's joy when the joy hit a seam.

*Concerned4Ashford* had found the win too.
The account had grown a banner photo since she'd last looked,
sunset over a stadium that wasn't Ashford's,
and tonight it was quoting town wisdom.

*Like the old timers here always say, you can't save a barn by
hugging it. Nights like tonight are what Ashford is really about.
Time for ya'll to be honest about the firetrap on Millrow. Some
things you love, and some things you outgrow. November can't come
soon enough.*

*AshfordSafetyMom* had agreed with it, in capitals,
inside of a minute.

She turned the phone toward Earlene without a word.
Earlene held it out at arm's length
and squinted down her whole face at it.

"I said that two mayors ago, and not like that.
It was about one particular barn, and that barn had it coming.
Now somebody's put a sunset on it and my words in its mouth."

"You're already page one," Marisol said.

"Then write this on page two.
Somebody stole my words before they stole anything else."

Marisol's thumb did the screenshots without consulting her.
One, two, timestamped,
filed to the folder that went out with the Sunday coffee,
rain or shine or nothing to report.

The next flicker was waiting in her messages,
the politest one yet, from a name she had never once seen.

*Everyone can see how much this group means to you, and how much
you mean to it. Which is exactly why a pause on House topics might
be wisest right now. For balance, and for everything you've built.
Sent with real appreciation.*

She read it twice, standing in the middle of the singing,
and understood the aim of it fine.
Whoever was building these accounts wasn't lobbying trustees;
trustees don't read a parents' group on Grapevine at midnight.
They were lobbying her.

She filed that one too,
set the phone face-down in her tote next to the spiral notebook,
and went back to the night on purpose.

The night, meanwhile, had opened a trial.
Earlene had entered the evidence into the public record,
which is to say she had said it once, down the bar,
at her own pace.

"Somebody out there is wearing a costume and my own sayings,
and using both against the House."

The Mule took up the case between verses.

"That's Tunk Ferrell," the dentist announced from his chair,
with total certainty, from that altitude.
"Tunk bid the deck work. Bid it out loud. Case closed."

Tunk Ferrell sat on the town board for Ward Two,
ran heat and air for half the county,
and if Boyd's parking decks ever got built,
his crews had the inside track
and had never once pretended otherwise.
That was the whole case.
Half the Mule convicted him on it inside of five minutes,
happily, at song volume,
on no evidence a court anywhere would recognize.

"He's got the Wi-Fi for it," somebody offered,
as clinching testimony.

"Motive," the dentist said, ticking fingers. "Opportunity.
And a bass boat he did not have last year."

"Every man in this bar has a boat he didn't have last year,"
Earlene said. "It was a good spring."

"Tunk Ferrell replaced my condenser on a Sunday,"
the grandmother said from the good stool.
"Guilty men don't work Sundays."

"That's exactly when guilty men work," the dentist said.

"Has anybody asked Tunk?" Marisol said.

"Asked him what? He'd only deny it."

"That's generally what asking is for."

"Ask him at the board meeting," Sonny Dillard said.
"Bring his round with you."

Nobody else heard her,
or everybody did and ruled it out of order.
Sonny raised two fingers to Peanut instead.

"Round for Tunk," Sonny said. "Wherever he's at.
Man's either a scoundrel or a customer,
and either way he's buying one back eventually."

"He does my walk-in every August," Peanut said, to nobody.
"Fair man on freon."

The round went down to the end of the bar in a mason jar
and sat there in absentia,
sweating gently on Tunk Ferrell's behalf.
Half the Mule toasted it.
The other half watched it like it might testify.

Marisol got the spiral notebook out of her tote.
The front of it was for the House's people,
Ray and Earlene and Verna and every Saturday still coming,
so she turned past all of that to a clean back page
and wrote, in bar light, in a steady hand,
*Tunk Ferrell?*

"What's the question mark for?" Earlene said.

"Honesty," Marisol said. "It's running short tonight."

The question mark stayed.
She had watched opinions hatch fully grown on a Tuesday,
and a town could convict a man
between verses of its own fight song.
The page could hold the name.
She'd hold the question mark
until something honest took it off.

The night let itself down slow after that.
The drumline played itself out somewhere down the square.
The cooler went to ice water.
The dentist came down off the chair, mostly.
The Mule started handing the town back out the door
in twos and threes,
and Marisol collected her tote,
washed one last jar because it was there
and her hands wanted the useful thing done,
and made the door a little past midnight.

At the door she looked back.
She meant the look for the room,
the mugs and the jars and the vase holding court at the taps,
one more picture of a night
the town would be retelling by Tuesday.

Cal was already looking.

From the back wall, across all of it,
the crew and the crockery and a verse that refused to die,
he had her fixed, steady as a level line,
no smile on him and no apology either.
She held it.
One beat, then the beat after,
long past where a crowded room hands you an alibi.
She did not smile.
She kept her mouth to herself and let the look stay a look,
and the room went to a hum somewhere underneath it.

Then Birdie came through shoulder-high with the vase,
calling "Vase coming home, flowers to follow,"
and Peanut hollered something about deposits,
and the line between the back wall and the door
had a parade in it,
and that was the end of that.

On the sidewalk, in air that finally had some cool in it,
she identified the thing she hadn't heard all night.
The jukebox.
Dark in its corner, cord looped over the top,
unplugged a good hour ago,
and not one soul had missed it.
The town had been carrying its own tune since the kneel.

Behind her, muffled by glass,
somebody started the fight song over again from the top.
She knew every word of it now.
She sang it quiet, the whole way home.
