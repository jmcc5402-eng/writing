The stadium at dawn was the emptiest room in the county.

Work lights burned down on the fifty with nobody under them.
The turf lay mowed into its checkerboard,
every edge sharp as a pressed collar,
one acre of grass set to carry
the whole county's Saturday on its back.
Cal walked the gate line with his key ring
and a list in his head,
and the place made its reports in order.
Latches, sound. Chains, sound. Panic bars, greased and quiet.
His breath showed at the rails.
First morning of the fall it had.

He was at the north tunnel when he heard the cart.

Eli Fountain drove his utility cart at one speed,
a walking pace with an engine under it,
and it came up the tunnel with the headlights off,
because Eli would sooner squint than glare at his grass.
Two coffees rode in the rack by the wheel.
Birdie Pham rode beside him in a canvas jacket,
both hands around one of the cups,
watching the tunnel mouth where the light was starting.

Cal had the gate unlocked before the cart stopped rolling.
He swung it wide and held it,
and Eli looked at him one second across the idle,
and Cal tipped his head toward the field,
and that was the whole conversation.

"Morning, Cal," Birdie said, going by.

He lifted two fingers off the gate rail.

Eli parked on the apron where the concrete met the grass
and would drive no farther, because the stripes were in.
He came around and handed Birdie down off the cart,
though the step was eight inches
and she hauled water buckets for a living,
and she let him,
which told anybody at any gate everything worth knowing.

"You can step on it," Eli said.

"You're sure."

"It's grass, Birdie. It's built for cleats."

"You rope off the logo in the spring. I've seen the ropes."

"That's the spring."

She stepped out onto the checkerboard
like a woman joining a dance floor between songs,
and stopped in the middle of the fifty,
and put her arms out,
and turned one slow circle under the work lights.
The bowl, the seats, the sky going pale at the rim, all of it,
and when she came back around,
her face had gone the kind of open
the flowers usually got to themselves.

"It's the biggest flower bed in the state," Birdie said,
"and you planted the whole thing green."

"It flowers," Eli said.

"It does not."

"Saturdays, around one." He looked up into the seats,
all sixty rows of them, and said it plain.
"Ninety thousand. Full bloom."

Her laugh went up the empty bowl,
and the bowl handed it around the rim
and gave it back to her doubled from the far side.
She spun to face the sound of her own self.

"Do it again," Eli said. "Holler something."

"I'm not hollering."

"Everybody hollers."

"FRESH ZINNIAS," Birdie hollered,
and sixty rows of concrete sold them right back,
and the two of them stood in the middle of the fifty
listening to the last of it climb out.

"How early do you start, to get it like this?" she said.

"Four."

"Four in the morning. For grass."

"For this grass."

"And if it rains tomorrow?"

"She drains in eleven minutes," Eli said.
"I built her that way."

"You built the rain a schedule."

"Somebody had to."

"Who else gets to stand out here?" she said, quieter.

"Nobody walks my field."

"Nobody."

"Band director, twice a year, and he hears about it.
The kicker, when he's earned it." Eli looked at the grass.
"Now you."

"Why me, Eli?"

Eli consulted the middle distance a good while
and came back with, "You grow things.
I wanted you to see the big one."

Somewhere in there her cup ran dry.
Eli traded her, his full cup for her empty one,
no ceremony in it, a man swapping fittings,
and the cup never once touched his mouth going or coming.
Eli Fountain did not drink coffee. Never had,
so far as the whole county knew, and the county knew.
He had carried two of them every morning of this fall anyway.
Both cups had been hers the whole time.
One of them just took the long way around.

Cal stood at the gate one beat past what the job required.
He had a wrench on a clean rag back at the shop
and a place card riding in a jacket he hadn't worn since,
and neither one had ever made the trip
that coffee made every morning.
He was in no position to grade another man's system.
Eli's worked.

Across campus the carillon took up its morning scales,
one bell at a time, a big instrument warming its hands,
and Cal came off the gate and left it standing open for them.
He never said a word the whole visit,
and neither of them heard him go.

He went out through the service lot the long way,
past the north lawn where a welcome tent
had folded three times one Saturday in August,
and he did not examine why he went that way,
because there was nothing to examine,
and the truck was mostly in that direction anyway.

The tripods came to Millrow at nine.

The crew was two men Cal had never met,
sent by the development office,
a level man with gray in his mustache
and a younger one who carried the cases.
They set three tripods in a ring off the east corner
and pointed everything they owned at the crack.

The crack was no secret. It never had been.
It came up out of the east foundation
and stair-stepped through six feet of old brick,
following the mortar joints,
wide as a pencil lead at its worst,
the House's one scar, older than anyone left to say so.

"How long's this been here?" the level man asked.
"It's not in any file we were given."

"Longer than me," Cal said. "It's in the brick.
That's the file. She settled a long time back and quit.
Buildings do that."

The younger one wrote that down,
then looked like he wished he hadn't.

Ratchet held the porch and graded the visitors from the top step.
Rental truck. Thin gloves. Careful shoes.
She kept her tail still all morning
and made sure Cal saw her keeping it.

"She bite?" the younger one asked, at the steps.

"She grades," Cal said. "Biting's beneath her."

"Two hours, maybe three," the level man said.
"We shoot the corners off the benchmarks,
gauge the crack, photograph everything twice."

"Door's open till dark," Cal said.
"After dark it isn't. House rules, not mine."

They worked clean, he'd give them that.
Corners off two benchmarks, the gauge at four heights,
every reading photographed in place.
The level man read the numbers out
and the younger one wrote them down.

"Thirty-two thousandths. Thirty-two. Thirty-two again."
The level man straightened up. "Well. She's consistent."

"She's done moving," Cal said.

"They all say that."

"Some of them are right."

They wanted a look at the footing before they packed,
and the bulkhead doors out back had swelled shut with the wet,
and the two of them rocked the handle in turns
and then stood looking at it with their arms crossed,
a posture that has never yet opened anything anywhere.
Cal reached past, took the handle,
and broke the doors loose one-handed,
the whole pull riding up through his forearm
and going nowhere else.
He laid the door back on its rest so it wouldn't slam.

"That door was stuck," the younger one said, to the yard.
"I want it in the record that the door was stuck."

"It's noted," Cal said.

They went down with flashlights
and gave the footing by the stairs its ten minutes,
measured what the sheet told them to measure,
and came back up.
Their lights never went past the boiler.
Nobody's sheet sent them past the boiler.
Cal stood in the yard with his arms folded
and watched them not walk the far run of the wall,
and said not one thing about it.

They packed at noon, cases first, tripods last.

"Good brick," the level man said, winding a cord.
"My granddaddy pointed chimneys in three counties.
Nobody strikes a joint like that anymore.
They quit teaching it."

"They quit asking for it," Cal said.

"When's the write-up?" Cal asked, at the truck.

"We just take the numbers," the level man said,
in the voice of a man who had been asked before.
"Somebody else does the grading."

"Never doubted it," Cal said.

The truck took the corner past the two oaks and was gone.

There was a jar of zinnias on the east porch rail,
market flowers a week old and holding,
and Cal topped the jar up at the hose bib on his way past,
which he would not have called a habit,
though the zinnias could have.

Then the House was his.
He went in through the open front door
and down the cellar stairs by feel,
past the boiler standing quiet in its off-season,
to the far run of the east wall,
where the crack came down through the old lime plaster
and no scope sheet had ever sent anybody.

He put his light on the wall.

Glass slides, a row of them, glued across the crack.
Little bars of glass set down the run of it,
each one bridging the brick on both sides of the gap,
because glass does not stretch and glass does not lie.
If the wall so much as breathed, the glass let go.
A snapped slide was a wall on the move.
A whole one was a wall holding still.

Every slide on that wall was whole.

Under each one, on a strip of paper gone tea-colored,
a date, in ink, in his wife's small square hand.

He didn't need the light to know the dates.
He read every one of them anyway, top to bottom,
and crouched at the last one, down low by the floor,
and put his thumb, light, at the edge of the glass.
Whole. The ink gone brown. Nineteen years old this fall.
Nineteen years, and not one pane of that glass had broken.
The wall had not moved a hair
since her hand wrote the first date under it.
She had loved this house enough to doubt it properly.
She had written the proof out by hand,
and the proof had outlived her.

He stayed down there a while. The cellar didn't mind.

He took out his phone and photographed the run of them,
slide by slide, dates and all, flash on,
and sent the pictures to nobody.

"You heard nothing," he told the boiler, going up.

Ratchet had come halfway down the stairs and stopped,
which was her entire position on cellars,
and stood there breathing at him until he took the hint.

He left the front door open to the afternoon on his way out,
because the House kept daylight hours
and the steward kept the dark ones.

The survey protocol landed in his mail at four.
Nine pages. No name on the cover,
the same as the invite back in August had carried none.

He read it in the truck at the end of the day,
parked in the motor-pool yard with the crew gone home
and Ratchet fed and sitting in the passenger seat,
and when the yard light quit reaching the page,
he turned on the dome light and read it again.

Page three had the sentence.
Every structure on the Millrow list would be graded
against current code. This year's code. Written for new work.
Stair rises taken to this year's inch.
Wiring judged against this year's book.
Egress widths out of a chapter
no building on that street was young enough to have heard of.

By that test the House had failed from the truck,
before a single tripod came off the racks.
By that test every old building he'd ever kept standing failed,
and the men who wrote page three knew it when they wrote it.

He read it twice, to be fair to it.
It came out the same both times.
The grade had been written before the measuring.
The tripods were theater with good posture.
The word Boyd wanted was already sitting in the rules,
waiting on one signature to stand under it.

And against page three stood a cellar wall
with nineteen years of whole glass on it,
and her handwriting under every pane.
The strongest evidence in the county was written in her hand,
and there was nobody he could show it to yet
who wouldn't ask how he came to know that wall so well.
She had promised, on a porch full of witnesses,
to fight his pen with everything she had.
She didn't know yet that the pen came loaded from the factory.
He did. As of tonight, he was the only one who did.

There was a fresh manila folder in the seat pocket
behind the clipboards, and he took it out
and wrote nothing on the tab.
Into it went the day's field notes in his own shorthand.
The photographs would go in tomorrow,
off his own printer, at his own kitchen table.
Not the office machine. Not the office anything.

Ratchet put her chin on the armrest and supervised.

"Not one word," Cal told her.
"Not to the crew. Not to the porch.
Not to anybody holding a pen."

Her tail went once against the seat.
Half the editorial she'd spent on it in August.
She knew a bigger secret when she was keeping one.

He turned off the dome light and let her out at her spot,
and the yard settled, and the gate took its chain.
The folder rode home on the bench seat, face down,
where the phone usually did its riding.
Out past the square the House stood dark on its street,
front door locked by now, side door minded,
the scar on its east wall holding still,
the same as it had held still every night for nineteen years.
Only now there were two of them keeping the count.
