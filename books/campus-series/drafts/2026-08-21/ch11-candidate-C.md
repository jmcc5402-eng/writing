The first cold front of the fall and the biggest win in twenty years
hit Ashford on the same Saturday night,
and the town never did settle which one it was drunk on.

The front came through at halftime and meant it.
Twenty degrees fell out of the evening in an hour,
and a town that had dressed for August at noon
was passing one denim jacket down the sidewalk by dark
like a bucket at a fire.

The Blind Mule was built to hold forty people.
By the fourth quarter it was holding a town.
Spilled-beer wood underfoot, the cigarette ghost of thirty years ago
still up in the ceiling tiles,
and the TV, which ran dark all week and earned its keep on Saturdays,
turned up loud enough to argue with the room and losing.
The team was two states away, playing the sixth-ranked team
in the country, and refusing, with two minutes left,
to be sorry about anything.
Marisol had the rail, wedged between somebody's grandmother
and somebody's dentist,
and she had given up pretending she was calm
somewhere back in the third quarter.

"If they run it here I'm leaving," the dentist announced.

"You've left four times," Marisol said. "You're still holding my arm."

"I'm steadying you."

The kick went up with no time on the clock,
and the whole bar leaned with it,
and then the Blind Mule came off its foundations.

She never got a vote on what happened next.
The room moved as one animal, and she went with it,
passed hand to hand down the length of the bar
like the last full pitcher at a family reunion,
laughing too hard to apologize to any of the shoulders involved.
Somebody's grandmother had both fists in the air.
The dentist was hugging a stranger and weeping frankly.
Out front the door got propped open into the brand-new cold,
and half a drumline came up the sidewalk out of nowhere,
pieces of uniform over street clothes,
and stood in the doorway light hammering the fight song
until the glasses walked themselves to the edges of the tables.
Nobody let them in. Nobody needed to.
The cadence came through the propped door with the cold air,
and the bar sang the words back out at the street,
wrong in three places and unanimous.

It was the glasses that broke first.

"That's it," Peanut Kyzer hollered from behind his own bar,
holding up two pint glasses the way a man holds the last
two sandbags in a flood. "That's the fleet. I'm out."

"You can't be out," Sonny Dillard said. "It's nine o'clock."

"Built this place for forty people, Dillard.
Count what's in it and tell me whose fault this is."

Marisol was close enough to see the actual problem,
which was that the sink held more glassware than the shelves ever had
and nobody behind the bar had a free hand to wash one.
She was around the end of the bar before she decided to be,
sleeves back, hip against the cooler.

"Give me the sink," she said.
"You pour, I rinse, and somebody go look in that storeroom.
Nobody runs a bar this old without a flat of mason jars in the back."

"Two flats," Peanut admitted.

"Then tonight you run a canning operation. Go."

The jars came out dusty and went into service by the dozen.
When the jars ran low, the coffee mugs came off the pegboard,
a parade of mismatched churches and bass tournaments,
and when the mugs ran low, Peanut stood a full minute
in front of the seltzer cooler like a man at a graveside
and then dragged it around behind the bar
and started handing the cans across on no particular system.
Somebody's grandmother ended up with a Pyrex measuring cup,
a half-pint of beer sitting exactly on the one-cup line,
and defended it the rest of the night from all comers.

"This is a candle holder," the dentist said, peering into his mug.

"It's a mug," Marisol said, rinsing.

"It says Ashford Sportsman's Club 1988."

"Then it's a collectible, and you're welcome."

Birdie Pham's flower vase went last and went hardest.
It was riding the end of the bar with six zinnias in it,
because it was Birdie's night off and the truck was parked outside,
and Peanut looked at it twice and then looked at Birdie.

"Don't," Birdie said.

"Pham."

"That's crystal. It was my mother's."

"It's October and I'm serving beer out of a rain gauge."

Marisol dried her hands, lifted the zinnias out,
stood them in the water pitcher with some ceremony,
and slid the vase down the bar to Peanut.

"On loan," she said. "Washed, returned, and he owes you flowers."

"He owes me a headstone," Birdie said, but she let it go,
and the vase came back down the bar two minutes later
full of draft beer and dignity, and the whole rail toasted it.

Her phone went off against her hip in the middle of the toasting.

*MOM.*

*MOM DID YOU SEE.*

*TELL ME YOU SAW*

She had it out and answered before the third one landed.
*Saw it. Screamed. A dentist cried on me.*
Then she stood one second in all that noise
with her thumb resting on the screen,
her boy's little gray dots going and stopping and going,
the first fall of her life where a win this size
happened somewhere she couldn't reach over and squeeze his shoulder.
Birdie set a bar napkin by her elbow without comment
and went back to supervising her vase.

*LOVE YOU. GO CELEBRATE,* he sent, like he was the parent,
and Marisol put the phone away and laughed at herself
and took the beer Peanut handed her in a jar with no lid and no label,
which by then was top-shelf service.

Cal Sutter was in the corner with his crew.

She'd known it for an hour, the way you know where the door is.
The cold had put half the town in flannel tonight,
and it was doing him a row of favors she declined to itemize,
the sleeves turned back, the shoulders under it
making the corner look small and well organized.
His crew had the jukebox table,
and the jukebox had been dark since the fourth quarter,
when the room had needed every inch of the broadcast call
and Cal had reached behind the machine without getting up
and pulled the plug one-handed, not even looking,
the way other men reach for their own coat.
Nobody had plugged it back in.
Nobody had missed it.
The town was the music tonight, and he'd known it an hour
before the town did.

Her phone buzzed again while she was not watching him.

It was the group on Grapevine, running hot with the win,
four hundred posts of scoreboard photos and hollering capitals,
and riding in the middle of all that joy, shiny as a new dime,
a graphic from *Concerned4Ashford*, cursive over a stock sunset.

*Old Ashford wisdom: ya'll can't save a barn just by loving it.
Sometimes progress is how a town says thank you.*

Earlene Tatum read it over Marisol's shoulder,
because Earlene read everything over everybody's shoulder,
and squinted at the screen a long moment.

"I said that two mayors ago, and not like that," Earlene said.

"You said that?"

"About a barn. An actual barn, with an actual tree through it."
Earlene straightened up, ninety percent offended,
ten percent flattered, all cattle rancher about the ratio.
"Now it's got a sunset on it."

Marisol's thumb did the screenshots before the rest of her voted.
One, two, timestamped, filed, the tidy motion
of a habit with calluses on it.
Sunday the logs would go out with the coffee, same as every Sunday.

The second one came in quieter, the way the second one always did.
A name she'd never seen, three weeks old, no photo,
polite as a sympathy card.
*So grateful for all you do for this community.
With feelings running so high, a lot of us quietly feel
the group would benefit from balance right now.
Sometimes the strongest leadership is neutrality.*

The politest one yet. They were improving.
And the thing she knew in her back teeth, filing it,
was that none of this was aimed at any trustee,
because no trustee alive was reading a parents' group
at ten o'clock on a Saturday night.
Whoever they were, they were lobbying her.

She put the phone face-down in her tote,
on her own schedule, and picked her jar back up,
because it was the best night this town had thrown all year
and she had decided at five forty one morning in August
whose Saturdays these were.

The trial started at the pool table around eleven.

"I'll say it out loud," Sonny Dillard said,
setting his mug down like a gavel.
"Deck bid's public record. Tunk Ferrell's outfit is on it
for both parking decks.
Somebody's been paying for pictures and typing up strangers,
and I know which somebody's got the invoice waiting."

"A bid's not a confession," the dentist said.

"It's not a character reference either."

Half the bar convicted Tunk on the spot, happily,
with the ease of people who had wanted a name more than a fact,
and the sentence was carried on a voice vote and a round of jars.
The other half took up a defense that was mostly sentiment
and window units.

"Tunk Ferrell carried my air conditioners up those stairs
two Julys running," Verna Poteat said from the shady end of the bar,
"and never once sold me freon I didn't need.
Put a beer on my ticket for him, wherever he is tonight."

"You want it in a jar or a mug?" Peanut said.

"In a glass."

"Woman, read the room."

So the Mule bought a sympathy round for a man it had just convicted,
in absentia, in a flower vase,
and both halves of the bar felt righteous going into midnight,
and it was all warm, and it was all neighborly,
and not one soul in the building had shown anybody a fact.

Marisol got out the spiral notebook.

She turned past the House's people, past Ray and Earlene and Verna,
all the way to the back page, where the questions lived,
and wrote *Tunk Ferrell* in her market-list hand,
and then, after a second, a question mark after it,
pressed in dark enough to read from a distance.

"You writing him down?" Earlene said.

"With punctuation."

Earlene looked at the page, then at the pool table,
where the conviction was being celebrated and appealed
at the same volume.

"What's the mark for?"

"It's the difference between a list and a mob," Marisol said.

Earlene considered that the way she considered a sky in April.
"Keep the pen," she said. "Town's got plenty of chalk."

Last call came and went and didn't take.
Peanut called it twice more with declining conviction
and finally just started washing.
The drumline had gone home to wherever drumlines go.
Through the propped door the cold came in clean as well water,
and out in the gravel three trucks ran WDSS with the windows down,
callers relitigating the kick for the fortieth time,
every one of them opening with "I'll be brief."
Somewhere up the hill the tower struck twelve
into air finally cold enough to carry it all the way down Millrow.

Marisol collected her tote and Birdie's zinnias,
promised the measuring-cup grandmother a ride Sunday,
and made the door on the third try,
handed along by the same shoulders that had passed her up the bar
three hours and one entire season ago.

At the door she looked back.

Cal was already looking.

Not caught looking. Looking, settled in it,
his forearms on the jukebox table and the corner quiet around him,
that weathered face carrying the night easy,
and the look crossed the whole loud length of the Blind Mule
like it had the room to itself.
She held it. One beat, then the beat after,
past the place where either of them could have filed it
as an accident, and the unplugged jukebox sat there beside him
saying nothing, the way it had all night,
and neither of them fixed that either.

"Pham!" Peanut hollered from the sink. "Your vase!
This is a bar, not a florist!"

The room laughed, and the look let go without anybody dropping it,
and Marisol went out into the first cold of the year.

She walked home with the zinnias against her shoulder
and her breath showing, a whole new fact of the season,
porch lights coming on ahead of her like the town saying good night
one house at a time.
The notebook rode in the tote with one name and one question mark,
which was more honesty than the pool table had managed.
And the look she'd carried out the door of the Blind Mule
stayed warm against the cold the whole way home,
which was not a thing she planned to log,
Sunday or any other day.
