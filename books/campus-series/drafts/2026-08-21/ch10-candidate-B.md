Cal ran an away Saturday off a list.

Shop till eight, radio low. Parts run at nine.
The House by ten, while the day still belonged to him.
On an away Saturday the crowd noise was two states gone,
the square belonged to the Curb Market,
and nobody needed him to attend one thing.
He meant to keep it that way clear till dark.

Doss Farm & Feed had the pregame going
on a radio older than most of the inventory,
and the store smelled of alfalfa pellets and leather
and the creosote soaked into the dock boards,
which was the smell of 1946 minding its own business.
The chick brooders at the back stood empty for the fall.
One whole wall was coolers now,
white and stacked and lit like appliances,
priced like livestock.
The county and the town stood in the same checkout line,
which was most of what the store was for.
Ratchet stayed with the truck
and ran the parking lot from the window.

Cal set his goods on the counter.
Hose washers. Fence staples. A spool of jute twine.

Sonny Dillard rang them up in the order they landed.
Counterman since he could see over the counter,
fire lieutenant on the volunteer side,
and level as a good floor.
"Jute's two for three this week," he said.
"Take the second spool."

"One'll do it."

"Two for three, Cal. It's arithmetic."

Cal took the second spool.

Down the aisle a man in a golf visor stood at the cooler wall
with his hands clasped behind his back, museum style.
"This top one really four hundred dollars?"

"Yes sir," Sonny said.

"What's it do?"

"Holds ice."

The man nodded, slow and respectful, and went on looking.

On the radio, a long way off, the band struck up
under Tick Moran's pregame,
and Sonny reached back and gave the volume one notch
without being asked, which was the store's whole civic policy.

Sonny bagged the washers.
A QR code was taped to the register now,
square and official, next to the fire department raffle jar,
and the jar was winning by a landslide of ones.
"Whole county's been through that door this week
wanting to hear my thoughts on the House," Sonny said.
"Like I stock opinions."

"Do you?"

"The House? Good bones, bad wiring. Same as the rest of us."
He laid the receipt flat in the bag.
"Raffle's a gun safe this year. Drawing's at the fish fry."

"My crew knows," Cal said. "It's half of what they talk about."

"Then I'll see you at the fry," Sonny said,
and turned to the visor man,
who had questions about ice retention.

***

Millrow on an away Saturday was porch flags and sprinklers
and a radio going in every third window,
the whole street following the same far-off game
politely, at different volumes.
Hartwell House stood back behind its two oaks
with the front door open to the daylight,
the same as it stood open every day the sun was up.

The climber rose lived on the east porch.
It had the east rail and half the trellis,
old wood at the base gone thick as his wrist,
and a summer's worth of new cane run out long and green
and certain it would get away with it.
It would not. The first hard front of the fall
would whip those canes raw against the trellis.
That was what the twine was for.

He did this job once a year.
He did it with the radio off and the truck out front
and the door open behind him,
and it had never once been on a list,
because a list was a thing other people could read.
He'd put the edge back on the shears at the shop that morning
for no job he had named out loud.

He cut the dead wood out first.
Then the tying. Cane drawn in, laid to the lattice,
tied soft, with room in the knot.
Tie a rose tight and the wind treats the tie as a dare.

He was three canes in when the gate spoke.

He knew the step that came up the walk.
He could have sworn to it in front of a judge,
and did not examine when that had happened.
The step reached the bottom of the porch stairs.
It stopped there. Then it started back the other way.

"Hand me that twine," Cal said, without turning around.

Both his hands were full of rose.
The spool sat on the rail six feet behind him,
which he'd have written up as poor planning in an employee.
The step changed its mind again.
The spool arrived in his open hand.

"I wasn't sneaking off," Marisol said.

"You were the other thing, then."

"Retreating in good order. There's a difference.
Ask any general."

She'd come straight from the market.
Saturday clothes, a yellow dress
that had conceded nothing whatsoever to October,
and a coffee can of zinnias riding her hip,
orange ones, the arguing kind.
He took the census of all of it in one glance
and went back to the rose,
which was slower to notice things.

"Those for the hall table?" he said.

"That was the plan. Birdie says they don't keep,
and neither does she." She set the can on the rail.
"I didn't know the House had roses.
The town mentions everything, and it never mentioned this."

"East porch. You have to want to be back here to see it."

"And I interrupted."

"You're about to be useful. Hold these two.
Mind the thorns."

She took hold of the canes bare-handed,
high, where he pointed, and held them still.
No flinch about the thorns, and no theater about it either.
A grip he could tie against.
He tied against it and kept the finding to himself.

"Why tie them at all?" she said.
"They made it this far on their own."

"Wind. First cold front through here
whips the long canes to ribbons on that lattice.
You tie the year's growth in ahead of the fronts."
He set the next knot. "And she's got a bloom coming,
whatever the calendar thinks about it."

"A bloom coming. It's October."

"Most of what I do all day
is for some month that isn't this one."

She was quiet a second, holding her two canes.

"Whose are they?" she said. "And don't say the trust's."

For a moment the only sound on the east porch
was twine coming off the spool.

"Hers," Cal said. "My wife planted it.
Her fellowship year."

The canes did not move in Marisol's hands.
She didn't say the thing people said.
She left him a quiet, and it was the right size,
and he tied the next cane in it.

"It's an old climber," he said. "Blooms late.
October, most years.
Some years it makes the whole street wait for November."
He drew the last long cane in against the wood.
"Out of spite."

The laugh was out of her before she could ask anyone's leave,
her whole face in it, quick and bright,
and then he watched her catch it and check,
one beat, the face turning to the porch
to ask whether laughing was allowed here.

"It's allowed," Cal said. "It's a funny rose."

And somewhere in the saying of it
his shoulders came down an inch.
He felt them go. He didn't call them back.

She watched him work a while after that,
arms crossed, head tipped, auditing.

"It's a lot, you know," she said.

"It's twelve canes."

"It's rose-tying, Cal. The sleeves, the forearms,
the little soft knots.
There are women in this county
who would pay cash money for this view.
I'm going to have to not tell anybody."

"Appreciate it."

"It's not a favor to you. It's crowd control."

He tied the next knot and let the corner of the morning
be pleased about itself without any help from his face.

Her tote buzzed against her hip.
She reached in without looking and thumbed it quiet.

"Twelve thousand people," she said,
"and every last one of them
can park themselves for one hour."

"They know it's your Saturday?"

"They're learning."

"Fellowship year," she said, after a while,
in the tone of a woman setting a phrase on a shelf
where she could find it again.

"Long time ago," Cal said, and reached for the twine.

She let it be.
He heard what the letting cost her,
a held half-second with a question folded inside it,
and he was grateful in a way
he had no plans to disclose to anyone.

"Move your left hand down a hand's width," he said.

She moved it. He tied off the last cane she held,
and she stepped back and looked at the wall of it,
the whole year's growth drawn in and lying quiet,
green against the old trellis, waiting on its own stubborn bloom.

"Verna's expecting me at two," she said.
"For the notebook. I'm told the good stories run you a pie."

"You heard right. Bring a big one.
She'll talk you clean through supper."

"I'm counting on it." She picked up her tote.
She did not pick up the zinnias.
"Those are staying," she said, with a nod at the can.
"Somebody's got to hold the east side till November."

He looked at her then, and she looked back,
and it went on past the length either of them had budgeted.
Up the hill the carillon took up noon, unhurried,
twelve of everything,
and somewhere around the fourth bell the glance let go
and the porch came back around the two of them.

"Enjoy the pie," Cal said.

"I'll be eating half of it. That's the loophole."

She went down the walk and out the gate,
and her step ran off up Millrow at market pace,
and he listened to it clear to the corner
while telling himself he was listening for the game.

***

He finished the last of the tying alone.
It went slower.
He would have testified the job went faster with the help
and slower without,
and he made no study at all of why that should be.

The coffee can sat on the rail,
orange and loud against a hundred years of white paint.
Cut flowers in October. A week in them, maybe.

He packed the tools into the truck.
Then he came back up the steps with the can,
carried it to the spigot at the porch's end,
and topped the water up till it nearly ran over,
which cut zinnias did not strictly require, and got anyway.
He set them back on the rail square to the street.

The roses were tied in for whatever the wind had planned.
They were hers, and they'd bloom when it suited them,
October or November, out of spite, seen or not.
The zinnias were company till then.
He stood on the east porch a minute in the noon quiet,
minding both,
and out at the curb Ratchet lifted her head in the truck window
and minded him.
