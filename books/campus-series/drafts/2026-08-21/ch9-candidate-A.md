Marisol took the corner at Beech Street at four in the morning,
three blocks from her own bed and gaining,
walking like a woman with an appointment she did not have.

The house had spent another night doing its new trick,
holding perfectly still and humming,
and somewhere around three she had quit negotiating with the
ceiling, laced her sneakers in the dark,
and left all that quiet sitting in the kitchen
to think about what it had done.

The town at four belonged to the sprinklers.
Porch lights held their small territories.
A possum crossed Marsh Street on personal business
and declined to acknowledge her.
Her phone buzzed once against her hip,
a mother in the group two time zones behind and wide awake,
whose freshman had a seven a.m. flight and a car that wouldn't
start.
Marisol typed the airport shuttle number from memory,
added *pinned post, my love, first run's at five,*
and put the phone away,
one small thing done right before the day had even applied.

The smoke found her at the foot of Millrow.

Hickory, low and wide in the dark,
the column off Delmar's pit standing over the square
the same as it stood over game days,
except now it had the whole sky to itself.
She had not planned to walk up Millrow.
She had also not planned not to.
She followed the smell the last two blocks
past storefronts still dreaming,
and the Magnolia Court's neon held its little pink weather
down the cross street, the only color on duty.

Delmar's was dark. The pit shed behind it was not,
one bulb burning over the smoker,
smoke leaking up through the seams,
patient as a kettle just starting to think about it.

"Screen door's unlocked," said the dark screen porch.

She stopped on the sidewalk with her heart going.
Then her eyes caught up to the hour.
Cal Sutter sat on Ray's screen porch at half past four
in the morning, in the corner chair,
a thermos on the rail in front of him,
and his chair was turned to face up Millrow,
which she noticed, and filed, and did not ask about.

"You always invite strangers onto Ray's porch in the dark?"

"Nobody strange is out at this hour," he said.
"Just neighbors and possums. Come up if you want."

She went up. The spring on the screen door had been oiled
down to a whisper, which told her whose hand had oiled it.
Two chairs stood on the porch with a milk crate between them,
and on the crate, face down, two enamel cups.

He turned one over and poured before she had picked a chair.
No questions came with it.
Not what she was doing out, not whether she was all right,
not one single why in the whole transaction,
just the coffee going into the cup, level and unhurried,
steam standing straight up in the still air,
and the cup arriving in her hand handle first.

She had answered four thousand questions since August.
She had been asked, kindly, by people who loved her,
what she was going to do with herself now,
in every register that question owned.
It had been months since anybody had simply handed her
something warm and let her alone inside it.
Her eyes went hot for exactly one second,
which the dark, an old friend of hers, kept.
Down at the square one window was already lit,
the Checkerboard's kitchen door propped open on the night shift,
and the sight of it did something small and good
underneath everything else.

"Sit anywhere," Cal said. "The good chair's the one you're in."

She had already sat.
The coffee was pit coffee, strong enough to vote,
and it improved her opinion of four in the morning considerably.

"Does Ray know you haunt his porch?"

"Leaves it unlocked on purpose.
I keep an eye on his smoke, he keeps a chair out.
Been the arrangement a long time."

"And you just sit out here."

"I listen," he said. "Town comes on in order.
Best show there is, and nobody watches it."

The quiet came back and sat down with them.
It was a different make of quiet than the one at her house,
occupied, warm at the edges, smelling of hickory.
She turned the cup once in her hands.

"Somewhere on that campus of yours," she said,
"a man is still holding my wrench hostage."

"He said he'd bring you the right one."

"He did say that. It's been a month of Saturdays."

"He's aware," Cal said, and topped off her cup,
and neither of them took it one word further,
and the debt sat on the rail between them,
comfortable as the thermos.

The sky had not changed yet, but something in the air had,
the dark going thin at its far edge.
Cal nodded down toward the square.

"All right. Since you're here.
Checkerboard griddle or the newspaper window.
Which comes on first?"

"The Checkerboard never closes."

"Never closes. Wakes up all the same.
Night side runs coffee and the back corner of the flat-top.
Five ten, the morning cook fires the whole griddle
and the vent fan kicks, and you'll smell it from here.
Question is whether the Courier's window beats it."

She considered the square with the seriousness
the stakes deserved, whatever they were.

"Stakes first," she said.

"Pie. Loser buys, winner picks."

"Then the window. News runs earlier than breakfast.
Deadlines are meaner than bacon."

"That's a real theory," Cal said, and let it lie.

At five ten by his watch, no light changed anywhere,
and she had one full breath of pure triumph.
Then the vent fan came on down at the square,
a low far churn like a boat leaving,
and after it, up Millrow on the small wind,
came bacon and hot griddle steel,
arriving ahead of any light on principle.

"Buttermilk," Cal said, mild as the weather.

"The window never even got a chance."

"Comes on at five twenty-five. It's a good window.
It's just never once beaten breakfast."

She looked at him.

"You knew the whole order. You hustled me."

"I offered you a bet I've watched a few hundred mornings run.
There's a name for that."

"Hustled."

"Buttermilk," he agreed.

The laugh got out of her, loud in all that dark,
and a dog three yards over asked one question about it
and let it go.

"You can have the win back," he said. "Call it a house rule.
First one's free."

"Absolutely not. I pay my losses.
It's why I'm so much fun to bet against."

"I'll write that down," he said, and did not smile,
and she heard exactly where the smile would have gone anyway.

At five twenty-five the window over the old five-and-dime
came on, yellow and dutiful, right on his word,
and she toasted it with the enamel cup.

"Give me the rest," she said. "The whole order. I want it."

He settled back with his coffee on his knee, eyes up Millrow,
and gave it to her in order, unhurried,
a service he had clearly said to himself a thousand mornings
and never once out loud.

"Griddle, five ten. You had that.
Courier window, five twenty-five.
Streetlights let go at five forty, all together,
like they took a vote and it carried.
Verna's neon about a minute behind.
It hates going last and does it anyway.
Fountain's puts its two chairs down at quarter to six.
You can hear the legs hit the floor from the street.
At six the carillon runs her scale, one octave up, one down,
so the bells know they're still bells.
Six oh five, Tick signs on and lies about his thermometer.
Six ten, produce truck at the Checkerboard,
and the Liars' Table calls itself to order.
Six thirty, my trucks."
He turned the cup a quarter on his knee.
"Then it's day. That's the whole service."

She sat with the order of a town in her lap
and understood she had been handed something,
whatever that flat voice would have claimed about it.

"How many mornings did it take to learn all that?"

"Didn't set out to learn it. It just proves out.
A town tells you what it is at this hour.
Rest of the day it tells you what it wants you to think."

The streetlights went at five forty, all together, motion carried.
The neon down the cross street sulked out a minute behind,
and she laughed again, quieter,
because he'd had that right too, down to the sulk.

The sky came up the gray of an old skillet going warm,
and the first of it found his face,
and she gave that one moment of honest attention.
Weathered-golden was the phrase her mind produced,
a face the sun had been working on for years
with evident plans to finish,
the gray at his temples earning its keep in that light.
The flannel shirt he'd added against the hour
sat across his shoulders like it had won something,
and she returned her eyes to the square
on her own schedule and nobody else's.

At six the tower over the campus hill cleared its throat
and walked eight notes up and eight notes back down,
unhurried, testing.

"Carillon's flat on the fifth this morning," Cal said,
to the town at large.

"You can hear that?"

"She'll settle once the sun's on her."

At six oh five he clicked on a pocket radio
no bigger than a deck of cards,
and Tick Moran arrived on the porch at a mannerly volume.
"Sixty-six at the clock tower," Tick said,
"and don't hold me to it. My thermometer lies.
Swap-and-shop after the hog report."
Cal clicked it back off, ceremony observed.

The porch kept the last of the dark to itself after that.
She should have been tired.
She was the opposite of tired,
whatever that was called at six in the morning
with pit coffee in it.

"I was never here," she said. "For the record.
I protect your reputation."

"Appreciated. It's fragile."

She looked over at that, and he was already looking,
and the look went on one beat past
where either of them could have filed it as porch manners,
and neither of them moved to end it.
Diesel ended it for them,
the first crew truck taking the turn onto Millrow,
gravel and downshift, the day arriving in company.

She stood and set the enamel cup on the milk crate.

"Thank you for the coffee. And the show."

"Thursday's the best morning for it," he said.
"You got the full order."

Ray came out of the pit shed to meet the trucks,
rag over his shoulder at six thirty in the morning,
took in the porch, her on the steps, the two cups,
and raised two fingers without one word of commentary,
which at that hour, from Ray, was the discretion of a bank vault.

She walked home down the middle of Millrow
with the town coming on around her exactly on schedule,
smelling of hickory clean through,
past the Checkerboard with the Liars' Table already in session,
and every light she passed was a light she could vouch for now.

***

That night the house did its trick again, held still and hummed,
and she let it.
She rinsed her one glass and turned off the lamp,
and stood a minute in the kitchen with her phone in her hand.
Twelve thousand members had heard from her since supper,
about hotel blocks, and Thanksgiving flights,
and one lost retainer, found.
Not one of them heard about the porch.

Then she set the alarm for four forty,
on purpose, thumb sure about it,
a woman with a standing appointment nobody had made out loud,
and went to bed to sleep like a town
with every light in the right order.
