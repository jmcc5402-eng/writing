The house did its loudest thing at four in the morning,
which was nothing at all,
and Marisol lay in it for ten minutes
before she quit calling that sleep.

The parents' group never slept either; it changed shifts.
The four a.m. crowd was small and told the truth.
No parking questions at that hour, no bedding sizes,
just mothers awake in dark kitchens, two states from their reasons.
One of them had been typing and deleting long enough
that the app kept announcing her,
a woman clearing her throat for half an hour.
*He hasn't texted since Tuesday,* she finally sent.
*I know that's nothing. Somebody tell me it's nothing.*
Marisol answered from under her own quilt, thumbs certain.
*It's week six, my love. Week six is when the quiet starts,
and the quiet means you did it right.
Set a standing Sunday call and defend it like a casserole dish.
Mine resurfaced the day the laundry ran out. Yours will too.*
A heart came back, then nothing,
which at that hour meant a woman had gone to sleep,
which was the whole job.

That left Marisol wide awake in a house
with nobody in it to wake.
She put on shoes and let herself out her own front door
quiet as a curfew breaker,
with no destination she was prepared to name.

The morning had the first cool edge of the season in it,
thin, barely a fact, the kind you had to be out walking to find.
Four blocks along, the smoke found her.

Hickory. Faint, then not faint,
riding the cool air downhill through the dark,
and she came around onto Millrow and there stood the column,
climbing off Delmar's stack into the last of the stars,
the one thing in town already at work.
People claimed you could steer by that smoke.
She had filed that as a figure of speech
until she was standing in Delmar's gravel at a quarter to five,
having steered by it.

The cinderblock was dark. The pit shed behind glowed at every seam.
Across the lot one window burned yellow, Ray's kitchen,
somebody's day already decided.
And up on the screen porch, in the dark, a chair moved.

"Hook's off," Cal said, porch-quiet. "If you're coming up."

She hadn't decided she was until he said it.

The screen door gave on a little brass hook hanging open,
and there he was in the corner chair, the one facing up Millrow,
because of course it was,
boots crossed at the ankle,
a canvas jacket gone soft with wear over the green polo,
doing nothing at all to disguise the shoulders under it.
His eyes went once over her hair,
down out of its knot for the first time in their acquaintance,
and came back to the street without filing a report.

A steel thermos stood on the rail.
He unscrewed the lid, which was also the cup,
filled it, and held it out,
his hands doing all of it in the dark without one wasted motion,
and he did not ask her a single thing.

Not what she was doing on Millrow at this hour.
Not whether she was all right.
Not even the small polite questions people use for prying.
The coffee arrived warm in both her hands,
and that was the entire conversation he required.

She ran the arithmetic before she could vote against it.
The last time somebody had handed her something
she had not organized, requested, scheduled, or paid for,
she had still owned a husband, technically,
and even then she'd bought the thermos.
Months, then. Months, and honestly longer than months.
Across the lot Ray's yellow window went on being lit,
and her eyes stung once, briefly, over coffee out of a lid,
and she elected to blame the smoke.

"You're not going to ask," she said.

"Woman's out walking at this hour,
she's either got a reason or she's rid of one."
He topped off his own cup. "Either way it's hers to keep."

She took the chair beside his,
and the two of them faced up Millrow like it had a curtain time.
Up the street the House kept its own dark behind the oaks,
and neither of them handed it to the other.
The coffee was the whole treaty, and it held.

"Does Ray know you haunt his porch?" Marisol said.

"Ray leaves the hook off on purpose.
I bring the coffee. That's the arrangement,
and it's older than either of us being interesting."

"And you sit out here in the dark and do what, exactly?"

"Watch the town come on."

"Come on."

"Light by light." He said it plain, no apology in it.
"Same order every morning. You could set a watch by this town,
if anybody still set watches."

"No town is that regular."

"This one is."

"Prove it." The coffee had made her brave,
or four a.m. had, or the company.
"First light on the square. You pick, I pick.
Loser buys at the Checkerboard."

"Fair warning," Cal said. "I've sat this porch a lot of mornings."

"And I've run twelve thousand parents through move-in week.
I like my chances. The paper.
Newspaper people don't sleep, it's a documented fact."

"That's your pick."

"That's my pick."

"Then I've got the griddle," he said, settling back,
"and this is found money."

They drank the bet down in a friendly quiet.

"Where's your dog?" she said.

"Ratchet keeps shop hours.
She's at the gate right now, grading the early arrivals."

The town lay dark past the screen,
the square's one traffic light running its all-night blink,
and the pit breathed behind them, low and patient,
smoke walking past the porch in slow ropes.

At twenty to six, the Checkerboard went first.
The kitchen glow at the corner of the square doubled all at once,
and a beat later the exhaust fan over the alley
took up its all-day drone, faint from four blocks off
but not arguable.
The morning cook had lit the griddle.

"Well," she said, to her cup.

"For the record," Cal said, and his voice was gentle about it,
"the griddle's been first since Ray's daddy lit this pit.
I have never one time seen that window beat it.
I should have said so before money changed hands."

"You hustled me."

"I let a bet happen to you. That's different."

The laugh got out of her, too loud for the hour,
out across the gravel before she could dress it down,
and she did not chase it back.

"Buttermilk," he said. "Whenever. No hurry.
A debt like that keeps."

"Say the whole order," she said. "You obviously have it."

He looked up the dark street and gave it to her,
level and unhurried, a psalm learned by attendance.

"Griddle, five-forty.
Courier's upstairs window at ten till,
and whoever that is keeps better hours than the paper does.
Six sharp, the carillon tries itself.
Three notes, then the scale,
then it thinks better of showing off and quits.
Quarter past, Verna's office light.
She'll tell you she sleeps in. Don't you believe it.
Twenty-five past, the first crew trucks come up Millrow,
and after that it's just daylight and everybody in it."

She watched him say it instead of watching the street.
The pit light reached the porch at about the strength of a candle
and it was doing him favors,
gold along the brown of his face,
the gray at his temples gone warm,
and she thought, with some resentment,
that nobody had any business looking like that
at five-something in the morning, sober, in a work jacket.

"How long did it take to learn all that?"

"Didn't set out to learn it. It's a round, same as any other.
If a light doesn't come on, I want to know why.
Verna's ran late one morning three winters back,
and by six-twenty I'd invented a plumbing question
and called the desk."
He drank. "It has never been late twice."

"You keep watch on a whole town before it's even up."

"Somebody ought to know the order things wake in."
He tipped his cup at the street.
"Also the porch is free and the company can't talk back.
Present morning excepted."

The quiet after that had room in it,
and she went ahead and furnished it.

"You still have my wrench."

He drank his coffee.

"I said I'd bring you the right one."

"You've had it since the tent."

"I know what I've had since the tent."

He said it level, eyes on the street,
and then he let it sit there in the dark,
one full beat, and then another,
long past where either of them could file it under hardware.
She looked into her cup. The cup had nothing to offer.

"The right one takes finding," he said at last. "I'm thorough."

"Be thorough faster," Marisol said,
and drank,
and left the debt standing where he'd set it,
in the dark between the two chairs, warm.

At ten till, the Courier's upstairs window came on,
her whole losing bet, right on his schedule,
one lit square hanging over the dark old five-and-dime.
At six the tower up the hill tried itself,
three notes, the scale, quitting exactly where he'd said it would,
the sound drifting down Millrow through the smoke.

"Showoff," Cal said, to the hill.

At quarter past, a small light woke off past the square.

"Verna's," Marisol said, before he could,
absurdly pleased with herself about it.

"There you go," Cal said. "Porch does that to people."

Then it was the two of them and the last of the dark,
and she turned to say something she had not picked out yet
and found him already turned,
already looking,
and the look went on past what the hour could excuse,
and then went on a little past that,
and neither of them spent one word on it.
Headlights broke it, swinging white across the screen,
one diesel turning into the gravel with a second behind it,
the crew trucks arriving on his psalm to the minute.

Cal stood and put the thermos under his arm.
She handed the lid back and he took it by the rim,
unhurried about her fingers,
and screwed it down like the morning was now officially sealed.

Ray came around the pit shed wiping his hands on his apron,
built for the day already,
took in the porch, the two chairs, the hour,
and said none of what he was visibly concluding.

"Pit's running sweet," Ray said. "You want a plate at eleven,
Sutter, get your order in before the phones do."

"Chopped. Extra pickle."

"I know your order. It was a courtesy."

Down in the gravel the trucks sat idling,
his day come to collect him, patient about it.
Marisol stood.

"Thank you for the coffee," she said. "And the education."

"Five-forty, the griddle," Cal said. "Don't let anybody hustle you."

She went down the steps into a morning that smelled
like hickory and wet grass,
crossed the gravel past the idling trucks and their nods,
and walked home with the smoke at her back,
the column standing over Millrow, tall and early,
the town's one flag already up,
and she steered by it clean to her own street.

***

The day went about its usual business.
The group surged at eight and again at five,
somebody's freshman had mono, somebody's freshman had joined
four clubs,
and both mothers got walked to the same conclusion,
which was that Thanksgiving was coming either way.
The four a.m. mother posted at supper
that a standing Sunday call was now on two calendars,
and Marisol pinned nothing and felt like a millionaire.

She was in bed by ten, which was unheard of.
Before the lamp went off she picked the phone back up,
not for the group,
and set the alarm. Four-fifteen.
Nobody's emergency, nobody's carpool, nobody's flight.
She put the phone face-down on the nightstand.

Four-fifteen was a ridiculous hour to want something.
She turned off the lamp and wanted it anyway.
