The first cool front of the fall came through Ashford in the dark,
and Marisol was awake for every minute of it.

The house had its overnight quiet on, the deep kind,
two doors open down the hall on two made beds,
the refrigerator thinking its one long thought.
Sometime after midnight the summer had lost its grip;
she could feel the change standing at the kitchen window at four,
a thin new edge coming in under the screen,
the first air in months that asked to be worn instead of survived.
She was not going to lie in bed while that happened.
Real shoes. The cardigan that had waited all of August in the closet
for exactly this morning.
She went out the front door with the phone in her pocket, face-down
even in there, and for once even the parents' group was asleep.

Four a.m. was a different town.
The streetlights had it to themselves.
Dew sat gray on every windshield on the block,
the cut-grass smell had gone cold and sweet overnight,
and the cicadas had finally handed the racket
to one competent cricket.
She walked with no route, which was becoming her favorite lie,
because her feet took the downhill turn without consulting her
and gave her Millrow, long and empty,
the square dark at the bottom, the campus dark at the top,
and standing over the middle of it all, pale against the stars,
a column of smoke tall enough to steer by.

Hickory. Two blocks off and it found her anyway,
smoke and black pepper riding the new cool,
and she followed it on the grounds
that smoke that good at that hour was a civic matter.

Delmar's sat dark under its sign, the pit out back
sending its smoke up patient and well fed,
and in the gravel by the porch stood one truck she recognized
faster than she would have admitted under oath.
On the screen porch, a tail went thump, thump against the boards.

"Porch is unlocked," Cal said, out of the dark, not loud,
as if that settled everything, which, at that hour, it did.

She came up the steps into the smell of a thousand old lunches.
Ratchet lay stretched across the boards like dropped luggage
and rolled enough to offer her belly without getting up,
and Marisol paid the toll, both hands, thoroughly.
Cal sat tipped back near the rail, boots crossed,
a canvas jacket gone soft at the seams over the green polo,
his shoulders taking up their usual unreasonable share of the chair.

He reached over, filled the thermos cup,
and set it on the rail beside the second chair.
He did it before she had said one word,
and he did not ask one question.
Not what she was doing out at twenty to five,
not whether she wanted it, not what she took in it.
He just poured, hands sure of the job in the dark,
and went back to his lean.

She looked at that cup one second longer than coffee needs.
She could not remember the last time anybody had handed her a thing
she had not arranged, scheduled, posted, or paid the nines on.
Months. More months than that.
Down the block one kitchen window came on, yellow,
somebody's morning hung up small and square in all that dark,
and she let the whole thing pass through her and out the screen,
and sat down, and drank the coffee.

It was good coffee. That was somehow the last straw.

"Whose porch is this at this hour," she said. "Legally."

"Ray leaves it unlocked for me. Been that arrangement a long time."

"And Ray gets what out of it?"

"Somebody on his porch smelling his smoke at five a.m.
Ray'd sell tickets to that if he could figure the seating."

She laughed, quick and quiet, mindful of the hour.
The pit out back settled a log with a soft collapse
and sent up a fresh column, sweet on the new cool,
and the dark down Millrow held perfectly still around it.

"Before you ask," she said, "the notebook's at home."

"Then we'll get along fine."

They got along fine.
The quiet that came was easy in a manner she had not budgeted for,
two people and a dog and a town not awake yet,
steam standing off the coffee in the cold,
and November could have the daylight.
The dark before five belonged to nobody at all.

"For the record," she said, into that quiet, not loud,
"you have had my wrench all season."

"I know it."

"You said you'd bring me the right one.
I remember it word for word."

"So do I," Cal said, and that was all he said,
and she found she'd rather be owed than paid,
and drank her coffee, and declined to examine that.

He was watching the dark down toward the square,
not idly, she realized. Watching for something.

"What are you looking at?"

"Town's fixing to wake up," he said. "It goes in order."

"What order?"

"Same one. Every morning the Lord sends."

She sat forward and put her elbows on the rail.
"Bet me. First light on the square.
I say the newspaper window — the Courier, upstairs,
over the old five-and-dime."

"You'd lose."

"Then it's easy money. Stakes?"

He considered it for the length of a sip.
"Loser pours."

"Poured. Past tense. You already poured."

"Next time," he said, to the street,
plain as a man reading a gauge,
and she made a careful study of the dark down Millrow
and let next time go by unremarked.

They waited.
The cool kept coming in through the screens,
enough that she was glad of the cardigan and said nothing about it,
and the sky over the square went from black to a doubtful charcoal.
First light got to his face before it got to the street,
which was unfair.
Gray at the temples, jaw a day past its shave,
a man completely at rest for once, hands still, in no hurry,
weathered the warm way, like porch wood that had been minded.
He looked good in the dark. That was the finding,
and she was a thorough woman, and she took her time filing it.
He glanced over and caught the study midstream,
and she held it, one beat, then another,
past every place either of them could have called it an accident.
Up the hill the tower cleared its throat,
three notes into the dark and a fourth it swallowed,
and the beat broke, and they both looked at the street
and let it be about the street.

"It does that at five?" she said.

"Carillon runs its test before there's anybody awake to blame it.
You only hear it on a still morning."

"It's a still morning."

"It is," he said, and did not look over, and neither did she.

Down on the square, a light came on.

Not the newspaper window.
A low blue-white strip woke up behind the Checkerboard's glass,
back of the counter, the one over the flat-top.

"That's the griddle," Cal said. There was no gloat in it.
It was closer to a man confirming sunrise.

"You knew."

"I knew. Phams' griddle opens this town every morning of the world."

"You let me bet knowing."

"You picked the window. It was the better pick."
He tipped his cup toward the square.
"Print night, the window takes it, works till dawn.
Rest of the week, the griddle's got the town."

"So I lost on scheduling."

"You lost on Thursday."

The laugh got away from her, too loud for the hour,
and Ratchet's tail endorsed it twice against the boards.
She put her hand flat on the rail and got herself back.

"Give me the rest of it," she said. "The whole order."

He was quiet a second, and then he gave it to her, unhurried,
eyes on the street, reciting something
he had never once had to memorize.
"Griddle first. Then Verna's office light at the Magnolia Court —
she'll tell you she doesn't sleep, and the light backs her up.
Courier window, that's your paper, upstairs.
Carillon test. Fountain's turns its sign when Odell gets there.
Tick signs 1340 on and plays the anthem to nobody.
Then my trucks come up Millrow at six,
and after that it's daytime,
and everybody gets to believe the town starts itself."

She listened to the whole order of service with her cup forgotten
halfway up. The square was graying in as he said it,
lights arriving on schedule under his voice
like the town was reading along over his shoulder.

"You know the whole liturgy," she said.

"A lot of five a.m.s."

"And what has you out here for all of them?"

"Coffee's better here," Cal said,
which was an answer to a different question,
and she let him keep the real one.
Porch rules, she decided, ran in both directions.

The gray came up enough to put shapes to things,
the smoke going from pale to gold at its top edge,
dew starting to burn off the truck hood in thin curls of steam,
first birds conferring in the oaks behind the lot.
Then, far off, gravel. Headlights swung onto Millrow, a pair,
then a second pair behind it, high and slow.

"That's six," he said.

"That's my cue." She stood and set the empty cup on the rail,
squared it there the same as she'd found it.
Ratchet got up too and escorted her the whole four feet
to the top of the steps, supervising the departure,
and got the smile Marisol had going spare.

"You don't have to run off," Cal said.

"I'm not running.
I've got twelve thousand people about to wake up needing things,
and you've got a porch about to fill up with witnesses."
She went down the steps into air that smelled of dew and hickory
and the first cold morning of the year.
At the bottom she turned.
"Thank you for the coffee."

"You lost the bet," he said. "Next one's yours to pour."

"Then I know my stakes," she said, to the street, and took it home,
downhill into the waking town,
past the Checkerboard already warm behind its glass,
while the crew trucks rumbled up the other way,
and the first one dipped its headlights at her, polite,
and the smoke stood over all of it like a flag run up early.

***

That night the group wanted what it always wanted,
parking and bedding sizes and whether anyone else's freshman
had gone completely silent, asking for a friend,
and she gave them an hour and the pinned post and her whole heart,
same as ever.

Then she washed one plate, one fork, one glass,
turned off the kitchen light,
and stood a minute at the window with the phone in her hand.
The cool had held all day.
It was going to be another still morning.

She set the alarm for four forty. On purpose.
Then she put the phone down gently, face-down, done for the night,
a woman with a debt to pay
at an establishment that didn't open for hours.
She paid hers promptly.
It was one of the several differences between them,
and come morning, over a cup he hadn't poured,
she intended to say so.
