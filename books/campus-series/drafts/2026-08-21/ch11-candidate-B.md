Marisol came down the bar of the Blind Mule hand to hand,
passed along like a bucket at a fire,
laughing too hard to apologize to anyone she landed on.

She had stood up at the final whistle to hug Earlene.
The town had done the rest.
A hand at her elbow, a hand at her shoulder blade,
somebody's grandmother steering her by the hip
with a grip that had put up forty years of tomatoes,
and the far end of the room chanting the score over and over,
in case the number changed when they quit.

Ashford had beaten a ranked team.
At noon, nobody in the Mule could have told you
the rankings mattered.
By eight o'clock they had mattered since the founding of the county.

The Blind Mule was built to hold forty people
and was holding a town.
The wood of that bar had been drinking spilled beer
longer than anyone leaning on it had been alive,
and tonight it was earning interest.
The TV in the corner, dark all week on principle,
was up loud enough to argue with,
and the replay ran on a loop nobody watched,
because everybody in the room had already been the replay.
Somebody's dentist stood on a chair, conducting.
Out front the drumline had found the bar,
half of it still in uniform, all of it still going,
and every time the door swung, the cadence poured in
and the room picked its feet up without voting on it.

Marisol fetched up at the rail between Earlene Tatum
and a man she was nearly sure had reshingled her porch in June,
and Peanut Kyzer set a beer in front of her
that she had not ordered, in a glass with actual sides.

"Last true glass in the building," Peanut said.
"Guard it like a title."

"What happens when it's empty?"

"History," said Peanut, and climbed onto the beer cooler
to address the republic.
"Listen up! We are out of glasses!
Not out of beer. Out of glasses.
Never once in all my years behind this bar.
Y'all did this, and I have never been prouder of anybody.
Improvise!"

The Mule improvised.

Marisol had Ray on the phone before Peanut was off the cooler,
and Ray answered the way Delmar's answered on a win night,
which was all smoker and no hello.

"Ray. Peanut's out of glasses."

"Out of glasses." A silence of real respect. "At the Mule."

"How many mason jars can you spare?"

"Tonight? All of them.
Tell Sonny to bring the truck around back.
And tell Peanut this squares us for the ice storm."

"Which ice storm?"

"He'll know."

She had Sonny Dillard dispatched inside the minute,
out the door with two volunteers into the cadence,
and the drumline, reading the mission exactly wrong
and exactly right, escorted the truck both ways.

The jars came in overhead to a standing ovation.

"Jars to the bar, mugs to the tables," Marisol called,
already stacking, "and nobody touches Peanut's last glass."

The Checkerboard's night cook sent over a rack of coffee mugs
with a ticket taped on top that said WE'RE STILL OPEN.
Peanut hauled the seltzer cooler around to the customer side,
declared it an honor system,
and the honor system filled a pickle jar with folded fives.
Somebody was building espresso martinis in two of the mugs
for two retired schoolteachers who paid in exact change.
Somebody's grandmother toasted the room from a mug
that said WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN
and would not say where she got it.

"You're the one solved the glasses," the grandmother told her,
"and that dress is doing its share for the evening too."

"Ray solved it. I just know his number," Marisol said,
and topped off the woman's mug from the pitcher,
which settled both compliments at once.

Birdie Pham surrendered the flower vase.

She did it formally, at the bar, zinnias evicted,
handing it across to Peanut
with the gravity of a treaty being signed.
Eli Fountain stood beside her holding the zinnias
against his chest, stem water and all,
making no attempt to look like a man who minded.

"It comes back," Birdie said.

"It comes back," Peanut swore, and filled it to the lip.

"You can set those down, Eli," Birdie said.

"I'm fine," said Eli, and held the flowers.

"That vase has sat on this bar longer than most marriages
in this room," Earlene said at Marisol's shoulder.
"Tonight it's drinking. That's not a comedown.
That's a promotion in wartime."

Cal Sutter was at the far end of the room with his crew.

She had not been looking for him.
She had been counting mugs, and the count ran him over.
Off duty, in a shirt washed soft and open at the collar,
doing quiet favors for his shoulders,
and he stood easy in all that noise, arms crossed,
while one of his men retold the fourth quarter with both arms.
The TV light kept finding the gray at his temples
and improving on it.
He looked like the last hour of good daylight in there,
and half the mothers at the dartboard end had noticed,
and were being gracious about it.
She went back to counting mugs and lost the count.

Her phone buzzed against her hip,
and it was her boy, from twenty minutes
and one locker room away.

*MOM. TELL ME YOU WATCHED*

A second one landed before her thumb found a word.

*sideline was the best seat in the state. ears still ringing*

She stood in the loudest room in the county
and let her eyes go hot for exactly one second,
the same budget she'd given them at the Checkerboard in August.
Across town a quiet house held an empty chair with his name on it,
and here was the trade, buzzing in her hand,
happier than she had ever heard him in writing.

*Watched it with the whole town,* she typed back. *Listen.*
She held the phone up into the roar for ten entire seconds,
which was the best use anybody found for a phone all night.

The next buzz wasn't him.

It was the group on Grapevine, twice,
and moderating twelve thousand people had left her thumb
with instincts of its own.
The first was a repost making the rounds,
an account with a sunflower avatar and nobody behind it,
Concerned4Ashford, three weeks old and fluent since birth.

*Our grandmothers said it best, ya'll — you can't save a barn
by loving it. Some things in this town are past sentiment.
Time to be honest about the House. Balance, neighbors.*

AshfordSafetyMom had agreed with it in all capitals
within four minutes of its posting.

She turned the screen to Earlene,
because Earlene was six inches away
and because the post had a smell on it.

Earlene read it at arm's length, squinting.

"I said that two mayors ago," Earlene said. "And not like that."

"You said it about the barn?"

"I said it about an actual barn.
It had a roof you could read the weather through.
It came down the following March, right on schedule."

"And now you're wisdom."

"I'm always wisdom. That's not the part that stings."
Earlene handed the phone back.
"The part that stings is the manners."

Her thumb did the screenshots without consulting her,
one, two, timestamped, filed
in the folder that went out with the Sunday coffee.

The second message was the politest one yet.
A name she'd never seen, a profile a week old,
every sentence wearing its church clothes.

*So many of us admire what you've built. Which is why,
on the House question, a voice like yours might serve
the town best by stepping back. Balance matters.
Just thinking of you.*

Screenshot. Filed. Phone face-down on the rail.
Trustees didn't read Grapevine,
which meant all that politeness wasn't aimed at the town at all;
it was aimed at her.
She picked up the last true glass in the building
and went back to the noise on purpose.

The conviction of Tunk Ferrell began at the dartboard end
around ten and reached the rail in under a minute,
traveling faster than the touchdown had.

"Tunk Ferrell," the dentist announced from his chair,
still conducting.
"His outfit's the one bid on those parking decks.
Public record. You want to know who's been carrying
Boyd's water all fall, follow the bid."

"There it is," said the dartboard end, largely in unison.

"Hold on now." Sonny Dillard set down the crate he was stacking.
"Tunk ran his crew at cost the week the hospital chiller quit,
worst week of August. I was there.
Man slept in the parking lot with his gauges."

"That was air conditioning," the dentist said. "This is football."

"It isn't football, it's the town," somebody called,
and got a cheer without anybody agreeing what it meant.

"He's had his eye on that deck money since spring," Earlene said,
to her mug. "That's not news. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic's not a crime," Sonny said.

"Didn't say crime. Said arithmetic."

"Has anybody asked him?" Marisol said.

The rail considered that.

"Asked him what?" said Peanut.

"Anything."

"Where's the fun in that," the dentist said, and got his cheer,
and the dartboard end went back to convicting,
happy as a jury on a field trip.

"House rule," Peanut said, over all of it.
"Nobody gets convicted in this bar
without a drink standing for the defense."
He poured a tall one into the best remaining mug
and set it on the shelf under the TV
where the Mule kept its trophies.
"For Tunk Ferrell. In absentia. Convicted and beloved."

Half the bar drank to the man
the other half had just found guilty,
and both halves used the same jars,
and nobody on either side held one piece of evidence
beyond a bid any man with a crew and a license
would have made in his sleep.

Marisol got the spiral notebook out at the rail,
ninety-nine cents of college rule, soft at the corners now,
the House's people filling it a Saturday at a time.
She turned past Ray and Earlene and Verna
to the first blank line and wrote *Tunk Ferrell,*
and then, because the room was sure and she wasn't,
she put a question mark after his name.
The question mark took longer than the name did.

"What did you write?" Earlene asked, not looking.

"A question mark."

"Generous," Earlene said.
"The dartboard gave him twenty years."

She left a little before midnight,
when the drumline had finally marched itself home down Millrow
and the score had settled into the walls to live.

At the door she turned back, for no reason she filed anywhere,
and Cal Sutter was already looking.

Not caught looking. Looking.
The whole loud length of the room stood between them,
jars going up, mugs coming down, the vase making another lap,
and none of it got in the way at all.
She held it.
One beat, then the beat past it,
past every place a look can still be called an accident,
and she did not smile,
and she did not look away either,
and neither did he.

"VASE COMING THROUGH," Peanut bellowed,
and Birdie's vase went overhead down the bar, hand to hand,
full again and riding the room like a parade float,
and when it had passed, the crew had folded him back in.

It was from the doorway, night air on her back,
that she finally noticed the jukebox.
Somebody had unplugged it, cord looped neat over the top,
and nobody could say when,
except that the Mule had been singing everything itself
since at least the sympathy round,
which put the jukebox dark at better than an hour,
and not one soul had missed it.

She stepped out into a street full of car horns and porch lights.
The notebook rode in her tote with one question mark in it,
the phone in her pocket held two screenshots
and a boy whose ears were still ringing,
and behind her the town sang the score to the empty square,
in case the county hadn't heard it yet.
