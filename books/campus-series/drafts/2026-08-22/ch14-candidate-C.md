Boilers went on the last Monday of September.
Cal had told the North dorms so on Friday,
and told them what blankets were for,
and now it was Monday and he was as good as his word,
which nobody up in those dorms had ever doubted
or thanked him for either.

He ran boiler day by ear, oldest plant first, worst actor early.
The North basement main had sat shut since April
and had seized where it sat,
and he set his feet, took the wheel in one hand,
and broke it loose in a single pull,
the whole fight traveling up his forearm and ending there,
nothing about it ever reaching his face.

His second looked up from the clipboard.

"We own a cheater bar," she said.

"Bar's in the truck."

"Truck's thirty feet away."

"Wasn't a thirty-foot problem."

She shook her head once, in the direction of his shoulders,
at whatever it was men his age
were supposed to have quit being able to do,
and wrote the time down.

He lit the boiler and stood with two fingers on the supply line
while it kettled and banged and settled into its working hum.

"There you are," he told it, and the hum held.

Upstairs the radiators started knocking, floor by floor,
heat climbing toward four floors of other people's kids
and half a country's worth of mothers
who checked the weather in this town before their own.

By nine the hill had heat, steam standing off every roof vent,
and Cal came down to the motor pool through steam he'd made himself.
The shop was running its Monday sound,
grinder and impact wrench and WDSS underneath everything,
and he could have taken attendance from the gate with his eyes shut.

The campus mail got there at a quarter past ten.

One manila envelope, thick,
his name and his title showing in the window,
the engineer of record's return stamp in the corner.
Cal took it into the office and shut the door,
which he did so rarely the hinge had an opinion about it.

The pages inside said DRAFT, corner to corner, gray, every page.
After that they said what the ground rules
had promised all along they would say.
Stair rise. Rail height. Wire gauge. Footing depth.
Hartwell House, graded line by line against the code
for a house going up this year, pass or fail,
and no line anywhere on the form for a hundred years of standing.

FINDINGS took one page.
RECOMMEND CONDEMNATION took two words of it.

Under that came the signature block, already typed.
CAL SUTTER, DIRECTOR OF FACILITIES,
with a clean line waiting above the name
and a date line waiting beside it.
Somebody in an office he had never visited
had reached his verdict for him,
spelled his name right doing it,
and left him nothing in the whole document but the initialing.

The cover memo ran one paragraph.
Signed and returned by Friday, close of business,
for inclusion in the trustees' November packet.

He wanted a month.
He wanted a month of that house with his own flashlight,
top plate to footing, the code book open on the newel post,
finding out line by line what was true, what was fixable,
and what was only old.
The memo gave him four days.

Ratchet left her parking spot
and lay down across the office threshold,
which she did not do for weather.

He read the packet through again. It said the same thing slower.

Behind the bench seat of his truck there was a folder
with nothing written on the tab,
photographs and dates, nineteen years of a wall not moving,
paid for in cash and printed off the campus books entirely.
That folder was one thing and this envelope was another,
and no version of this Monday ended with the two of them touching.

---

The letter hit the crew corkboard before eleven.

It had come up the hill in the same mail run, ten copies,
addressed to the shop, to every shop,
and somebody pinned one by the time clock
between the safety poster and the burn-ban notice.
The letterhead had the new north end drawn across the top in gray,
decks and glass and the long lawn running for the gates.

Ronnie came out of bay three wiping his hands
and read it with his thumb riding under the lines.

"No winter reductions," he read. "New equipment.
Roofs on the row barns." He looked around the shop.
"Roofs on the row barns. In writing, boys."

"It says pending," Cal's second said. "Trustees in November."

"Rain says pending. I'll take it."

The crew came off their machines a few at a time to read it,
and the reading put spring in the whole floor,
and the grinder came back on sounding better about everything.

"You know about this?" Ronnie asked him.

"I read the paper," Cal said,
and Ronnie nodded like that settled it, because from Cal it did.

The letter was dated that Monday morning.
So was the memo on his desk.
He stood at the corkboard while the crew talked around him
and did the arithmetic on the two of them
landing an hour apart, one to his office and one to his crew,
and came out with the answer he had walked in with.
Then he went and found work for his hands,
and the morning gave him plenty.

---

The swap-and-shop hour opened at noon with a pontoon boat.

"Twenty-two-foot pontoon," Tick Moran read, over the shop radio,
in the voice he kept for legal notices.
"Two motors, one of which runs.
Seller is asking twelve hundred, firm."

By half past, firm had loosened to or-best-offer,
and the shop had suspended all other business.

"Floor's soft," Ronnie said. "You can hear it in the man's voice.
That's a forty-dollar boat."

"Forty," said Cal's second. "The rails alone are worth forty."

"Then he can sell me the rails and keep the boat."

"It's got two motors," his second said.

"It's got two anchors," Ronnie said,
"and one of them came with a steering wheel."

Tick came back at one o'clock
in the tone of a man renegotiating with the open air.
"Folks. In this same hour I have moved a stock trailer,
a set of box springs, and a prom dress, worn once.
The pontoon remains available.
I'm told the price is now flexible,
and I'd describe the seller's afternoon the same way.
He says she can be seen at his dock.
I'd go at low water."

The hour ended with the boat unsold at any figure,
and the shop's bid holding firm at forty, on principle,
though not one man in the building wanted a boat,
and no one ever picked up a phone to offer it.

"My cousin in Opelika had a pontoon," Ronnie said.

Nobody had anything for that. The impact wrench started back up.

Cal heard the whole hour from the parts counter,
doing inventory that could have waited,
and laughed once, out loud, at the low-water line,
in spite of everything on his desk.

---

Mid-afternoon he took his coffee out to the fence line,
where the gravel ran out and the pasture took over,
and stood at the fence a booster had once admired.
It was still a great fence.

He had the phone out and her name on the screen
with no memory at all of the steps in between.

MARISOL, in the same plain letters the phone gave everybody,
which seemed insufficient, and there she was.

His thumb rested on her name.

He knew exactly how she'd take it. That was the trouble.
Chin first. The chin would come up
before he got to the end of the first sentence,
she'd be on her feet by the second,
and somewhere around midnight
the county would start hearing from her.

She carried things.
It was the best thing he knew about her,
and it was the reason his thumb stayed where it was.
Hand her an unsigned verdict, twelve paychecks,
and a Friday deadline,
and she would pick all of it up and carry it
on top of twelve thousand other people's Tuesdays,
and he had not decided one thing yet
that was worth loading her with.

Then the gravel started in on the back road,
and Ratchet gave two thumps from her stencil,
and by the time Ray's truck cleared the gate
the screen had gone dark in his hand.

Ray came around the tailgate with a to-go box
and put it in Cal's hands without ceremony.

"Didn't order," Cal said.

"Didn't ask." Ray nodded at the box.
"Monday after a fifth Sunday, nobody in this town eats right.
I'm running plates. You're the far end of the loop,
so it rode up front with me."

The lid had marker across it, big enough to read from the truck.
MULE, THURSDAY — WE'RE CELEBRATING NOTHING, COME ANYWAY.

"What's Thursday?" Cal said.

"Nothing. You're reading it.
Town's been arguing two months straight,
and Peanut wants one night
where nobody's for or against a single thing.
No speeches, no clipboard, no whereas.
First round's full price, and I'm catering at cost,
which is a sight you'll want to say you saw."

"Dog invited?"

"Dog's the guest of honor.
It's a nothing party, Sutter. She's exactly qualified."
Ray pulled his door shut and hung an elbow out the window.
"Bring the crew. Bring the whole hill.
Bring that parents' army if you can stand the company."

"I'll bring the dog."

He was back in the cab before Cal could reach for his wallet.

"What do I owe you?"

"Thursday," Ray said, and took the loop back down the hill.

The box was warm through both palms,
and the town had been arriving all day, was the thing.
The heat. The letter. The hour of boat. The box.
It kept arriving, and it did not know one word
of what was sitting on his desk with his name under it.

---

The crew knocked off at five thirty,
and the shop shut down in stages,
impact wrench first, then the grinder,
then the compressor's last cycle,
then the radio, which his second silenced on her way past the bench.

She stopped at the door with her keys.

"That letter," she said. "You believe it?"

"Paper's real," Cal said. "November decides the rest."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know it," Cal said, and she let him keep it, and went home.

He fed Ratchet at six.
She ate, and then lay back down in the office doorway
instead of her spot, chin on the threshold,
keeping whatever it was she was keeping.

He sat at the desk and read the draft once more under the lamp.
It had not improved.
The findings read the same, the two words read the same,
and his name sat under them, typed by somebody sure of him.

The bottom right drawer of the desk had a lock,
and the key to it had lain in the pencil tray for twenty years,
because in twenty years the shop had never held
one piece of paper worth locking.

He squared the pages, laid the memo on top,
set the whole of it in the drawer face down,
and turned the key.

Then he got the phone out on purpose this time.

Her name came up the same as it had at the fence,
plain letters, insufficient,
and he sat in the lamplight with his thumb above it
and let himself have the whole minute.

If he called, he'd start with the wall,
because the wall was good news,
eleven little panes of glass and not a crack among them,
and she'd laugh.
He could see the laugh, all of her face going into it,
same as on the porch in the roses,
right up until she heard the rest.
The ground rules. The two words. The Friday line.
The twelve names that never get typed so long as his does.

Half of it was hers by rights already.
The House had her Saturdays in it now,
her name on the second page of Ray's book,
her coffee can of zinnias on the porch rail.
He had no idea how to hand a woman half of a thing,
and less idea how to sit across a folding table from her again
holding the other half back.

He set the phone down on the desk, face up, her name still on it,
and watched the screen time out and go dark.

The key went into his shirt pocket, next to the House key.
Two keys now that didn't get explained to anybody.

He cut the office lamp, then the bays, east to west,
and stood in the yard while the yard light buzzed on
and the last of boiler day's steam stood off the hill in the dark.
Ratchet walked him as far as her stencil
and curled down on her name.

Up the hill the carillon counted eight o'clock down Millrow.

The drawer was locked. The shop was dark.
