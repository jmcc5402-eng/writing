The Zinnia Wagon quit in the middle of its own getaway.

Half loaded, tailgate down, nose already pointed home,
and Birdie Pham turned the key of the white flower truck —
the Zinnia Wagon, the whole county called it —
and got one short cough for her trouble.
The second try was shorter.
The third wasn't anything at all.
The truck had said what it had to say.

"Don't you dare," Birdie told the dashboard.
"You have flowers aboard."

Marisol heard the whole business from two stalls down,
where she was boxing the market's unsold squash for the pantry van,
a job she'd invented her second week in this town
and had somehow been in charge of ever since.
By the time the box was handed up and the van doors shut,
the truck had drawn men the way raised hoods draw them,
four deep at the fender, arms crossed, contributing.

Sonny Dillard came around from his own tailgate,
listened to one more crank with his head tipped,
and called it from six feet out.

"Fuel pump. She's not starving, she's cut off entire.
Crank on it till frost if you want. It'll cost you a battery too."

"And where does a person find a fuel pump for this truck?"
Birdie said.

"Pump's the cheap part. The finding is the sport."

The Shed Thread had the situation posted before the hood was propped,
and the situation had answers before Birdie had a plan.
Somebody knew a man two counties over.
Somebody's brother had parted out the same truck years back
and thought the pump might still be in a coffee can.
And somebody, evidently, had gone straight up the hill and knocked,
because twenty minutes later a diesel came down Millrow
in no hurry at all,
and it was the stadium's turf tractor, mowing deck off,
a tow strap coiled on the fender,
Eli Fountain up in the seat in his Saturday clothes,
which were his weekday clothes with the hat off.

"No," Birdie said, to the whole street.

"It's a tractor," Eli said. "Tractors tow."

"That is the stadium's tractor."

"It's my Saturday." He was already down and walking the strap out.
"And she needed the miles."

He hooked on under the frame like he'd built the frame,
took the slack out slow,
and stood up alongside the running board.

"Ride here," he said. "One hand on the wheel through the window.
We'll take the square at parade speed."

"What's parade speed?"

"Slow enough to wave."

He looked out across the bricks toward the fountain once,
reading them.
"Square's crowned. Inch and a half,
fountain down to the storm drains, for the rain.
You won't see it. You'll feel it in the boards on the turn."

"Then stand me right," Birdie said.

Eli moved her half a step along the running board
and set her free hand on the mirror bracket,
like the half step mattered, because to him it did,
and she stood where he set her.

They came across the square at exactly parade speed.

Every radio at the market was already on the swap-shop hour,
and Tick Moran left a caller's gooseneck trailer hanging mid-sentence.

"Folks, if you are anywhere near the square, find a window.
The flower truck is coming through behind the stadium tractor
at two miles an hour,
and I want the record to show this station
called it a parade before anybody else did."

Chairs turned on the Checkerboard's sidewalk.
Somebody propped the Mule's door to see better.
Marisol stepped off the curb at the crossing and put one hand up,
and four trucks sat and waited on a parade with one float in it.

Birdie rode the running board with one hand on the wheel
and gave the square the other,
a slow, wrist-deep wave off the top of a float,
homecoming-queen work, side to side, no hurry anywhere in it,
and the sidewalks gave it back with both hands.

"Hope they cleared it with the parade committee," Sonny said.

Tick took two more calls about the gooseneck
and came back to the window both times.
"Still waving, folks. We'll update you as the story develops."

At the far corner Eli eased the whole procession
around the turn on the high side of the crown,
and if the running board moved under her,
Birdie never grabbed for anything,
because she was standing where he'd set her.
The tractor took the grade up toward the shops,
the strap held, the wave went on to the last row of watchers,
and Tick signed the story off the only way it deserved.
"That's the news. Somebody buy that gooseneck."

Marisol was two pitchers into the tea line
when the fifth Sunday hit its stride.

Mt. Nebo did the fifth-Sunday dinner for four congregations at once,
folding tables run end to end under the oaks,
tablecloths clipped down against the breeze,
and enough covered dishes that the tables quit being furniture
and became geography.
The afternoon came down through the oaks in pieces
and moved across all of it.
The same toddler was retrieved from under the dessert table twice.

She poured tea until the pitchers ran dry,
got thanked by name by people she had met exactly once,
and when she finally sat down with her own plate,
the seat that was open was the seat
across the corner from Cal Sutter.

He had on a pressed shirt with a collar, Sunday-issue,
sleeves rolled to the forearm anyway,
and the shoulders under it were doing the shirt a favor
the whole yard had likely noticed
and gone on being Christian about.

They had said their good afternoons like two people
who had never once argued on a porch,
and since then they had been looking at the food, the oaks,
the tea, and the middle distance, in turns, diligently.

"I hear the square had a parade Saturday," Cal said, to his plate.

"A float and a queen," Marisol said, to the oaks.
"You'd have hated it. People enjoyed themselves in public."

"Heard it set a record."

"Slowest in county history. Tick's having a plaque made."

The corner of his mouth did the thing it did,
briefly, and got put away.

Down the table, Odell had been watching Eli Fountain
all through the chicken,
and somewhere between the chicken and the cake
he set his fork down and went ahead.

"Fountain. That tractor of yours made the radio."

"It did," Eli said.

"Towed her the length of the square at a walking pace,
whole county watching,
her waving off the running board like the fair came early."
Odell let it sit there a second.
"Son, what exactly are you waiting on?"

The men's end of the table went quiet enough
to hear the ice shift in the tea.

"The truck needed a fuel pump," Eli said.

It was the worst answer ever given to any question
asked out loud in that county,
and every man at the table received it like scripture.
Odell nodded, slow, a judge accepting a plea he admired.
Somebody's grandfather said "fuel pump" to himself, twice,
putting it away for later use.
Down at the cake table, Birdie studied the frosting
with her whole face.

Then the sheet cake came down the line.

Whoever made it had not been playing.
A full sheet, white buttercream,
cut in squares the size of hymnals,
each square on its own paper plate,
and the plates traveled hand to hand down the tables,
because that was how everything traveled here.
Cal took each one as it reached him
and handed it across the corner, eyes out on the oaks,
and Marisol took each one and sent it on down her side.

The last plate was hers.

He handed it across the same as all the others,
and she reached the same as all the others,
and her fingers came down over his under the plate.
Skin on skin, the two of them holding one paper plate
of sheet cake in the middle of a churchyard.
Nobody's hand moved for half a second longer
than the plate needed.

Then the plate was hers and his hand was back at his tea,
and he was asking Odell whether the pudding table
needed relieving,
and she was saying thank you in a voice one size too polite,
and the two of them went back to looking
elaborately elsewhere.

Her pulse came up like she'd taken stairs.
The frosting was too sweet and she ate every bit of it.

He said less than usual after that, even for him,
and she put it down to Sunday.

Two seats down sat a mother Marisol knew off the group,
her boy's first fall away,
and beside her a folding chair had gotten left empty
in a yard where empty chairs did not happen.
Nobody said a word about it, and neither did she,
and she laughed at the fuel pump with everybody else,
her thumb resting on her phone under the table.
Marisol sent the first square of cake her way without asking.

The talk under the oaks had gone where all talk went now.
The House. The trustees.
Petitions, and who ought to call a meeting,
and which hall in town was big enough to hold one.

Delores Fountain-Mabry stood up at the next table over
and did not need to ding a glass.

"I have sat through three meetings in my life," she said,
"and I hated all three.
This town does not do meetings. It does suppers.
You want this county to remember what it's fixing to lose,
don't hand them an agenda. Hand them a plate.
Set the date. String the lights.
Put this whole town on that porch with its good dishes,
and make November stand out in the yard
and look it in the eye."

The amens came up from four congregations at once.
By the time Delores sat down,
three tables were arguing tents and two were arguing dessert,
and Earlene Tatum had her phone out
doing math with weekends,
which meant it was already happening.

Marisol looked up out of the middle of all that yes
and found Cal looking back.
Neither of them put it down.
It went on past what a churchyard gives you cover for,
and then Tunk Ferrell walked through the middle of it
with a bowl in his hand, headed back for the banana pudding,
and the yard had them again.

"Second bowl," Earlene said, approving, to the table at large.
"That man ushered both services at First Methodist this morning,
early and late, plates and every bit of it,
and still made it out here before the pudding went.
You cannot teach that."

"It's good pudding," Tunk said, and kept walking.

Marisol's Sunday logs had gone out with the coffee that morning,
same as every Sunday, rain or shine or nothing to report.
In the dinner's coffee lull she gave the group its minute
and read what the morning had added.

Concerned4Ashford had been busy on Grapevine.
Eight-fifty-one. Nine-twenty-two. Ten-oh-four. Ten-forty.
Eleven-eleven, and straight on through the noon hour,
post after post, filling every slot a Sunday morning has.
AshfordSafetyMom had agreed with every one of them in capitals,
four minutes behind, each time.

She wasn't reading the posts anymore. She read the clocks on them.

Both services at First Methodist ran the whole length of that list.
Call to worship to benediction, twice over,
and through every minute of it
somebody had been sitting somewhere with two free thumbs
and a great deal of feeling about Ashford,
while Tunk Ferrell stood in plain sight of God
and two hundred neighbors,
handing an offering plate down the pews, hand over hand.

Down the yard, Tunk laughed at something Odell said
and went in for thirds.

She got the spiral notebook out of the tote.
Past the House's people, past Ray and Earlene and Verna,
to the back page, where the questions lived.
Tunk Ferrell sat there in her market-list hand,
question mark pressed in dark,
right where the Mule had put him.

She didn't erase a thing.
She wrote his name again at the bottom of the page,
question mark and all, pressed just as dark,
and left the space above it open
for whoever it actually belonged to.

The space was the question now.
Somebody had spent the whole of a Sunday morning
being Ashford's loudest neighbor,
and it wasn't the man at the pudding table,
and there was not one name in her notebook to put above his.
Who was left over when the whole county was in church?
