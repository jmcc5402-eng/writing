The carillon struck midnight up on the hill,
and the sound came down the back road to the motor pool
and found one light burning on the whole dark campus.
The bay light over Cal Sutter's bench.

He had been there since ten.
The shop was swept. The crew was long gone.
Ratchet lay on her stencil by the bay door with her eyes open.
Down the hill the Blind Mule turned out its last two trucks,
and Delmar's smoke went up straight into the October cold,
and Ashford put itself to bed
believing a thing about him that wasn't true.

His second had sent the screenshot at ten with three question marks.
Office of the Facilities Director. His letterhead. His name.
Twelve thousand phones had it by now.
He had typed an answer four times and sent none of them,
because the real answer was in the shop drawer under his hand,
and a drawer is no way to answer anybody.
He'd wanted time. He'd had a day of it.
The town had spent his day for him.

Ratchet's ear went up.

Then tires hit the gravel out on the back road,
fast and light, quicker than anybody drives it by day.
Headlights swung across the bay door and quit.
A car door slammed.
Cal stood up off the stool and put the rag down.

Marisol came in out of the dark with the cold on her jacket
and her hair all the way down,
full of the wind she'd made getting there.
She had dressed for a ladder a long time ago that evening,
canvas jacket, jeans that had done real work,
a woman who had strung lights over half a town
and then had the town taken out of her hands.
She stopped at the edge of the light, and Cal saw her lay eyes
on the bench, the drawer, him, in that order.

Ratchet got there first.

The dog crossed the concrete at her own pace,
put her head down on Marisol's boot, and left it there.
Marisol looked at the dog a while.
The fight she'd carried in from the car had to stand and wait.

"Tell me you didn't sign it," she said.

Cal didn't answer with his mouth.
He pulled the drawer, lifted the packet out,
and set it on the steel where the light was best.

She came to the bench.
Across every page, corner to corner, gray as a ghost under the type,
ran the same two words.

DRAFT — UNSIGNED.

She turned the pages one at a time.
He watched her face make the whole trip,
the fury first, then the crack in it,
then relief flooding the low ground behind it.
Her mouth, which he had spent six weeks not staring at,
opened and closed on nothing.

"You never signed."

"No."

"Not any of it."

"Not one page."

She set the packet down.
The relief drained off as fast as it had come,
and what stood up behind it was worse than the fury,
because it was quieter.

"When did this land on your desk?"

"Monday."

"Monday." She said the word like she was checking its teeth.
"It's Tuesday night, Cal."

"I know what night it is."

"I stood on a ladder on Millrow tonight.
I told half the county the House had friends.
And you sat one hill away with this in a drawer."
Her voice didn't rise. It went the other way, down and level.
"I found out my seat was gone from a notice.
I found out your name was on the wrecking ball from a screenshot.
Twelve thousand strangers got told before I did.
Say the part where that was fine."

"It wasn't fine."

"Then say why."

"Twelve paychecks." He kept his eyes on hers and gave it to her
plain, because plain was all he had left.
"There's a winter list. There's always a winter list,
and the pen on it is mine.
If Boyd's gift dies, the list lives.
I wanted a way out of that math before I handed it to anybody."

"Anybody."

"Before I handed it to you. You first of all.
That's why you last of all."

"That is the worst arithmetic I have ever heard,
and I referee arithmetic for twelve thousand people."
She stopped. "Refereed."

"You told me once," Cal said. "The House you'd fight for.
The man holding it, you leave alone.
I held it the only way I knew. Alone."

"Then the rule was wrong, or it was half a rule.
Leave-alone was never supposed to mean lied-to."
She came around the end of the bench and stood close enough
that he could see the ladder-grime still on her knuckles.
"You don't get to decide by yourself what I'm strong enough
to carry. And you don't get to stand there with those forearms
and that tired-hero face and call the silence a favor.
It's obnoxious. It doesn't work on me."

"It's working a little."

"It is not," she said, and it was, and they both let that stand.

He took the middle section out of the packet
and put the sheets in her hands.

"Before you spend all your mad on me," he said,
"spend some on the back pages. The ground rules.
Every structure on the Millrow list gets graded
against current code. Not the code it was built under.
The code for a house going up this year.
Stair rise. Rail height. Wire gauge. Footing depth.
Pass or fail, line by line, no credit for standing."

She read. He watched her get there fast,
which he had known she would.

"Then nothing on Millrow passes."

"Not one building. Not the opera house.
Not the Checkerboard. Not Delmar's,
if a man gets honest about wire gauge."

"They wrote the ending first," she said,
"and hired your handwriting."

"That's the shape of it."

"So the survey was never a question."

"It was a verdict with a date left blank.
And the blank under the verdict is mine."

She looked at the sheets a long moment, and then at him,
and some of the mad went out of her and left room
for something with more patience in it.

"There's more," Cal said. "It's in the truck."

He went out into the cold and came back with a manila folder,
no name on the tab, and laid it open beside the packet.
Photographs, printed on the office printer, squared in order.
Eleven of them. In each one, a little rectangle of glass
rode a crack in an old plaster wall,
a date inked small in the corner.

"What am I looking at?"

"The east parlor wall. The crack everybody points at.
Somebody glued microscope slides across it, years back.
Glass won't stretch and glass won't lie.
If that wall so much as breathed, the glass would crack too."

She went through them one at a time. "They're clean."

"All eleven. The oldest one's been up nineteen years.
That wall hasn't moved the width of a pencil line."

She looked closer, at the small square writing in a corner,
and Cal watched her find the question before she asked it.

"Whose dates?"

"My wife's," he said. "She kept the watch on that wall.
The dates stop where she did."

The shop was quiet enough to hear the pit smoke settle
down the hill.
Marisol didn't say she was sorry, which he was grateful for.
She reached out and straightened one photograph
so its date sat square with the others, and that was all,
and it was enough.

"Nineteen years of proof," she said,
"against one page of rules."

"That's the fight."

"Then we're going to need better tools than a screenshot."

Cal went down the bench.

At the far end, on a clean rag with a whole shop's worth
of nothing else on it, lay a small crescent wrench.
She saw it. He watched her see it.

"That's mine."

"It is."

"It's been on a rag. Six weeks in a working shop,
and it's cleaner than my car."

"It's a good rag," Cal said.

Then he reached up to the parts wall
and took down the wrench that had hung four feet from his hand
since the first home Saturday.
Right size. Right jaw. Six weeks of Mondays it had hung there,
and every Monday he'd left it hanging,
because bringing it meant the errand was over.

"I said I'd bring you the right one."

He held it out flat across his open palm.
She put her hand over it, and for a second the steel
carried both their palms, her hand on his,
cold metal going warm in the middle,
and neither one of them was in a rush about the weight.

"Six weeks," she said. "That's the slowest hostage release
in county history."

"The right one takes finding."

"Thorough," she said. "I remember."

She closed her fingers around the wrench and kept it.

"So here's what we do," she said. "First thing. You don't quit.
If you resign, they type a new name under the same verdict
by Friday, and the new name signs it.
You stay in that office and you make them do it slow.
Every ground rule read out loud. The wall on the record.
The glass in the file, dates and all."

"Inside," Cal said.

"Inside. And I'm outside.
They took my seat and left the woman, which was careless of them.
Delores wants a supper, the whole town on that porch,
making November look it in the eye.
I don't need a badge for porches.
I know every porch in this county by its casserole."

"And in public?"

"In public we've never met.
Your name stays out of my mouth,
mine stays out of yours.
The first pair of names Boyd can tie together in a bow
is the last day either of us is any use."

"Hard rule to keep in this town."

"It's the only kind worth keeping," she said.

She was close. They were both inside the bay light by then,
and the shop smelled like diesel and WD-40 and the cold
coming off her jacket, and under all of it something warmer
that was only her.

He kissed her.
Or she kissed him.
The wrench was between them the whole time, her hand and his
and the steel, and afterward neither of them ever ruled
on who had moved first.
It was slow, and then it was not slow,
and she tasted like relief and like trouble,
both at once, and he quit trying to tell them apart.

She pulled back far enough that he could see her face,
all of it, close, plain in the bay light,
nothing between them for the first time
since a tent fell down in August.

"Well," she said. "That wasn't on the survey."

"It wasn't on mine either."

Ratchet got up off the stencil, took a long look at the two
of them, and went around the far side of the truck to lie down
facing the road, a dog with somewhere professional to be.

Marisol set the new wrench down on the clean rag,
next to the wrong one, a matched set at last,
and took two fistfuls of his flannel.

After that it was her mouth, and the smell of cut grass
still in her hair from somebody's lawn,
and the cold jacket going warm between his hands.
He picked her up and set her on the bench top, easy,
one motion, the packet shoved clear,
and heard her laugh against his mouth.

"Obnoxious," she said, and did not stop him.

Her jacket came off her shoulders and went to the concrete,
the first thing either of them had let fall all night.

Down the hill, Delmar's smoke stood over a sleeping town
that believed the screenshot and would believe it till morning.
The two people who knew better let it sleep.
The only lit door on campus went dark with the two of them
behind it, and the alliance held.
