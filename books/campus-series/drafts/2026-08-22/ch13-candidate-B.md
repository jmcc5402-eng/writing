The last Saturday of September, the Doss County Curb Market sold
out of mums by ten o'clock and spent the rest of the morning
being pleased about it.
The cold that had come through the week before had stayed on,
thinned down to a morning edge that burned off by noon,
and the market packed up in shirtsleeves under a long yellow light,
jackets waiting on the tailgates just in case.

Marisol was breaking down Earlene Tatum's table,
crating the eggs and the last of the dahlias,
because Earlene's knees quit bending around eleven
and because nobody had asked her to,
which was her favorite kind of job.

"That sweater's a good color on you," Earlene said.

"It was eight dollars."

"I said what I said."

The Zinnia Wagon was loaded and nosed toward home,
buckets strapped, coffee cans of zinnias riding the front seat,
when Birdie Pham turned the key and got a click.
The second try got less than that.
The third try got nothing at all,
and the vendors, who had been leaving, stopped.

Birdie turned the key a fourth time.
"I just filled you," she told it.

Sonny Dillard came over from the Farm & Feed curb
and stood in front of the opened hood with his arms crossed,
and so did six other men,
and they said fuel pump, and they said solenoid,
and not one of them touched anything.

"Flower truck that won't start," Sonny said.
"That's your basic wilting, right there."

Nobody took it up. The hood stayed up too.

Somebody's tailgate radio had the swap shop on,
and Tick Moran, it developed, already knew.

"Folks, if you are just now joining us," Tick said,
live from the WDSS window up the street,
"the Zinnia Wagon has quit at the curb market.
Hood is up, town has gathered, and I am told there is a committee.
Caller, hold that thought about your washer-dryer.
It is not going anywhere and neither is she."

Birdie had her phone out to call somebody
when a diesel came around the corner of the square,
geared all the way down and taking its time,
and Eli Fountain rode the stadium's turf tractor
up the middle of the street with a tow strap coiled on the fender,
which answered the question of whether the radio carried up the hill.

He didn't wave and he didn't explain.
He swung wide, backed to the Wagon's bumper,
and had the strap hooked in two motions.

"Neutral," he said. "And somebody's got to steer her."

"I'll steer her standing," Birdie said,
and stepped up onto the running board with the door swung open,
one hand in through the window on the wheel
and the other one holding her hat.

They went across the square like that, at parade speed,
the tractor walking, the flower truck rolling along behind,
and the whole market put down what it was carrying to watch.

"For those of you asking, this is live," Tick said,
between callers.
"The university has sent a tractor.
They are traveling at parade speed.
We estimate three miles an hour at the courthouse.
Folks, the tiller is sold. That is not why we are on the air.
Quit calling about the tiller."

"That boy," Earlene said, in the tone she kept for weather
that had done exactly what she'd predicted.

Past the courthouse, Birdie gave up on dignity as a lost cause,
took the hat off, and waved it.
The full wave, wrist and elbow, homecoming-queen certified,
to the shed, to the courthouse lawn,
to nobody in particular and everybody at once,
and the market waved back with its arms full of crates.

By the time the tractor turned off the square,
the pictures were already on Grapevine,
forty of them and climbing, every angle of the same slow glory,
and for one entire Saturday the group Marisol ran
had exactly one opinion, and it was unanimous.

Fifth Sundays belonged to the circuit,
and this one belonged to Mt. Nebo.

Folding tables ran end to end under the oaks,
white paper down the middles,
fried chicken and butter beans and three kinds of sheet cake,
Birdie's zinnias in coffee cans every eight feet,
and the cut-grass smell coming up wherever a chair leg bit in.
The cold had backed off to shirtsleeves by one o'clock.
September was going out the same way it had come in,
warm side up, the oak shade sliding around on the tables
whenever the wind remembered it.

Marisol had hauled the tea urns from her trunk at noon
and had charge of refills by half past,
which is what happens at a church dinner
to any woman who looks even slightly organized.

"Marisol, sugar, you're a gift," Delores Fountain-Mabry said,
going by with a pan of something under foil.

"I'm mostly ice," Marisol said, and poured.

Up and down the tables the families sat in their after-church
clothes, and here and there a folding chair stood empty
in the shape of somebody nineteen and gone,
sleeping through the Lord's own afternoon up the hill.
Marisol had two of those chairs herself,
one twenty minutes off and one at Bellamy,
and she let the fact sit with her while she poured,
and then she handed Odell Fountain his tea
and it went back to being Sunday.

Cal sat across and one down,
in a white shirt pressed sharp enough to pass inspection,
sleeves down, collar buttoned, Sunday-broad through the shoulders,
and she had noted all of that on arrival, once, thoroughly,
and had found the butter beans riveting ever since.

By the time the cobbler was down to corners,
Birdie and Eli had been married off so thoroughly
up and down those tables that the date was the only open item,
and Odell Fountain, deacon of this church
and great-uncle to the man in question,
set down his fork and asked it.

"Eli. What in this world are you waiting on?"

The near tables went quiet enough to hear the oaks.

Eli looked at his plate for a while.

"The truck needed a fuel pump," he said.

It was the worst answer ever given in that churchyard,
and every man at the table sat back and admired it.

"A fuel pump," Odell said, to the congregation at large.

"It did need one," Cal said. "Went in last evening.
He wouldn't let another man's hands near it."

Which was a worse answer and a better one at the same time,
and the men admired it past speech,
and Marisol caught the corner of Cal's mouth going up
before he got his coffee to it in time.

"For anybody worried," Birdie said, to her sheet cake,
"the pump came with a ninety-day warranty."

The sheet cake moved by relay, cut in the kitchen
and passed down the tables plate over plate,
and one plate came up the line to Cal
with Marisol the next hand in the chain.
He handed it across, palm flat under the plate,
because paper gives with cake on it,
and her fingers came down over his underneath it,
skin on skin under somebody's coconut sheet cake,
and stayed a half-second past necessary.
Mutual, public, remarked on by nobody.
Neither of them looked at anything but the cake.
Then the plate went on down the line,
and they went back to being two people
seated near each other at a church dinner.

While the coffee went around, Eli stood,
and Birdie stood up with him,
and they walked out from under the oaks
to the open grass the sun still had.
Forty people managed to watch without turning one chair.

"First grass I ever kept, this yard," Eli said.
Churchyards carry; nobody had to lean.

"Cut it crooked two summers straight," Odell said, to his coffee.

"There's a crown under it," Eli said, to Birdie.
"For the rain. Falls away both directions off the middle.
You can't see it. You can feel it in your feet
if you stand exactly right."

"Then stand me right," Birdie said.

He took her by the elbow and moved her a foot and a half,
like the foot and a half mattered,
and she stood where he set her in her church shoes,
eyes shut, hat still, and the yard held its coffee and let her.

Delores Fountain-Mabry had watched all of it
from behind her banana pudding,
and when she spoke, she did not speak small.

"Look at this yard," she said.
"No invitations. No agenda. No microphone.
And not a soul missing that can still walk.
I have chaired meetings four people came to,
and one of them was the janitor.
This town does not do meetings. It does suppers.
So quit signing that clipboard down at Delmar's
like it's a funeral book, and somebody set a date.
Put this town on that porch — the Hartwell porch,
every table, every pan, every folding chair in the county —
and make November look it in the eye."

Nobody asked what she meant by November.

"Pick the Sunday," Marisol said,
"and twelve thousand phones will know it by Tuesday."

"Then it's called," Delores said, and it was,
that easily, no gavel anywhere in sight.

And when Marisol looked up from shaking on it,
Cal was already looking,
across two tables of paper plates and pound cake,
steady, with the whole afternoon behind it,
and she held it past the point a churchyard gives you cover.

"Cal Sutter!" Odell hollered from the fellowship steps.
"Quit anchoring that chair and come fold tables.
From the middle, gentlemen. They fold from the middle."

Cal went. The yard went back to being a yard.

The tables came down and the coffee stayed up,
and Marisol sat with a styrofoam cup and did the Sunday logs,
because it was Sunday, and the logs went out with the coffee,
fifth Sunday or no.

The week's screenshots were all where she'd put them.
It was the timestamps that were new.
Concerned4Ashford, cursive over a sunset,
had posted at nine forty, ten fifty-five, and eleven twenty
that very morning,
and AshfordSafetyMom had come in behind each one
inside of four minutes, capitals blazing, regular as a relay.

Down the table, Tunk Ferrell came back from the dessert end
with his second bowl of banana pudding.

"Tunk Ferrell," Delores said. "Two services of ushering
at First Methodist and you walk into my churchyard for the pudding."

"I'd usher a third for the meringue," Tunk said.

Marisol looked at the man, and at the bowl,
and back down at her phone.
Nine forty. Ten fifty-five. Eleven twenty.
A man cannot pass a collection plate through two services
and post through them too.
And every soul who was anybody's actual neighbor
had been in a pew or a kitchen all morning
and was standing in this churchyard right now,
holding pudding, folding tables, calling a supper into being.
While she watched, the sunset account posted again,
fresh, into a county that was very nearly all
in one yard at one time.

She got the spiral notebook out of the tote.
Back page, where the questions lived,
Tunk Ferrell in her market-list hand,
the question mark pressed in dark from the night
the Mule convicted him for sport.

She didn't erase it.
She moved it, name and mark and all,
down to the bottom of the page where it could keep,
because a question mark was still the honest thing to hang on him.
And in the space above, in the same market-list hand,
she wrote the one the timestamps had handed her instead.

*If Tunk Ferrell spent the whole morning passing the plate,
who was doing the typing?*
