On the last Monday of September, the boilers went on.

Oldest first. That was the order every fall,
because the old ones needed the whole morning
to remember what they were for,
and no schedule out of any office
had ever talked Cal Sutter out of it.

He went cellar to cellar with the new hire for a shadow,
letting heat back into pipes that had sat empty since April.
At the north dorm the main shutoff had seized over the summer,
an annual event,
and the new hire got a pipe wrench on it,
then a cheater bar on the pipe wrench,
then both boots set wide,
and moved nothing in that cellar but himself.

"She's not stuck," Cal said. "She's set in her ways."

He set one hand on the wheel and it gave,
the pull stopping somewhere short of effort,
and he ran the valve open to the stops
while the kid was still catching his breath.
The new hire looked at the wheel, then at Cal,
then back at the wheel,
and adjusted some private figure upward.

"Grip it at the rim," Cal said. "It's leverage."

It was not entirely leverage.
Thirty years of this work had built him the shoulders it needed
and kept on building past the requirement,
and the green polo fit him the same at forty-seven
as the day the college issued it.

Number three lit hard, banged away down in the dark for a minute,
and came quiet when he bled the line.
By ten there was heat moving in every building on the list,
and Cal came down the hill to the motor pool
with the morning spent and the day, as far as he knew,
still ordinary.

The envelope was on his bench.

Hand-carried, no stamp, his name typed on the front.
Inside was a gray cover and a hundred-some pages,
every structure on the Millrow list graded line by line
against this year's code, pass or fail,
exactly as the ground rules had promised,
and the word DRAFT printed up every page
in letters the color of weak tea.

He read it standing, with the radio going.

The House took up the middle of it.
The east wall was in there,
measured three ways by men who had stood all day on the lawn
and never once gone in.
Stair rise, rail height, wire gauge, footing depth,
fail and fail and fail and fail,
no credit anywhere for standing,
and at the back, on a page of its own,
RECOMMEND CONDEMNATION.

Under that sat a signature line.
Under the signature line, already typed,
C. SUTTER, DIRECTOR OF FACILITIES.

Somebody in some office had reached his conclusion for him,
set his name under it in clean type,
and left him nothing to contribute but the ink.
The transmittal sheet asked for the signed original back
by week's end.
He wanted a month. The sheet gave him a week.

He stood at the bench a while.
The radio took a caller with strong opinions about the secondary.
The shop ran on around him at its usual pitch,
air tools and sockets and somebody singing wrong,
every man in that yard drawing a paycheck
that ran through the office that had typed his name.

He had the phone out and her name up on the screen
before he caught his own hand at it.

MARISOL.

His thumb lay flat on the name, not pressing.
The screen dimmed once and he woke it without moving his thumb.
She'd answer by the second ring.
She'd know something was wrong by the third,
because she read a voice the same as he read an idle,
and there was no half-answer of his
that would survive the fourth.
And twelve paychecks stood where they had stood all month,
between that phone call and the winter.

The roll door ran up on the far bay,
and the yard came in with it, diesel and shouting,
and his second was crossing the floor with a clipboard.
Cal set the phone face down by the vise.

That made once.

"Heat's up," his second said. "Four dorms report a miracle.
Two have opened every window they own."

"Thermostat's not a debate. Tell them."

"Also East Third has a new smell. They say this one's different."

"Different how?"

"They say it's got ambition."

"I'll walk it after lunch," Cal said.

"And this came for the board." She held up a cream envelope.
"Every shop on campus got one, I'm told. Same hour, same knock."

She pinned it to the crew corkboard herself, square to the frame.
Heavy paper, the Gateway across the top in watercolor,
three decks and a glass club level and the boosters' tower,
a lawn running green from the gates down Millrow,
and no House anywhere in the picture.
Under the picture, in type a man could read from the parts cage,
the pledge. Every job on the facilities payroll, guaranteed,
through every phase of construction. Guaranteed in writing.
Rex Boyd's signature sat under it, large, sure of its welcome.

The crew came to the board in ones and twos through the day,
hats back, lips moving,
and the yard got a notch easier every time.
One of the older hands read it twice
and went back to the mower deck whistling.

The draft on Cal's bench had come hand-carried that morning.
The letter on the board was dated the same Monday.
Two offices, two envelopes, one day,
timed tighter than campus mail had ever run on its own,
and the only man standing where both papers could be seen at once
reached up, squared the letter on its tack
so his crew could read it level all week,
and went to walk the dorm smell down himself.

---

WDSS gave the afternoon its swap-and-shop hour,
and the hour had a pontoon boat in it.

"Twenty-two-foot pontoon," Tick Moran read out, the first time.
"Runs good. Trailer included. Motor needs attention.
Twelve hundred dollars, and the caller says he's flexible.
Lines are open, Ashford."

The lines stayed open.

Eli Fountain came in off the yard for a mower-deck belt
and stayed on the strength of the pontoon.

"Runs good, motor needs attention," the new hire said.
"Which one is it?"

"Both," said the older hand at the press.
"It ran good the day he wrote the ad."

"How old's the ad?"

"How old's the boat?"

Tick came back at the bottom of the hour.
"Pontoon's still with us, people. He's down to nine hundred.
Says that's firm."

"He said firm at twelve hundred," the new hire said.

"A man can be firm more than once," Eli said.

"What's it actually worth? Eli. Real money."

"Trailer's worth three hundred if the axle's straight," Eli said.
"Tires another forty if they hold air.
The boat, you'd pay somebody to come take it off."

The new hire worked it out on the bench top in soapstone,
trailer plus tires minus the hauling,
carried the one,
and stood back from the arithmetic.

"That's forty dollars."

"Forty dollars," Eli said, "and he delivers."

That got the whole bay.
Eli picked up his belt and went back to the yard
with the face of a man who had said nothing funny.

Tick tried once more near the top of the news.
"Last call on that pontoon. Nine hundred of anybody's dollars.
Somebody be brave."

"I wouldn't take a boat for free," the new hire said.

Nobody took it up.
Tick moved on to a dinette set, six chairs, two of them loose.

Ray's boy came up the hill at four with a to-go box
and set it on the end of Cal's bench
like he'd been making the stop for years.
Grease pencil on the lid, all capitals, Ray's hand.
MULE, THURSDAY — WE'RE CELEBRATING NOTHING, COME ANYWAY.

The box was a chopped plate, still warm through the cardboard,
and Cal ate it at the bench and read the lid while he ate,
and when the box was empty he tore the lid off square
and propped it against the radio,
where a man closing up in the dark would see it
and remember he'd been asked somewhere.

---

The carillon struck five up the hill.
The yard wound down on schedule,
trucks out by five thirty, the compressor cycling to a stop,
his second last across the floor with her keys out.

"Anything for the morning?" she said.

"Boilers'll hold. I'll open."

"You opened today."

"I'll open tomorrow too."

She had a look at him on her way past, kept it to herself,
and went.

He fed Ratchet and let the shop empty all the way out.
Ratchet ate her dinner and then came and lay down
inside the office door where she could keep the bench in view,
which was not her spot,
and neither of them remarked on it.

He took the envelope out to the truck first.

Behind the bench seat, in the milk crate with the House's parts,
rode a manila folder with nothing on the tab,
the wall's whole history in glass and dates and photographs.
Cal stood at the open door with the envelope in one hand
and did not put it in.
The crate was the House's.
The envelope was the thing aimed at the House.
Those two did not ride together.

He went back inside and cleared the bottom drawer of the bench,
the one drawer in the shop with a working lock,
moved the title work and the spare padlocks to the shelf,
and laid the draft in flat, gray cover up.
He shut the drawer and turned the key,
then worked the key off the big ring, small and brass,
and dropped it into his shirt pocket,
where the House key already rode by itself.
Two keys now, one pocket,
neither one explained to anybody.

He woke the phone on purpose this time.

Her name came up and he put his thumb on it and held it there,
not pressing,
the office light the last one burning,
the pledge letter square on the cork,
the Mule lid propped against the radio,
Thursday three days off.

She had planted herself in his path once
over a three-dollar wrench,
palm up, chin up, sun on her shoulders,
and she had not moved until she got an answer.

This was not a wrench.

He took his thumb off her name
and set the phone face down on the bench.

That made twice.

Ratchet lifted her head at whatever sound a man makes
doing a thing like that,
and after a while she put it back down.

He hooked two fingers under the drawer pull and tried it.
It held.
He put the bay lights out walking, back bay to front,
took his jacket off the hook,
and let himself out the office door.
Ratchet walked him as far as the gate and turned back.

He pulled the gate to and chained it.
The draft was in the drawer, the drawer was locked,
and the shop was dark.
