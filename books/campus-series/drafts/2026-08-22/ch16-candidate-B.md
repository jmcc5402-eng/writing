The last light Cal owned on this campus was the one over bay two,
and he sat under it near midnight with nothing torn down
and nothing going back together,
which any man on his crew could have told you was the bad sign.

The phone lay face-down on the bench
under the shop rag he'd dropped over it around ten,
and it kept lighting up anyway, a slow pulse through the cloth.
He'd quit turning it over. The picture didn't improve with the angle.
His own letterhead, gone gray from being a picture of a picture
of a picture, and one line of it blown up big enough
to read across a bar. *Office of the Facilities Director. C. Sutter.*
Twelve thousand phones, and his name loose in every one of them,
wearing a signature he had never put on anything.

Ratchet had left her stencil by the bay door an hour ago
and lay against his boot instead, not asleep.
She wasn't fooled either.

Then the gravel started in about a car.

Small engine, coming up the back road too fast,
braking late where the good asphalt quit.
Nobody drove out past the brick at midnight by accident.
Headlights swung across the bay doors and died,
and a car door closed hard on the first try.

She came in through the open bay
in the clothes she'd been stringing lights in,
flannel sleeves shoved back, dust ground into one knee,
her hair all the way down and past caring,
and the bay light found her face and stayed on it.
Fury, and road, and underneath the two of them
the whole night's wreckage.
She was the best thing he'd seen since Friday,
and she had driven out here to take him apart.

She got three steps in with her mouth already loaded.

Ratchet crossed the concrete first.
The old dog went straight to her, heavy and certain,
and put her head down on Marisol's boot, all the weight of it,
and stayed.

Marisol looked down at the dog.
Some of the loaded went out of her shoulders
and none of it out of her voice.

"I came here to ask you one question," she said,
"and your dog is cheating."

"She votes early. Ask it."

"Did you sign it?"

He didn't answer with his mouth.
He stood, took the ring off his belt,
found the small key he hadn't touched since Monday,
and unlocked the bottom drawer of the bench.
He set what was in it under the light and stepped out of her way.

Across every page, corner to corner, in letters gray as rain,
ran the same two words. DRAFT — UNSIGNED.

She turned the pages with just her fingertips,
like paper that might go off.
RECOMMEND CONDEMNATION, in type nobody had asked him for.
The signature line under it, bare.
She read the last page, then the first page again,
and when she looked up, the relief and the anger
arrived on the same breath, and the anger won the toss.

"You never signed it."

"No."

"Then twelve thousand people are wrong about you tonight,
and you let me be one of them."
Her voice didn't rise. It got precise, which was worse.
"When did this show up with the verdict already typed?"

"Monday."

"And the rules it was graded on. When did you read those?"

He could have made it softer, and didn't. "Friday."

"Friday." She set the pages down flat
and took her hand off them.
"I stood on a ladder tonight stringing lights on that lawn,
and you'd known since Friday the test was fixed.
So no, Cal. I'm not here about the survey.
A survey is a fight, and I know what to do with a fight.
I'm here about the silence."

"I know it."

"Ray's porch. I made you a rule and I kept it.
I fought the pen and left the man alone.
Nobody told me the man would go and leave me alone too."

"Then you'll have all of it," he said. "Standing up, if you want."

"Standing up."

He gave it to her straight, in order, the whole accounting.
Boyd in the shop gravel back in September, friendly as weather.
The gift, the shortfall, the list with twelve names on it
and the pen that could keep them off it, his.
'Unsalvageable,' over his signature, as cover for a trust
that couldn't be the one to sell the town's porch.
Friday's packet and its back section.
Monday's draft with his name pre-typed under a verdict
he had never reached.

"Every day I didn't sign bought the House a day," he said.
"The minute any of it got loud, Boyd quits needing my pen
and starts typing names. I've got men whose whole winter
is that list. And you —" He stopped and made himself finish it.
"You move on things. I hand you a rigged rulebook,
you're in six rooms by Sunday.
I never figured out how to arm you without lighting the fuse,
so I sat on it. Nobody knows what that drawer held.
Not my second. Not Ray. You're the first."

"Don't you dare hand me the silence like it's a gift."

"I'm not. I'm telling you what it cost.
I'd have called it the right call yesterday.
Tonight I watched them spend my name anyway.
The quiet bought nothing,
and it cost everything the quiet was supposed to protect."

She looked at him a long moment across the paperwork.

"What else is there," she said. "All of it, you said."

"Stay put."

He went out to the truck,
hauled the milk crate over the bench seat one-handed,
folder and all,
and carried it in through the lit door.

She watched the whole trip with her arms crossed.
"For the record, hauling fifty pounds one-handed at midnight
is a cheap way to argue."

"Forty."

He set the crate on the bench and took out the folder
with nothing written on its tab.
First the packet. He slid the back section free —
the ground rules, the little expectation sheets
that had decided everything before one man climbed out of one truck —
and put those pages in her hands.

She read standing up, one hip against the bench.
Current code, line by line. Stair rise. Rail height. Wire gauge.
Pass or fail, and no credit anywhere for standing.

"This fails the opera house," she said.
"This fails the church I ate sheet cake in on Sunday."

"It fails Delmar's, if anybody gets honest about wire gauge."

"It was never a test."

"No. It's a finding with the date left blank."

Then the photographs, printed off the office printer,
and he laid them out in order down the clear end of the bench,
top of the wall to the floor. Eleven.

"Glass," he said. "Microscope slides, glued across the crack
in the east parlor wall. Glass won't stretch and glass won't lie.
If that wall had moved a hair since they went up,
one of these would show a line."

She went down the row twice, bent close, touching nothing.

"There's no line."

"There's no line."

"These dates." She stopped over the oldest one,
the ink gone tea-colored, the small square patient hand.
"Whose writing is this?"

"My wife's."

He squared the last photograph to the edge of the row
and left his hand beside it.

"She kept the watch on that wall for nineteen years.
The survey crew stood all day on the lawn
and never once went inside to read it."

Marisol stood over the row of dates
with her palm flat on the bench beside them, not touching,
and gave the handwriting its minute.

"Then we've got a witness," she said at last.
"Nineteen years of sworn testimony."

The air he'd been holding since Friday got away from him,
all at once, and he let it go.

Up the hill the carillon let midnight loose,
the sound coming down the back road thin on the cold air,
and neither of them moved until it finished.

He went to the parts wall then.

The right size had hung on its peg since the first home Saturday,
four feet from the bench, all season, never coming down.
Four feet from where her little crescent lay on a clean rag
with a whole shop's worth of nothing else near it.
He took the right one off the wall at last and crossed back to her.

She looked from the wrench in his hand to the rag on the bench.

"You kept mine on a rag."

"Best rag I've got."

The laugh got out of her sideways, wet at the edges,
and it was the first good sound the shop had heard all night.

"I said I'd bring you the right one," Cal said,
and set it across her open palm,
the same palm she'd planted in his path in August,
and her fingers closed over the steel with his hand still under it,
palm to palm with the wrench between,
and neither hand went anywhere for a while.

"Two months," she said. "I was getting attached to the debt."

"Some of it's still owing."

"Then here are my terms. Second edition."
She kept hold of the wrench and made him keep hold of it too.
"You work it from the inside. You don't sign.
You make them grade the wall they never walked in to see,
and you buy every day a day can be bought.
I take the outside — the supper, the town, all twelve thousand —
and none of it ever wears your name.
Not one soul hears your name out of my mouth in public,
and mine never leaves this shop.
And the drawer stays open between us. Everything, both directions,
the same day it lands."

"Both directions," Cal said. "Taken."

"Say the part you're not saying."

"Come morning you're still out to beat the thing
my crew's winter rides on. This doesn't fix November.
It just means nobody's alone in it."

"Nobody's alone in it," she said. "That's the contract."

The look after that went long,
and then it went on past every excuse either of them had left,
and Ratchet's tail went against the concrete, twice,
loud as applause in all that quiet,
and Marisol laughed low, and Cal closed the distance
while the laugh was still on her mouth.

He kissed her in the smell of diesel and WD-40,
under the one light he'd left burning,
her hand still full of the wrench
and her knuckles caught between them against his chest.
She tasted like relief and like trouble,
and he quit pretending he had ever wanted one without the other.

She pulled back just far enough to see him.
The bay light was full on her face and she didn't move out of it,
and for once there was nothing standing
between him and whatever her face decided to do next.

"This changes nothing in November," she said, near his mouth,
which was not where a person stood to change nothing.

"Not one line of it."

"Opposite sides of a payroll."

"Same side of the truth," Cal said. "It'll have to hold."

She set the wrench down on the stack of it,
draft and rules and photographs all,
steel across the paperwork like the last word on the subject,
and took two fistfuls of his jacket.

He lifted her onto the clear end of the workbench
without losing her mouth,
and her boot hooked behind his knee,
and his hands learned the warm line of her waist
through a flannel that had been up a ladder all night,
and she pushed the canvas jacket back off his shoulders
and he let it go wherever jackets go.
Her hands came back for the flannel's buttons, hers, undoing them
with the same efficiency she ran a town with,
and he helped where helping was wanted,
which by then was everywhere.

Down the hill the Blind Mule let out its last table,
voices small and happy in the dark,
and Delmar's smoke stood straight up over Millrow,
the town's flag flying at midnight for nobody,
and every porch light on the square was long since down.
In the one lit room on campus, her flannel hit the shop floor,
and the terms held.
