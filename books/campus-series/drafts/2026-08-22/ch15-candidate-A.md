They strung the lights the first Tuesday in October,
and the rig-up was a hoedown by the second ladder.

Delores wanted the lawn lit by Saturday,
when the whole town would sit down to supper on that porch
and make November look it in the eye,
and the town had heard *lights* and understood *party,*
and the town was not wrong.
By dusk there were three ladders, a cooler of seltzers
nobody would admit to bringing,
and more helpers than jobs,
which is the correct ratio for getting nothing done beautifully.
Sonny Dillard had run two work lights up a pole
and taken personal charge of the breaker box.

"One plug to a socket," Sonny told a man holding three.

Marisol was up the tall ladder with a strand of bulbs
looped over her shoulder like a bandolier,
walking them from the big oak toward the first porch post.
Birdie Pham had the ladder's feet and the artistic direction.

"Droop," Birdie called up. "More droop. That's a clothesline.
I want lazy."

"You want lazy, climb up here and relax it yourself."

"I'm foreman. Droop."

Earlene Tatum had brought her own lawn chair for the purpose,
set it dead center of the lawn with a thermos,
and supervised.

"Left end's low," she reported.

"The left end is art," Birdie said.

"Then the right end isn't.
And Delores wants it done by Saturday.
Delores also wants the good tables. Somebody tell Delores."

"You tell Delores."

"I've met Delores," Earlene said, and poured her coffee.

Down at the porch steps, Verna Poteat had claimed a couple
who had only come over to carry cords.

"Second board from the top," she was telling them.
"Not the bottom step. The mum holds the bottom step.
Second from the top, golden hour,
and her mama can see the whole thing from the walk.
Sixty years of honeymoons at the Magnolia Court, sugar.
I know my staging."

"We're not engaged," the young man said.

"You're on my porch," Verna said,
and moved him a foot to his left.

Supper had been ordered at six-forty from the one app
that would drive this far out,
and the phone propped against Earlene's thermos
had the driver twenty minutes away ever since.
The little car on the map circled the water tower,
turned down a road that did not exist,
and thought about it a while.

"Still twenty minutes," Birdie said, at half past eight.
"That man has seen things."

"I don't use the apps," Earlene said.

"Droop," said Birdie, sighting down the strand.

Marisol came down, moved the ladder, and went back up,
and it was a good ladder to be up.
The evening was mild for October,
the group in her pocket running warm with supper plans
and folding-chair diplomacy,
the lawn below her loud with people
she could name in the dark by their laughs.
A year ago she'd have watched a night like this
through a windshield.
Now she was the one at the top of it, hanging the gold.

The couple from the porch steps came past under her ladder
with a coil of cord apiece,
and the young woman stopped and looked up.

"You're the tow-number lady. Mama still tells that story."

"The number did the work," Marisol said. "Tell her hey."

"You've got a show-woman's eye, sugar," Verna called over.
"Those bulbs sit like a necklace."

"It's the foreman's necklace. I just climb."

Behind all of it the House stood dark and easy,
its front door pulled to for the night, same as every night,
and the spill of Sonny's work lights reached
as far as the east lattice,
where the rosebush held every bud shut.

Birdie found the plug at nine and called for a test.

"Everybody hush.
If this pops the breaker, we never wanted lights anyway."

The first strand came on all down its lazy line,
forty warm bulbs against a dark house,
and the whole lawn cheered.

"Verdict," Birdie demanded.

"Lazy," Marisol said. "Perfect and lazy."

"Leave it burning," Birdie ruled.
"A lit thing gets finished faster than a dark one."

So they left the one strand up and coiled the rest
for Thursday, and the party got louder for being lit and half done,
and nobody went home on schedule.

The group ran on in her pocket the whole time,
and into the ten o'clock quiet of it a first-flight mother
posted about a boy who hadn't called since Friday.
Marisol stood at the foot of the ladder
and built the reply she had built a hundred times,
*week nine, my love,* the standing Sunday call,
the laundry math that always brought them home.

She was half through it when the reply quit under her thumbs.

The moderator mark was gone from beside her name.
In its place sat a small gray banner.
*Moderator privileges for this account are pending review.*
And under that, formal and whole, a name she mostly saw
on tax documents. *Marisol Pruett.*

She read it twice. It said the same thing twice.

Her pins were gone off the top of the board.
Four years of them, the parking answer set to music,
the first-flight thread, *pinned post, my loves,*
unpinned in the time it took to hang forty bulbs.
The half-built reply sat in its box
with nowhere it was allowed to go.

Something went cold under her ribs,
and it wasn't the review.

A review meant flags. Flags meant members,
a lot of them, all at once, all willing.
She knew which members.
The shiny August accounts, born mad and fluent,
three days old the first time they typed *firetrap,*
and every one of them waved through the gate in batches
by her own thumb, from a stopped car on a Saturday,
approve, approve, approve,
four minutes start to finish, being helpful.
She had held the door herself.

Up the lawn the bulbs burned gold over the folding ladders,
and the people under them went on being the town,
and for the length of one long breath
Marisol stood on the dark side of her own party,
holding a phone that had just changed owners.

The time was nine forty-one.
She knew because she screenshotted the banner,
which is what Sunday had trained her to do,
and the screenshot wrote the hour in the corner for her,
neat as a postmark.

She told the lawn nothing.
The lawn had earned its evening, Saturday needed it whole,
and there was no version of *they took the group*
that wouldn't land like a tree through a roof.
She stacked chairs. She minded cords.
Her hands stayed useful the whole time
her thumb kept going back to the banner.

They were loading the last ladder onto Birdie's truck
when every phone on the lawn went off in the same second.

She knew that shudder.
She'd been standing in the curb market in August
the first time the whole county's pockets went off together,
and it had not meant good news since.

Verna Poteat had her phone out and up
while the men around her were still finding their glasses,
thumb and finger going like a card dealer's,
screenshot, crop, blow it up, brightness to daylight.
She read it out the same as she'd read the rendering,
loud enough for the lawn and most of Millrow.

"'Condition survey. Millrow structures.'
'Finding. Recommend condemnation.'
And the letterhead. 'Office of the Facilities Director.'
Typed under the office, plain as a headstone. 'C. Sutter.'"

"Cropped tight, too," Verna added, turning the phone sideways.
"Whoever sent this trimmed it to fit the name on a screen."

Nobody was listening to that part.

"That's the survey," somebody said.

"That's the House's survey."

"He said it his own self on Ray's porch," Sonny said.
"One name on that clipboard, he said. His."

"And him minding that porch all fall," Verna said,
"roses and gutters, like it was family."

"Minding it for the paperwork," somebody said,
and nobody stepped on it,
and that was the sound of a name being welded to a thing.

Earlene, for once, said nothing at all.

Marisol stood with her own phone lit in her hand,
the same page, the same letterhead,
the name she'd once looked at too long in a diner
sitting under a word no town forgives.
She had drawn her own line on Delmar's porch
in front of half this lawn.
Fight the pen with everything. Leave the man alone.
It had been an easy rule when the pen was a rumor.
Now the pen had found its paper,
every soul on this lawn was reading his name off it,
and she could not tell whether the rule
was asking to be kept or to be paid out.

She was still looking at the name
when Birdie took her elbow.

"Hey," Birdie said, under the noise. "Where'd you go?"

"Nowhere yet."

Birdie looked at her, then at the phone, then back.
"You want company for the nowhere?"

"No. Thank you. Get Earlene home."

"Text me when you get wherever nowhere is," Birdie said,
and let the elbow go.

The lawn broke up in twos and threes,
phones out, voices low,
the argument already moving to kitchens and group threads
where it would run all night.
For the first fall in years,
wherever this town talked next,
she couldn't pin one true thing to the top of it.

Nobody unplugged the strand.
Whether that was heart or forgetting, the bulbs stayed on,
forty warm lights over an emptying lawn,
half a job, gold against a dark house.

Her car sat across Millrow under the oaks.
She got in and did not start it.

The screenshot was still open in her hand.
Twelve thousand phones had it by now,
and the phones behind those phones,
the county doing its physics,
nobody sending it and everybody forwarding it,
and by morning there would not be a soul
between here and the state line
who hadn't read his name off it.

She put her thumb on the name.

Not the banner. Not the handles. The name.
*C. Sutter,* in somebody's letterhead type,
under her thumb the exact width of the word.

She knew things about the man the letterhead didn't.
She knew what his hands did with a folding tent frame,
two motions, a lift and a quarter-turn, done.
She knew what they did with twine and a rose cane,
tied in against the October wind,
tight enough to hold, loose enough to grow.
She knew the pit light at five in the morning
going gold along the gray at his temples
while he handed her coffee and asked her not one thing.

And she knew the page under her thumb
came out of his office, over his title,
aimed at the porch where this town
had knelt for a hundred years.
Both things sat on the same square of glass,
and her thumb covered the name and resolved neither.

Fight the pen. Leave the man alone.
Either that rule was the truest thing
she'd said all fall, or it was the dumbest,
and the only man who knew which one
was on the far side of campus with the answer.
*Whole campus runs through his key ring,*
Earlene had told her in August,
back when that was a fun fact.
If your name went off like a bomb
across a whole county at night,
you'd be where your keys were.
She was betting the last of a very bad evening on it.

She started the car.

The strand of bulbs held its gold in her mirror
the whole way down Millrow,
and she drove away from it with both hands on the wheel,
ten and two, the first time in a year
she'd needed the wheel that much.

The campus at midnight was shut down building by building,
dark windows, dark doors,
the tower striking twelve up over the oaks
as she came through the gates.
The pavement quit past the last streetlight
and the gravel took up under the tires, loud, honest,
and at the far end of the service road
one bay light stood over one door,
the only light burning on the whole campus,
lit, not reached, and she kept driving.
