Boilers went on the last Monday of September.

The rule was older than anybody left to ask about it,
and the rule was Cal's now, so he came through the motor-pool gate
at six with the truck bed loaded, pump heads, gasket stock,
a spare aquastat riding shotgun where a passenger would go.
Ratchet gave the diesel two thumps from her parking spot
and walked him to the office door.
The radio went on before the lights did.
WDSS 1340, the Monday show, Tick Moran promising weather
and delivering opinions.

His second came off the yard with the clipboard.

"Sixty-one work orders. Fifty-eight of them are the word heat."

"They'll have it by dark."

"They'd like a time."

"Dark is a time." He took the three that weren't heat,
read them walking, and handed two back.
"Those keep till Wednesday. The gym roof doesn't.
Send the new hire up with me after North Two."

North Two's circulator pump had seized over the summer
and was not going to apologize for it.
The new head ran eighty pounds with the volute,
and Cal took it off the tailgate and carried it down
the mechanical-room stairs one-armed,
set it, seated it, and had the bolts started finger-tight
before the new hire got the toolbox to the landing.

"That's a two-man lift," the new hire said.

"It was."

The new hire looked at the pump, then at Cal,
and quietly moved some of his own ambitions back a few years.
The boiler lit on the first try, swore in steam for a minute,
and settled down to work.
They went building to building after that, north to south,
wheels broken loose, lines bled, gauges tapped and believed,
and behind them the campus warmed up one basement at a time.

---

The van came into the yard at nine with a rental hum in it.

Ratchet's head came up and her tail went still,
and her system had never once returned a wrong answer.
Cal signed a screen with one finger at twelve past nine
and carried the envelope into the office,
engineer's letterhead, his own name through the window,
sealed to him alone.

He shut the office door. He did that maybe four times a year.

The findings ran the thickness of a parts catalog.
The numbers in them were the field men's numbers,
and the numbers were honest. He'd watched them shot.
It was the grading that did the work.
Every number stood against the rules from the back section,
stair rise, rail height, wire gauge, footing depth,
this year's code held up against a hundred-year house,
line by line, no credit anywhere for standing.
Then the last page.

RECOMMEND CONDEMNATION.

Under it, already typed, OFFICE OF THE FACILITIES DIRECTOR,
C. SUTTER, and a date line left blank for him to be helpful on.

He read it standing. Then he sat down and read it again.

He had not reached that verdict.
Nobody had asked him to reach it.
It had been reached in somebody else's shop, weeks ago,
and shipped here with his name already under it,
the whole thing needing exactly one thing from him,
and that one thing was ink.

The cover sheet asked for the signed original back
inside the week. [CHECK: a deadline is briefed ("the deadline
gives him none"); its exact day is not ruled — written around as
"inside the week"]

He wanted a month. He'd have settled for a weekend.

Eleven pieces of glass on Millrow said the wall hadn't moved
in nineteen years.
The glass was not in the packet.
Nobody had asked the glass anything.

The office desk had one drawer with a lock,
bottom right, the only lock inside the shop,
and the smallest key on his ring had never had a job before.
The envelope went in flat, typed page down.
The key turned. He tried the drawer once. It held.

Then he opened the door and went back out into the noise,
because fifty-eight work orders said the word heat,
and heat was a thing he could still put his hands on.

---

The crew came in at ten and found the letter on the corkboard.

It had come over in the morning campus mail,
addressed to the shop, MEMO TO ALL FACILITIES PERSONNEL,
and his second had pinned it square
between the church-league schedule and the safety card,
because that is what a shop does with a memo.
One cream page, development-office letterhead, four paragraphs
in round, friendly type, pledging that every name
on the campus facilities roll would have work
through the Gateway construction and work after it.
It was signed by nobody in particular
on behalf of everybody important.

"Every job on the roll," the welder read out,
"carried through the build and after."

"Says who?"

"Says the letterhead."

The shop got quiet in a register the radio couldn't fill.
Men read it in twos, coffee going cold in their hands.
Twelve names on that roll. Twelve kitchens attached to them.

His second came and stood at his shoulder.

"Want it down?"

"It's a corkboard," Cal said. "Leave it."

He had signed for the envelope at twelve past nine.
The letter had gone up before ten.
One page sealed to him alone, one page addressed to every man
he was responsible for, both of them landing
the same Monday morning, timed like a tide chart.
He did the arithmetic once and kept the total to himself.

---

At noon Tick Moran opened the swap-and-shop hour
with a pontoon boat.

"Twenty-two foot," Tick said. "Motor turns over on the second try.
He is asking two thousand even, and folks, he is firm."

The shop had opinions before the sentence was done.

"Forty dollars," the welder said. "Sight unseen. Forty."

"Motor alone's worth three hundred," the paint-bay man said.

"Motor turns over on the second try. You heard the man. Forty."

"You have never seen this boat," his second said,
passing through with an armload of filters.

"I've heard it described. Forty."

By half past, Tick was back.
"The pontoon is still with us.
He has come down to eighteen hundred,
and he'd like me to mention the trailer is included.
The trailer was always included. That was implied."

"Thirty-five," the welder said. "It lost value just now."

At one o'clock the boat was entertaining trades.
"He would consider a gooseneck, a generator, or, quote,
a serious conversation," Tick read, and the laugh went up
off the shop floor and carried out into the yard.

Near the end of the hour a caller got through,
and it was Sonny Dillard's voice on the little speaker.

"I know that boat. It's sat in his side yard four years.
It's green."

There was air noise while Tick waited for the rest of it.
The rest of it never came.

"Thank you, Sonny," Tick said, and went to the stock report.

The crew argued the boat the rest of the afternoon
and never once called the station,
and the price on the shop floor closed firm at forty dollars,
on principle, from men who did not want a boat.

---

Ray came up the hill at two, between the lunch rush
and the supper prep, and handed a to-go box
through the office door.

"You didn't come down."

"Boiler day."

"Figured. Chopped plate. Pickle's under the cornbread,
where it rides."

Cal went for his pocket and Ray was already backing out the door.

"What do I owe you?"

"Read the lid," Ray said, and left.

The lid had marker on it, Ray's big block hand.
MULE, THURSDAY. WE'RE CELEBRATING NOTHING. COME ANYWAY.

Cal ate at the bench with the lid propped where he could see it.
Thursday the whole town would be in that one room,
celebrating nothing, glad to. She'd be in the middle of it,
laughing that laugh that took her whole face along with it,
and every soul in the place would be easier
for not knowing what a drawer up the hill had in it.

He took his coffee out to the truck, to have a door he could close.

The phone came out of his pocket.
Her name was in it under M, just the one name,
the way she'd put it in herself.
His thumb found it. He hadn't asked it to.

One tap and it would ring wherever she was,
and she'd know by the second sentence,
and by Friday she'd have the county rearranged,
chin up, hair coming out of its knot one strand at a time,
magnificent, twelve thousand phones behind her.
And the letter on the corkboard said what happened
to twelve paychecks if a man made trouble,
in four friendly paragraphs that never once said it.

Knuckles on the glass. His second, holding a work order.

"East Third says their radiator is speaking in tongues."

"That's air." He put the phone face down on the bench seat
and got out. "I'll bleed it myself."

---

By dark the campus had its heat, all of it,
north to south, fifty-eight orders closed.
His second signed out at six with the clipboard under her arm.

"East Third now describes the radiator as reformed."

"It is."

She looked once at the office door, which was shut,
and had been shut more today than in the year previous,
and she didn't ask, and he didn't help her with it.

"Night, Cal."

"Night."

He fed Ratchet. She ate her dinner and then lay down
by the truck's front tire instead of her spot,
which was as much as Ratchet ever said about anything.

He went back in and unlocked the drawer and read it all again,
the numbers, the rules, the last page, his name in type
under a sentence he had never said.
The shop was quiet around it. The compressor cycled and quit.

He sat at the desk and took the phone out.

Her name lit up the dark office, one word under M,
and he looked at it a long time,
past the point where looking had an errand,
her name and his thumb and nothing moving.
Then ten o'clock came down the hill from the carillon,
one note after another through the cold air,
and the office came back around him, bench and board and bay.

His thumb was still on her name.
This time he had put it there himself, and he knew it,
and that was the part he sat with.
Of everybody in the county, she was the one he wanted to tell first.
There had been a time that honor went elsewhere,
and he sat with that too, a minute, in the dark.

Two sentences and she'd know.
She'd know all the way, because she was who she was,
and then it would be hers to carry into a room
full of people whose jobs he was still standing in front of,
four days from a deadline he had not agreed to
and could not stop.

He pressed the side button.
The screen went dark with her name still on it.
The phone went in his pocket.

The draft went back in its envelope, the envelope flat
in the drawer, typed page down.
The small key turned, and he tried the drawer once, and it held,
and nobody in Ashford knew what it was holding but him.

He cut the shop lights and pulled the gate to behind him,
and Ratchet came as far as her parking spot and sat down in it.

The drawer was locked. The shop was dark.
