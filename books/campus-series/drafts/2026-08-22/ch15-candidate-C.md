Marisol was at the top of a borrowed orchard ladder,
a hundred feet of string lights over one shoulder,
when the delivery app moved its estimate again.

"Twenty minutes away," she called down. "Still."

"It said twenty minutes at seven o'clock," Birdie Pham said from the
footing position, both hands on the rails.
"Left a little. Your swag's drooping."

"My swag is generous."

"Your swag is asleep."

"Lights ought to droop," Earlene Tatum ruled, from a lawn chair she
had carried up the hill for exactly this purpose,
thermos socked into the cupholder, supervising under her whole hat.
"A tight light is a gas-station light. Droop it at the porch."

"Who made you foreman?" Birdie said.

"The chair. Authority sits where authority brought its own seat."

The first Tuesday of October had come in dry and easy,
cool enough to make ladder work a privilege,
warm enough that nobody had gone home for more than a flannel shirt.
Work lights stood around the lawn on their stands,
moths working the beams,
and down at the east end of the porch
the rosebush held every bud shut in the spill of them.

Up the lawn the House kept its own dark,
front door pulled to for the night,
and she hadn't asked who'd minded that on his way off the hill,
because she knew,
and the knowing sat warm under the flannel,
nobody's business but hers.

The lights were Delores Fountain-Mabry's doing, technically.
This town does suppers, not meetings, Delores had said on Sunday,
put everybody on that porch and make November look it in the eye,
and by Tuesday the supper had grown a guest list, a porch,
and now a ceiling,
and half of Millrow had walked up after work to help hang it.

Verna Poteat had stationed herself at the foot of the porch steps,
giving a couple off the Shed Thread an orientation
nobody remembered requesting.

"When the time comes—and look at the two of you, it's coming—it is
the third step. Inside. The pale one."
Verna held up one finger.
"Not tonight; tonight the House is asleep.
But it's the third step, facing the door, and you kneel slow.
Sixty years of honeymoons at the Magnolia Court,
and I have never once had to give a refund."

The couple, who had come up the hill to hold a ladder,
held hands instead, which was the correct use of the information.

"Marisol, sugar," Verna called across the lawn.
"Come tell this child the pie rule."

"The good stories run you a pie," Marisol said, from the ladder.
"Verna sets the price. I just collect the stories."

"And spell Tatum right," Earlene said.

The app chimed against Marisol's hip at half past eight.
"Dinner is twenty minutes away," she reported.

"That man has been twenty minutes away since dark," Verna said.
"He's not a driver, he's a comet.
He'll come back around in June."

"He's circling the roundabout," Birdie said. "They all do."

"I had a ladder like that one," Earlene said.

Marisol tipped the phone so the chair could see the little map,
the car icon making its slow lap of the square,
and everybody in earshot laughed,
and Birdie went to the truck for the cooler,
because this town fed itself or it didn't eat.

At nine, just to see, Marisol called it.
"Throw it, Birdie. The done half."

"Throwing."

The ceiling came up gold, half a ceiling, oak to porch post,
swaying a little where the air moved,
and everybody under it made the sound a crowd makes at fireworks.
Marisol stood at the foot of her ladder and looked,
longer than checking the droop required.
The lit half swayed. The dark half waited on tomorrow.
Behind all of it the House held still with its door pulled to,
and out past everything, where nobody was looking tonight,
November sat unimpressed.

"Well, there's the ceiling," Verna said from the steps.
"Now all this lawn wants is a floor and a band."

"The band's the radio," Earlene said. "It has always been the radio.
And you'd photograph well up there, Marisol. Somebody ought to."

"It's the lighting," Marisol said, and moved an extension cord.

Her phone had run ordinary Tuesday traffic all evening,
and she'd worked it one-thumbed between rungs.
A mother two states off was deciding between Thanksgiving flights.
Marisol had the reply half typed, book the Tuesday, my love,
the Wednesday flights are a casino,
and was reaching for send when the send button grayed out.

A small gray box came up instead.

*You no longer have permission to post in this group.*

She backed out of the app and went in again,
the fix that fixed everything.
The group loaded fine. Fast, even.
The badge was gone from beside her name.
At the top of her notifications, stamped 9:41 p.m.,
sat one line from Grapevine itself, gray as a form letter.

*Moderator status — Marisol Pruett: pending review.*

Her thumbs ran the screenshot drill on their own authority,
one, two, stamped and kept,
before she had finished reading the line they were keeping.

The cold came in a second behind the drill.
Review meant votes. Votes meant members in good standing,
and she knew which members had the time tonight.
She had counted them all fall,
shiny and fluent and born mad in the same week,
and she knew to the day how they had gotten past the door,
because she had held it open herself.
August. A truck failing to parallel park on Millrow,
four hundred pending in the queue,
and her own thumb, brisk and helpful,
approve, approve, approve.

She came down the ladder one rung at a time
and stood on the grass with the phone in both hands.

The pinned post was not hers anymore.
Hers, the parking answers and the supper sign-up,
had slid down the page unpinned,
and the one at the top now wore a stock sunset and cursive,
Concerned4Ashford under it,
AshfordSafetyMom agreeing in capitals inside of four minutes.

Twelve thousand people,
and not one door in the place her key still fit.

"Marisol," Earlene said from the chair. "You've quit hanging."

"Regrouping," she said, and went back up,
and hung the next run so straight Birdie passed it without notes.

"That app say anything new?" Earlene called, a while after.

"Twenty minutes," Marisol said, which was true of something.

She hung lights. She minded the couple's ladder.
She said good night by name to everyone who peeled off down Millrow,
and not one of them could have said anything was wrong,
which was the last thing she still ran, so she ran it.

The lawn thinned toward eleven.
Dinner never came; Birdie's cooler had carried the evening.
Verna commandeered the couple for a ride to the Magnolia Court.
"I don't drive at night," she informed them, boarding.
"That's what engaged people are for."
Then it was down to the three of them and the cords
when it came in the way the rendering had come in, back in August.
One buzz among the many,
then the same buzz on every phone at once,
Birdie's on the tailgate, Earlene's in the cupholder,
hers in her hand, one shudder the whole lawn wide.

It was a photograph of a page.
Gray, close-cropped, shot in somebody's lamplight,
CONDITION SURVEY — MILLROW STRUCTURES across the top,
one line halfway down doing all the work,
RECOMMEND CONDEMNATION,
and at the bottom, in tidy type,
Office of the Facilities Director. C. Sutter.

She spread two fingers on the page looking for ink,
a date, a signature, anything a hand had touched,
and the picture went to gray blocks before it gave her any.

"Who took that," Birdie said. "Whose desk is that."

"Somebody's," Earlene said, in the tone she used for road closures.
"Nobody sent it. Everybody forwarded it."

"That's our House they're talking about," Birdie said.

"It's one page of a paper nobody's seen," Earlene said.
"Watch the town read it anyway."

By half past eleven it was everywhere a screen lived.
The Shed Thread had it.
The group had it, where she could watch and not steer.
Four hundred comments while she watched, his name in half of them,
real people now, people with casserole photos and porch flags,
saying it like a verdict.
By midnight the town she had been stringing lights for
believed it knew exactly whose pen had killed the House,
and it did not want the pen. It wanted the hand.

She had made herself a rule on Delmar's porch, in front of
witnesses.
I will fight your pen with everything I've got.
The man holding it, I leave alone.
The town had applauded the first half.
Tonight it had no use for the second.

And under the anger, hers and the town's both,
sat the question with the cold on it.
He'd told her at table nine, enjoying himself, water always wins.
He'd told her on Delmar's porch he doubted buildings for a living.
She had watched those same hands plane the kneeling step level
for couples he would never meet,
and tie his wife's roses in against the October wind,
and she could not make those hands sign that page.
She could not swear they hadn't, either.
She knew what his whole face did when a laugh got out of him,
and she did not know this one thing.

Birdie shut the tailgate and stood by it, keys quiet in her fist.

"You want company," Birdie said, not especially as a question.

"No," Marisol said. "I want a fact."

"Whose?"

"The only one who has it."

"Then go get it," Birdie said. "I'll take the ladders."

"Leave the lights on," Earlene said, folding her chair.
"No sense the whole hill going dark over this."

Nobody threw the switch.
She walked to her car with half a ceiling burning gold behind her,
swaying where the air moved,
and sat with the door open and the screenshot still up.

She put her thumb on the name at the bottom of the page.
C. Sutter, small type, no ink anywhere near it she could find.
The thumb covered the name entire.
She held it there, not typing, not calling,
the screen's little heat coming up into it,
while up the hill the tower struck midnight,
twelve of them, slow,
coming down through cool air that carried a last thin ribbon
of Delmar's smoke.

She pulled the door shut on the ninth one and started the engine.

Millrow ran dark and decent past the windows,
porch lights out, flags in,
the square's one light blinking to nobody.
She came in the campus gate past the brick,
past where the good asphalt gave out,
onto the back road with her headlights full of fence line.

The service road took her off the pavement,
and the gravel came up under the tires, talking.
One light burned at the end of the row of dark buildings,
low and yellow over the motor-pool bay door,
the only lit thing on the whole campus,
and she drove at it down the middle of the gravel,
fifty yards out and closing.
