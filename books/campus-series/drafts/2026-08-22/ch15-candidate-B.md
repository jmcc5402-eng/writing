Marisol was two rungs from the top of the ladder,
forty feet of string lights paying out over one shoulder,
when the daylight went out of the first Tuesday of October
and nobody on the lawn so much as slowed down.

"Left," Birdie called from the foot of the ladder.
"Your other left.
That swag is hanging like it heard bad news."

"The swag is level."

"The swag is close.
This is for the supper.
The supper gets matched."

The supper was Delores Fountain-Mabry's idea,
spoken over sheet cake at Mt. Nebo and adopted on the spot.
This town does suppers, not meetings.
Put everybody on that porch
and make November look it in the eye.
So the town had come up Millrow after work with ladders,
and by dark the House's lawn was half hoedown,
work lights standing in the grass on yellow legs,
cords taped down where they crossed the walk,
somebody's playlist working through songs
everybody claimed not to know and sang anyway.

Earlene had brought her own lawn chair
for the express purpose of supervising,
set it dead center of the lawn,
and was discharging the office faithfully.

"That end's low," she reported.

"You said the right end was low."

"It was. Now it's the left. This is why I'm here."

"You could zip-tie all this and be home by now," Birdie said.

"Twine unties," Marisol said.
"Come January, somebody has to take it down kind."

"Who taught you that. The hardware store?"

"A Saturday," Marisol said, and left it there,
and tied off two turns and a tail,
no slack left for October wind to work on.
She had held the thorny half of that lesson once,
canes steady in both hands
while a man tied in knots slower than any knot required,
and neither of them had called it teaching.

Verna Poteat had a couple stopped on the front walk,
a big careful man and the woman who was going to marry him,
and was pointing at the dark porch with two fingers.

"Third from the bottom, when your day comes.
Second's too low, fourth is showing off.
And it happens on a porch board or it doesn't happen.
You cannot kneel on sod. Sod won't hold a kneel."

"Which one's third?" the man said.

"You count up from the bottom, sugar. That part's free.
The rest of the history runs you a pie.
And when he goes down, you let him get all the way down
before you answer. A yes too quick spoils the picture."

The House stood dark over its own lit lawn,
front door pulled to for the night,
minded before any of them had gotten there.
The work-light spill reached the east lattice,
where the rose canes rode their ties
and every bud was still shut.
When Marisol's run of lights drifted that direction,
Birdie's voice arrived ahead of the sag.

"Not on the roses. Over. Nothing hangs on that bush."

Marisol moved the run.
She knew whose bush it was.
She had never said so to a soul,
and she did not say so now.

Dinner for the rig-up was out on the four-lane somewhere,
riding in a stranger's back seat, twenty minutes away.
It had been twenty minutes away since eight o'clock.

"Refresh it," Earlene said.

"Refreshing doesn't feed anybody, Earlene."

"It feeds me. Refresh it."

Marisol refreshed it from the top of the ladder.
The little car on the map sat exactly where it had sat
through two whole songs and a change of extension cords,
serene, twenty minutes out,
in no traffic that existed anywhere on this earth.

"Still twenty minutes," she reported.
"He's been twenty minutes away for an hour.
He's not dinner anymore. He's a lighthouse."

"That driver has passed on," Earlene said,
in the tone she used for road closures.
"We are tracking a ghost."

"Maybe he's walking," Birdie said.

"When it comes," Verna said from the walk,
"I am eating mine on principle alone."

The laugh went down the lawn and came back around,
and Marisol stayed up the ladder a minute with the tie done,
looking at the dark face of the House above all that gold,
tall and quiet and out of reach of the lights.

"If you're resting," Earlene called, "rest lower.
My neck is not what it was."

They lit what was hung at nine, to test the run,
and the lawn came up gold underneath,
half the job done and all of it glowing,
and everybody quit working to admire it,
which was the town in one sentence.

She was halfway through typing the interstate towing number
from memory, for a mother in the group stranded
on an off-ramp two hours south,
when the badge went out beside her name.

No sound to it. No flourish.
The little moderator mark had been there
when she started the sentence,
and when she glanced back up the thread it was gone,
and a notice sat where her own pinned post had lived.

*Moderator status for this account (Marisol Pruett)
is pending review.*

It was nine forty-one.

She did the useless things first.
Closed the app, opened it.
The queue was gone. The pins were gone.
The tools she had built years of Saturdays around
were simply not where they lived,
like a kitchen where somebody had moved every drawer overnight.
She finished the towing number anyway and sent it,
and it went out naked, no badge on it,
one voice out of twelve thousand.
The number was still the number.
That was the last thing that worked all night.

The how was not hard to find. It never was.
Enough reports from enough accounts will trip a review
on any platform ever built,
and the reports had come in ranks.
The accounts were shiny, fluent, born in August,
sunsets and retrievers in the profile circles,
every one of them suddenly civic-minded tonight.
And she knew their birthdays,
because she had been standing at the door when they arrived.
August, the drive to Millrow, the line of traffic,
the truck ahead of her failing to park,
four hundred and change sitting in the pending queue,
and her own thumb, steady, being helpful.
Approve. Approve. Approve.
She had held the door open herself.

Her hands went cold around the phone,
and she was still standing in the light spill
getting that swallowed
when every phone on the lawn went off.
Not one at a time.
All of them inside the same half minute,
Earlene's in the cup holder of the lawn chair,
Verna's in her jacket pocket,
his and hers out on the front walk,
one long shudder of other people's pockets.

Verna had hers out and up first,
glasses down her nose,
cropped it, blew it up, ran the brightness to the top.

"It's a page," she said. "Somebody photographed a page."

The lawn closed in around her phone.
Letterhead across the top,
OFFICE OF THE FACILITIES DIRECTOR.
Below it a table of numbers too small to survive the crop,
and one line that had survived just fine,
because some helpful hand had drawn a red box around it
before sending it on.
RECOMMEND CONDEMNATION. HARTWELL HOUSE.
And under that, typed, small, a name.
C. Sutter.
The picture stopped at the name.

"Sutter," Earlene said,
and for the first time in Marisol's acquaintance
the road-report voice didn't hold.

"That's the survey," the big careful man said.
"The one with the tripods on Millrow.
My cousin watched them set up."

"They're saying he signed it Monday," Earlene said, reading.
"They're saying that's why it came quiet."

"Who's saying?" Birdie said.

"Everyone is 'they' tonight."

Verna put her phone down by her side.
"I have watched that man go up ladders nobody sent him up,"
she said, to nobody who had asked her anything.
"The rest of you may do as you please."

The lawn was already somewhere else.
By ten the picture was in the group,
and in The Shed Thread,
and on three porches Marisol could name from where she stood,
and by eleven it had quit being a post
and turned into the place
where every other post was happening.
She watched names she knew arrive under it
and set his name down with a period on it.
People who had passed him a plate at Mt. Nebo.
People who had forwarded the honey-table picture in August
with their own thumbs up.
A couple of Saturdays back, the Blind Mule
had convicted Tunk Ferrell on a voice vote
and bought him a sympathy round in a flower vase,
and it had been warm, and neighborly,
and nobody had shown anybody a fact.
There was no round for Cal Sutter tonight.
Tonight the town had a fact, red-boxed, easy to read,
and the difference between a list and a mob
was nowhere in twelve thousand phones.

She had given him rules on Delmar's porch,
in front of witnesses, and kept every one.
I will fight your pen with everything I've got,
in every room in this county.
The man holding it, I leave alone.
Tonight the whole county had it turned around,
leaving the pen alone and going after the man,
and the worst of it was simple.
She had a thermos lid arriving warm in both hands
with no questions attached.
She had a porch he kept with no work order in it.
And she had one photographed page with a red box on it,
and no way, at eleven o'clock on a Tuesday,
to make those be the same man.

The lawn drained off in twos and threes,
quiet, phones lit, the playlist long dead.
Nobody turned the string lights off.
Half the job done, all of it still glowing,
gold on the grass with nobody under it.

Marisol sat down on the bottom rung of her own ladder
with the picture open.
She put her thumb over the name.
Not the box. The name.
The screen dimmed and she pressed it bright again
and kept her thumb where it was,
over the C and most of the Sutter,
and with the name covered
it was a page about an old house,
and she took her thumb away,
and it wasn't.

"I can run you home," Birdie said. "Truck's loaded."

"I'm not going home."

"No," Birdie said. "I didn't figure."
She took the ladder off Marisol's hands
and asked nothing else.

***

She drove up through campus with no speech ready
and both hands on the wheel.
The buildings went by dark, every one of them,
offices, halls, the tower a blacker shape on the sky,
and by now there was not a phone in the county
without that page on it, and midnight coming on.

Past where the brick ran out and the pavement ended,
the gravel started in under her tires,
honest about her the whole way down.

At the end of the service road one bay stood lit,
a single door's worth of yellow in all that dark,
the only light left burning on the whole campus.
She had seen a smile go once
like a door opening on a lit room and closing again,
and she did not know which man
was standing inside this one.

She came down the last dark stretch of the road,
the lawn's half-hung lights still burning small in her mirror,
the bay light ahead of her, seen, and no closer yet.
