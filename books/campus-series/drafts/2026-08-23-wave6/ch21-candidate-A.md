# Chapter 21 — Under the Home Stands

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate A. Variance card: D2.

---

Homecoming morning put the whole county on Millrow by nine.
Lawn chairs stood three deep from the square to the Hartwell House
gates, ninety thousand more due up the hill by kickoff,
and Birdie Pham had backed the Zinnia Wagon against the House fence,
tailgate down, mum buckets ranked by color like a paint aisle.
Marisol hauled water to them from the House spigot,
two buckets a trip, no spills yet,
the blue dress taking one splash for the team.
A fair portion of the curb watched her make the trips.
She walked accordingly.

"Read it again," Birdie said, jamming stems into a coffee can.
"Slow. I want to enjoy it."

Marisol set the buckets down, dried her hands on her skirt,
and held the phone up like a hymnal.
The text sat where it had landed two nights ago,
at one o'clock in the morning, from her son.

*MOM WHERE DO PEOPLE PARK SATURDAY*

*LOVE YOU BIG*

"One in the morning," Verna Poteat said from her lawn chair,
not looking up from her funnel cake.
"Boy waited up special to ask the parking lady."

"Twelve thousand people ask me where to park," Marisol said.
The joke went soft in her mouth and she let it.
"Turns out one of them is mine."

"You answer him?"

"With a hand-drawn map. Arrows. A star for the good shade."
She pocketed the phone. "He said love you big.
At that hour, that's a sonnet."

"You going up for the game?" Birdie asked.

"Second half. Jar duty first. Somebody has to sit with the money."

"I'll sit with the money," Verna said. "Money and me get along."

"That dress could get a road paved in this county," she added,
because Verna Poteat had never once run a thought through a filter.

"It's got bucket water down one side," Marisol said,
and went back to the spigot for two more.

Sonny Dillard came through the crowd sideways, hat first,
carrying a page in a plastic sleeve flat on both palms,
like a pie he did not trust.

"Miz Pruett." He tipped the sleeve until the sun quit glaring
off it. "You know what I've said across that counter since
before either of us needed reading glasses."

"Good bones, bad wiring."

"Good bones, bad wiring. Buy a fuse at Doss, you hear it free."
He tapped the page through the plastic, one finger, gentle.
"Well. Now it's got a stamp."

The page was typed and short.
At the bottom sat a stamp in oxblood ink,
the office's name run half to blur in its own pad,
and the word above the signature line was not a kind one.
It was the House's wiring, written down and made official,
and Hartwell House stood right there behind the fence
with its porch — the porch, the one where Ashford had been
getting engaged for as long as anybody could prove —
holding its paint on in the morning sun.

"It's true, Sonny," Marisol said. "That's the thing.
It's the one true thing they've got."

"Don't I know it. I've been true about it for years."

Birdie put the jar on the tailgate before anybody asked.
She pulled the zinnias out of it, dropped them in a bucket,
wiped the big pickle jar out with her shirttail,
and set it down empty by the mums, dead center.

Verna got up out of her lawn chair,
which regulars on Millrow knew to read as historic,
and dropped a folded bill into the bottom of the glass.
"Porch and jar," she said. "No committee. No logins.
You put money in, you watch the house get fixed.
Trustees walk that porch the first Tuesday of November.
Let them walk past a full jar."

Marisol pulled the butcher-paper roll to the tailgate edge,
tore off a yard, and wrote across the top in stem marker,
HARTWELL HOUSE — WHAT YOUR PLEDGE BUYS, FIRST TO LAST,
and under it, first line, biggest, REWIRING.
She taped Sonny's sleeve above the jar, stamp facing out,
where nobody walking by could miss it.

"You're leading with the bad part," Birdie said.

"I'm leading with the true part."

"What do I owe the jar?" Sonny asked, going for his billfold.

"Sonny, it's your stamp. You've been paying in for years."

"Then I'll pay like everybody," he said, and did.

She took the photo herself, jar and list and stamped page,
and posted it to the group she no longer ran,
under her own name, one voice out of twelve thousand.
*The wiring at the House is bad. That part is true, and now
it's stamped. So rewiring goes first. The jar's on the Zinnia
Wagon's tailgate till the mums run out. Come by.*

Ray had come up the block from Delmar's,
sauce still on his apron, to see about the crowd.

"You pledging, Ray?" Birdie asked.

"Put me down for Mondays. We're closed Mondays."

The band came past, brass first, and the curb stood up for it.

Eli Fountain arrived at the tailgate walking like a man
who had rehearsed walking.

"How much for the mums," he said.

"Which bucket?"

"All of them."

Birdie set down her shears. "Eli. There's nineteen buckets."

"For the end zones," Eli said.
His phone was already hovering over the card reader
taped to the Wagon's rail.
"It's a." He looked at the mums. "Color program."

Birdie started laughing on *program* and did not stop.
She laughed while the payment went through,
laughed while she helped him load,
laughed, by Verna's estimate, a block and a half of parade,
start to finish.
"The peach man never bought a bucket," she managed,
wiping her eyes.

"I'll return the buckets," Eli said, arms full, petals crowding
his jaw. "Tuesday. Wednesday. Whenever suits."
He carried all nineteen to his cart two at a time,
bound for two end zones that had never once in the history
of football asked for flowers.

Marisol half-saw it with the tape roll still on her wrist,
phone in the other hand, checking on the post.
Between a picture of the jar and somebody's video of the band,
three words in capitals went by, CONDEMNATION — FINAL,
and then a float horn went off, and the crowd surged for thrown
candy, and a kid in team colors ran into her knees
and had to be caught and aimed back at his mother.
By the time she got the phone up again it was not there.
Not anywhere. Mums all the way down.
She put the phone in her pocket with the marker
and kept the cold of it to herself.

By the second quarter the mums were gone to the end zones,
the jar had grown too heavy to carry one-armed,
and Marisol took it up the hill toward the parents' tent
through a concourse crowd that moved like poured concrete.

The gate was chain-link, under the ramp,
open exactly one person wide, and Cal Sutter held it.

"Shortcut," he said, to the air beside her head,
which was how strangers talked.

She went through. He latched it behind them,
and the concourse roar changed shape,
went ribbed and slatted, sifting down with the light.
Under the home stands the sun came through the bleacher gaps
in long bars across concrete and painted steel,
and overhead sat ninety thousand people she could not see.
When all of them stood up at once, the steel took a breath.
She felt it arrive through the soles of her shoes.

"Does it always do that?" she asked. Quietly. No name.

"Only when they're happy."
He moved ahead of her down the bars of light,
and the stripes crossed his shoulders one at a time,
and she was in no rush about the view.

He put his palm flat on a cross-brace as they passed.
Not showing off. Checking.
"That one's mine," he said. "The rail on that stair too.
Every bolt from here to the ramp has had my hand on it twice."
He said it upward, at the steel.
"Twenty years of Saturdays up there.
It's held every one of them over everybody's head."

She watched the hand on the brace longer than she listened,
and saw no reason to apologize to herself for it.

"And you come down here during," she said. "On purpose."

"Somebody has to know what it sounds like when it's fine."

"What does it need?" She tipped her head at all of it.

"Paint. Patience. Somebody who minds."

The touchdown arrived as weather.
The roar came down through the steel and into it,
through her shoes, off the braces, all of it at once,
and it pushed her a step forward and pushed him the same step,
and then the pickle jar was the only thing between them,
glass and bills and Sonny's taped page,
her arms around it, his hand coming up against the side of it
to steady the whole arrangement, fingers an inch from her wrist.
Nobody upstairs quit roaring.
Neither of them moved again, in either direction.

Tires squeaked on concrete off toward the ramp,
and DeShawn's voice came ahead of the cart.
"Boss? You under here? West-side ice machine's down again."

Cal took his hand back off the glass,
and the jar's whole weight returned to her arms.

"On my way," he called, in a voice with nothing in it.
Then, quiet, to the air beside her:
"Far gate puts you out at the tents."

They came out of the striped dark into sunlight
with a stranger's distance between them and kept it,
past the cart, past DeShawn, past everybody,
her arms full of jar, his hands empty,
both of them good at it by now.

Halftime came down the hill in pieces, drumline first.
At the parents' tent the jar got a cleared corner of the drinks
table, Sonny's page taped to the glass,
too full already to jingle when it was set down.
Verna had produced a second jar from the motel supply room
and was peeling the label off with a thumbnail.

"Before the half," Verna said, chin at the full one.
"Your wiring post did that. Leading with the bad part.
Who raised you."

"It wasn't the post," Marisol said. "It was the stamp."

She got her phone out to take the picture,
full jar and empty jar side by side,
and caught her thumb scrolling first,
down through mums and band video and mums again,
past the spot in the morning's feed where the three cold words
had sat.
Nothing sat there now. Mums all the way down.
She was still there, thumb flat on the glass,
when Verna pushed the empty jar into her free hand.
