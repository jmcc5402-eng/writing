# Chapter 23 — Millrow, After

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate A. Variance card: D6.

---

Millrow was a short street.
It had been a short street since the founding,
and in all that time nobody had ever minded,
until tonight, when Marisol discovered that a short street
was a shortage, a scandal, a municipal failure of planning.

Cal walked her down the middle of it, slow,
his one good jacket hung over her shoulders,
and behind them Hartwell House stood lit to the eaves,
music still spilling off the porch,
half the town still on it, and not one soul up there
pretending not to watch them go.
Earlene waved with both arms, like ground crew.

"They're going to talk about us," Marisol said.

"They started at the second verse," Cal said.
"Tunk had his phone out sideways. That's his serious mode."

"And you're all right with that," she said.

"I asked you in front of the whole porch, didn't I."

"In front of Earlene," said Marisol.

"In front of Earlene," Cal agreed.
"No place in the county more public than that."

He had crossed that porch in front of everybody
and asked her for the last dance,
and she had handed him the smile she'd been holding back
since August, and the town had made a sound
she expected to hear in her good dreams
for the rest of her life.

"Did you plan it?" she said.

"Plan is a strong word.
I stood at the rail about an hour first,
getting the porch's opinion."

"And what was the porch's opinion?"

"Quarter-sawn oak," Cal said. "Those boards.
You quarter-saw a board and it won't cup on you.
Won't twist. Holds a finish through anything.
A hundred people stomping on her in dress shoes all night,
and she never gave up one creak.
That's wood telling you it can be trusted."

"Cal Sutter," said Marisol,
"I have never heard anything more romantic in my life."
The terrible part, the truly incurable part,
was that it was the plain truth, all the way down.

He slowed.
Then he slowed further,
to a pace that could no longer honestly be called walking,
and he started a sentence twice and let it go both times,
and she did not help him,
because a man deserves to lose a race fairly.
His nerve ran out at the corner.
Millrow ran out maybe ten steps later.
It was that close.

Her street was darker, two porch lights and a moon,
and her gate stood where it always stood,
under the live oak, where the oak kept its own private midnight.

At the gate he stopped her with nothing.
No hand, no word. Just stopped.
Then he took the cap off his head.
Not pushed back, not resettled. Off,
held against his chest,
straight out of movies made before either of them was born,
the gray at his temples catching what little light
got through the branches,
and if any other man alive had tried it on her
she'd have laughed him into the next county.
She laughed anyway. So did he.
They were laughing before their foreheads even touched,
and then the kiss arrived, slow and sure,
the kiss of a man who had done his worrying
weeks ago in the dark and come out the far side certain.
He had given her his jacket and stood there in shirtsleeves,
and between one thing and another she found time to be furious
that shirtsleeves like those had spent a whole summer
hidden inside a green polo.

Then nobody moved.
He stood holding his cap. She stood holding her keys.
The music from Hartwell was two streets gone,
small as a radio in another room,
and the porch light hummed,
and the oak went on with its midnight,
and still neither of them moved,
long enough that a truck came up Millrow behind them,
slowed — somebody getting their look — and rolled on,
and even then they let the quiet sit a while longer,
because it was theirs, and it was good,
and nobody was coming to take it.

"Marisol," Cal said finally, quiet, like the start of a speech.

"Don't you dare say goodnight," said Marisol.

"Wasn't going to," Cal said, and put his cap back on,
which turned out to mean the opposite of leaving.

She opened the gate. She went up the walk,
and he came after her, and they did not touch once
the whole way to the door,
which was somehow the most indecent part.
The key found the lock on the second try.
The porch light stayed on.
The door shut behind them,
and in the hall his one good jacket slid from her shoulders
and landed on the floor.

---

Sunday morning she was downstairs by nine,
sunlight all over the kitchen,
two mugs in the sink where one usually sat.
She took her keys off the hook beside the calendar,
June's page, the one date circled,
and locked the back door behind the both of them.

The Checkerboard on a Sunday ran church clothes on one side
and the Liars' Table on the other,
and when the bell over the door announced them,
the whole room performed not-looking so badly
that Verna laughed out loud from the pass.

"Well," said Verna, arriving with coffee nobody had ordered.
"Look what the dance dragged in."

"Morning, Verna," said Cal, and took his cap off at the table.

"It surely is," said Verna, and went away beaming.

Marisol's phone had been buzzing since the square.
She had been ignoring it out of good manners,
and she was done being polite about it now.
The parents' group. Twelve thousand members,
and by the look of things
most of them had been up since dawn.

Somebody had a photo of the last dance.
Not a bad one, either. The porch lights, her dress,
a tall man holding her like he'd been issued instructions,
his face turned just far enough out of the light
that Ashford's finest investigative minds had been left
to work with a jawline and one good jacket.

Nobody in the group had any doubts about the woman.
The woman was Marisol.
The woman had answered nine hundred parking questions;
they would have known the back of her head in worse light.
The man was the open seat,
and the group had been holding hearings on him since dawn.

"Oh no," Marisol said, scrolling. "Oh, no. Cal."

"Read it," Cal said.
He wrapped both hands around his mug and settled back,
a man at a hearing that could not touch him.

So she read it. All of it, out loud,
in the voice she used for board meetings,
fully prepared to die in the booth and be buried in it.

"Leading theory," she said. "The new strength coach."

"Strong program," said Cal.

"You are not enjoying this," said Marisol.

"I'm having the best morning of my life," said Cal.

"Second theory, and I quote.
The country singer. In town for somebody special."

"Flattering," said Cal.

"Third theory." She had to set the phone down
and pick it back up.
"'The tall one from facilities. Asking for a friend.'"

Marisol put the phone flat on the table
and looked across at Earlene, two tables over.

"You're in that group, Earlene.
You could have ended this at dawn."

"Why on earth would I," said Earlene.
"That group hasn't had a morning like this
since the retainer."

The Liars' Table had gone quiet in a listening formation.

"I had money on the strength coach," Tick Moran said.

"Asking for a friend," Marisol said, to the booth,
to the counter, to the room at large.
"As a concerned citizen of this group,
no badge, no title, a civilian these days, purely,
let me offer that friend some guidance.
The tall one from facilities is spoken for.
As of last night. In front of witnesses.
On quarter-sawn oak."

And Cal smiled.

Not the quick one.
Not the flicker she'd been living on since August,
there and gone before you could swear to it.
The whole thing. The door onto the lit room,
opened all the way and left standing open,
in front of Verna, in front of the church side,
in front of the entire seated senate of the Liars' Table,
every one of whom saw it land and would testify.
Marisol, forty-three years old and a civilian,
felt her breath snag on it.

"There it is," said Earlene, not even pretending.

Cal set down his mug.

"Question for the group," he said,
level, carrying exactly as far as he meant it to.
"Would her friend like my number?"

The Liars' Table looked over. All of it, at once.
In Ashford that amounts to a press release.

Verna was fighting the church rush three tables deep,
so when Marisol flagged her down it took a minute,
and while it took, Marisol slid out of the booth,
lifted the pot off the warmer,
and worked the counter row end to end, refilling as she went.

"You're an angel," said Birdie Pham,
at the register with an armload of sunflowers.

"I'm between the pot and the door," said Marisol.
"It's geography."

Then she sat back down and did the thing
she had walked in there to do.

"Verna," she said. "The buttermilk. A whole one.
One fork, and the fork's not mine."

Pie rates at the Checkerboard were set by Verna
and reviewed by no one.
Verna named a figure, invented on the spot,
and Marisol laid the money by the sugar caddy, plus some.

"Weeks ago," she announced, to the senate,
"on Delmar's porch, I lost a bet to this man,
fair and square,
and I have owed him one buttermilk pie ever since.
A debt like that keeps."
She turned the tin to face him.
"Paid in full. I wanted witnesses. You'll do."

"What was the bet?" asked Tunk Ferrell.

"That," said Marisol, "stays on Delmar's porch."

Cal picked up the fork.
She had watched those hands fix half a campus,
and it turned out they addressed a buttermilk pie
with the same short, certain economy, nothing wasted,
and she watched the whole operation with open interest
and zero shame.

"Tastes like victory?" asked Peanut Kyzer, hopeful.

"Tastes like buttermilk," said Cal, and kept going.

Her phone went off once more against the table.
Not the group this time. Kat.
One line, warm as anything, margaritas in it,
the word immediately in it,
and more exclamation points than the sentence could seat.
Marisol turned the phone face down.
Kat deserved the whole story, and a pitcher,
and a booth with nobody else in it. Later.

"Sundays now," said Verna, going by with the coffee pot.
"I'll have one waiting. The rate depends on my mood."

When the pie was down to the tin,
Cal put his cap back on and stood and waited for her,
and when she rose,
his hand settled at the small of her back
and he walked her out with it,
past the church side in its Sunday best,
past Tunk Ferrell and the sideways phone,
past Earlene saluting with her coffee cup,
past Peanut Kyzer, who applauded,
past every last grinning face in the Checkerboard,
and out the door into the sun on the square.

The tower rang eleven.
