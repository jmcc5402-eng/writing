## What you are writing

Chapter 22, Cal POV, third person, past tense, 1,800–2,300 words.
THE HOMECOMING DANCE AT HARTWELL HOUSE — the same Saturday as the
parade and the game, now full dark, late October. **Dialogue:
≥15%, target ~25%, named speakers ≥80%.**

Context you need: Cal and Marisol kissed weeks ago and are secret
allies; in public they are strangers by agreement — the county
wrongly believes Cal signed a condemnation recommendation against
this very House (he didn't; only the two of them and the reader
know). The dance is the House's own tradition, generations deep,
and this year everyone quietly fears it is the last one. Marisol
has NEVER ONCE smiled directly at Cal — every smile since the day
they met has gone past his ear, and Cal has kept that count for
months. Tonight that ends.

## The law (frozen beats — each an EVENT someone causes)

1. **The dance goes up the way it has for generations.** String
   lights along the rail, a fiddle band on the steps, cake tables
   under the live oaks — and THIS year, for the dance, **the
   House's front door stands open after dark for the first time
   all season**, the hall lit behind it (spend this gently; the
   reader has watched that door stay shut at night all book — one
   clean image, no essay).
2. **The careful goodbyes.** More photographs get taken than any
   one night needs; nobody names the thing circling the House —
   it is just there, like rain smelled through an open door
   (write your own weather; do not import that phrase). Verna
   Poteat stands a long time at the third step of the hall
   staircase with her hand on the rail, and says nothing about
   it. (The third step is the town's kneeling step — a century of
   proposals. She does not kneel, explain, or prophesy.)
3. **Cal is furniture along the rail** — the man the county
   convicted, at the House the county loves, both of them strung
   with lights. He came anyway (staying home would have been a
   sentence said out loud). Let a town moat form and let one or
   two chorus voices cross it.
4. **The crossing and the ask.** Toward the last set, Cal crosses
   the porch in front of the entire town and asks Marisol for the
   last dance. Secrecy dies at the precise moment he stops
   wanting it — his choice, deliberate, unforced by any slip.
   The fiddler, who has waited years to be useful in exactly this
   way, slows the tune without being asked.
5. **THE SMILE — the chapter's crown beat.** At the ask, her
   smile comes STRAIGHT ON, dead level, aimed at him alone inside
   the public frame — the first smile she has ever given him
   directly, and he has kept the count long enough to know it to
   the day. Land it in his POV: the man who kept the ledger
   watches it close. Cite nothing; land everything. Private
   inside public: the town sees a woman smile at a man; Cal
   receives the only smile that has ever come all the way to him.
6. **One dance, public, correct.** A hand at a waist, a hand in a
   hand, nothing the town couldn't photograph — and it does
   photograph it (phones up, warm, zero dread language; what the
   photos become is another chapter's business). The porch boards
   give and hold, give and hold, under every couple Ashford has.
