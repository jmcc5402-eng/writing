# Chapter 24 — As If It Were the Verdict

POV: Cal, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate A. Variance card: D1.

---

Monday came into the shop by ear, the same as every Monday.
By seven Cal had the county pickup up on the lift,
the compressor cycling behind the parts cage,
Ronnie's impact gun arguing in short bursts out of bay three,
DeShawn whistling something with no particular tune in mind,
Ratchet stretched out by the tire rack with her feet twitching,
running something down in her sleep.
He could have called the hour blind from the office
with the door shut.

The phones started a little after eight.
Not one phone. All of them,
a buzz on the bench, then two together, then a patter of them,
first fat drops on a tin roof.
Then the whistling stopped.
That was the first report Cal got all day, and it was accurate.
The gun quit mid-burst.
The compressor kicked off on its own schedule
and nothing rose up behind it to fill the room.

DeShawn came across the floor holding his phone out flat,
carrying it more than showing it.
"You'll want to see it standing still," he said.
"Show me."

It was the Hartwell House report.
The cover sheet, the two summary pages,
and a strip cut from the last page.
It was not the old screenshot going around again.
The old one had been lazy, a phone photo of a phone.
This was scanned clean and cropped straight. New.
On the copy locked in the drawer ten feet from where Cal stood,
DRAFT — UNSIGNED ran corner to corner in gray, big as weather.
Cropped to a band across the middle of the last page,
that gray read as scanner shadow and nothing else.
The signature block sat inside the band.
The line, and under the line his own name, typed,
CAL SUTTER, DIRECTOR OF FACILITIES,
and the crop ended half an inch below it,
close enough that at phone size the type and the line
and the shadow read as one finished thing.
Signed, the county would say. Executed.
Whoever cut it had cut it gently enough to look like an accident.

Cal took the drawer key off his ring and worked the lock
in front of nobody, because the whole crew had suddenly
found work to look at.
Both envelopes. The gray running corner to corner on every page.
No ink anywhere in that drawer.
No ink had ever been anywhere in that drawer.
He locked it again and put the key back on the ring.

"That's not the page," Ronnie said from bay three.
"That's a piece of the page."
"It's the piece that travels," Cal said.
"Hartwell's the one with the wraparound porch," Travis said.

Somebody had the bench radio on 1340 by eleven,
and Tick Moran had the county down to four words by noon.
*Facilities condemns Hartwell House.*
He said them at the top of every half hour,
and by one o'clock the four words had stopped needing him.

The crew took lunch on the tailgates the way they always did,
and Cal could hear the difference from inside the office.
Twelve men eating is a noisy thing.
There is ribbing, and lids, and somebody's cousin's truck,
and DeShawn holding court about a catfish
that gets bigger every telling.
Monday it was lids and wax paper and not much else.
Ronnie reached in through the office door,
turned the radio off without asking,
and went back out to his sandwich.
Nobody turned it back on.

Cal set the toe on the county pickup to spec.
He torqued the pattern in a star, dropped the truck,
road-tested it out past the water tower and back,
and changed a cabin filter nobody had paid for
because it was black and he was in there.
He initialed the work ticket,
which was all the writing he did that day.
His second put the afternoon's list in front of him at noon
and did not put anything else in front of him,
and he was glad of her.

The photo reached the shop by two.
Ronnie brought it over without a word, which was the kindest
thing anybody did all day.
Saturday night, the dance.
Marisol with her face turned up at him under the lights,
giving him the first smile she had ever handed him in public,
in front of the whole town, on purpose.
Two days old.
Next to it, the crop.
The story running under both of them, everywhere by paraphrase
before suppertime, went like this: he had signed it weeks ago,
and she had known it when she smiled; the smile was the flip;
and the crew's winter was what the smile would cost.

There was not a true word in it end to end.
He did not wonder a single thing about her.
That was not where it hurt.
Where it hurt was the caption itself.
Saturday she had stood up in front of Ashford
and given him the realest thing she had,
and by Monday afternoon the county had it filed under strategy.

The shop kept its opinions in its hands the rest of the day.
Nobody said her name and nobody said his,
and the gun in bay three ran longer bursts
than the work called for.

A gasket set was waiting at the parts house,
and Cal walked for it, four blocks each way,
because the shop was loud in the wrong key.
The town told him what it believed without one person
saying a word to him.
A conversation on the hardware store steps went down
to half volume as he passed and came back up behind him,
a door of quiet that opened for him and closed after.
Somebody's window radio had 1340 going.
A woman he had known twenty years said hey, Cal,
from across the street, same as any day,
and the hey landed wrong by half a note,
pitched the same as it would be at a funeral home.
He said hey back and kept his pace even,
and listened to his own boots do it.
On the way back the carillon struck five over the campus roofs,
and under the last note of it, out the propped door
of the Blind Mule, the other bell rang.

He stopped on the threshold.
He had never once been through that door
and he did not go through it now.
He stood at the edge of the light with a gasket set
under his arm and listened to the inside of the bar happen.
Glass noise. Chair legs. Peanut Kyzer over the top of it all.
"All right, all right. Who's it on?"
"Round for the winter work," somebody called back.
"Sutter's pen finally found some paper."
The bell rang twice more and the room came up under it
in one glad noise, thirty voices with no space between them.

His own name, buying a round he never poured.
The bell got him under the sternum, one clean strike,
same place the carillon landed at five.
Nobody looked at the door.
He was a shape against the daylight, and the round
was already landing.

He had one foot turned to go when Verna Poteat came up the walk
from the direction of the Magnolia Court,
a bushel basket riding her hip.
She did not see him, or she did not spend it.
She set the basket down square in the Mule's doorway,
where every boot leaving would have to reckon with it,
and addressed the bar entire.

"Peanut Kyzer, I heard that bell from my porch,
and I am not here for the round."
"Verna," Peanut said, in the voice of a man
who had lost to her before.
"I have known Cal Sutter since he alphabetized
the church pantry. Sixteen years old.
Applesauce to yams, A to Z, with a little card up front
so the rest of us could keep it that way.
We did not keep it that way.
He came back every week and fixed it and never once
named the backsliders, and half this room is drinking
on pantry corn to this day."
Nobody in the Mule said anything to that.
"A man who files beans right when nobody's watching
does not sign a lie when everybody is.
Lies don't hold up a porch.
Hartwell's porch has held up half the men in this room,
one Saturday or another,
and it will be holding up better men than you
when that bell has gone for scrap."

She required no reply and collected none.
The pears in the basket were for the pantry,
same as every October,
and she stood beside them with her arms crossed,
a second door on the Mule, narrower than the first
and harder to get through.
Cal walked his four blocks back lighter than he had
walked them out, and nobody at the Mule ever knew
he had been in the door.

The crew drifted off at six, engines starting one at a time
out in the lot, each one a name if you knew the lot,
Travis's diesel last because Travis's diesel was always last.
His second put the cage key on his desk and went.
Rex Boyd came at dusk, when the lot was empty.
Cal heard the car before he saw it,
or rather he heard how little of it there was to hear,
tires on gravel and hardly any engine under them at all.
Ratchet came up off the floor and stood. She did not bark.
She just stood, which from Ratchet was a paragraph.

"Cal," Boyd said.
"Boyd."
"Terrible day." He said it at the volume he kept
for funerals and closings, which was the one volume he owned.
"Leaks. No respect for a man's process."
Cal went on racking the day's sockets, deep to shallow,
and Boyd stood where the clean light was,
and his boots stayed clean.
"Here is where it stands. Friday.
The Friday we discussed; I moved it once,
and I will not be moving it again.
You sign the genuine article, and the county finds out
it was right all along. Nothing to correct.
Everybody's photograph goes back to being a photograph."
"And if the drawer stays locked," Cal said.
"Then the winter letter firms up."
Boyd let the compressor cycle once before he went on.
"You have seen it. It still says pending.
Pending can firm either direction, Cal.
Twelve names on that list. Ronnie. Travis. DeShawn.
I don't know the rest of them, but the letter does."
He straightened a socket Cal had already straightened.
"Friday, then."
He let himself out at the same volume he had come in.

Cal fed Ratchet, and for a while the shop was only that,
kibble against a steel bowl, the tick of the lift cooling,
the day's heat coming off the block walls in silence.
The phone went once in his shirt pocket. Marisol.

*Best day the jar has ever had. Every dollar of it
came in mad at you. I'd give you that smile again tonight,
bigger, and let them photograph it. Tuesday holds.*

He typed with grease still in the seams of his thumbs.

*Nothing ever left this shop with ink on it.
Drawings are dry and riding where they ride.
Friday is his problem, not mine. Tuesday holds.*

He read hers twice more and put the phone away.

He shut down the bays, left the one light on over bay three
for the morning, and ran the day's numbers once, flat,
because flat was the only way they would run.
The crop said signed.
The jar filled up because the crop said signed,
angry money, the best day it had ever had,
and it was two days old.
The jar was paying for the fight to keep Hartwell standing,
which was the fight against the page
with his own name typed under the line.
If he stood in the square tomorrow and told the truth —
the drawer, the watermark, no ink anywhere, ever —
it would land as the photograph's proof,
the smile doing exactly what the caption said it had done.

Sign by Friday, and the twelve worked the winter,
and the House came down.
Leave the drawer locked, and the letter firmed the other way.
Four days to Friday, eight to the first Tuesday of November,
and the numbers came out the same every way he set them up.
The lie was funding her fight against him,
and any truth he told now would sound like exactly
what the photograph said.
