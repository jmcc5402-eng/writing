# Chapter 22 — The Porch Holds

POV: Cal, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate A. Variance card: D4.

---

By full dark, Hartwell House had put on every light it owned.

The string lights went up same as every October anyone present
could remember, looped along the porch rail end to end
and doubled in the tall black windows.
The fiddle band took the front steps,
four chairs and a stand-up bass angled to clear the handrail.
The cake tables went under the live oaks
on the same folding legs and the same pressed cloths as always,
coffee urns steaming against the first honest cold of the season.
And the front door stood open to the night,
the hall lit warm behind it all the way back to the stairs.
It had been shut at dusk every evening since August.
Nobody made a speech about it.
People coming up the walk just slowed a step
at the sight of the hall light lying down the boards,
and wiped their feet longer than any feet needed.

Half the county had come straight from the stadium,
hoarse and happy, team colors showing under good clothes,
and by eight the lawn was full and the porch was fuller.
Ratchet, the shop dog, had taken up her post
under the cake table before the band tuned,
and held it all night,
and nobody had the heart to enforce a single rule on her.

Cal came in his one good jacket.
Dark wool, older than most of the marriages present,
and it still fit across the shoulders, snug where it mattered.
He had worn it to weddings and to one funeral,
and he wore it now at the end of the rail
with a cup of punch he wasn't drinking.
The keys to the House rode in his shirt pocket underneath,
the whole ring, same as every day,
and the door those keys were cut for
stood open on its own forty feet away.
He thought that was fine.
He kept thinking it was fine at regular intervals.

Coming at all had been the choice.
A man who stays home the night the whole county gathers
has said something out loud,
and Cal had decided not to say it.
So he stood where he could be seen,
strung with the same lights as the House —
the man the county had convicted without a trial,
propped against the rail of the House the county loved —
and the crowd did what it had done for weeks,
which was thin around him without anybody visibly walking away.
Nobody was rude. Rude would have been easier to answer.
Inside ten minutes he had six feet of painted floor to himself
in a crowd that had every other inch spoken for.

Earlene Tatum crossed it with two plates of cake.

"You own a jacket," she said, handing one over.
"I'd heard it rumored. I like to verify."

"It comes out for occasions."

"Well, it picked a good one."
She settled in beside him at the rail, a public position,
and took it up with her whole posture.
"Delores made the sheet. Birdie piped the flowers on it.
Both of them are watching who eats it, so eat it visible."

Cal ate it visible. "Door's open," he said.

"I saw."

"First night since August."

"I saw that too."
Earlene ran her thumb around the rim of her cup.
"Verna aired the hall linens two days for it.
You'd think the governor was coming."
She watched the band a while. "Eat your cake, Cal."

Ray stopped at Cal's stretch of rail
on his way to the punch bowl
and considered the lights overhead.
"Straighter than last year," he said, and moved on.

More photographs got taken than any one night could use.
The band got photographed,
the cake got photographed whole and then cut,
and the door got photographed from the porch, from the walk,
and from halfway down the lawn,
people backing farther and farther out
to fit the entire House in one frame.
Whole families lined up on the steps by generation,
oldest in the middle, and held still,
and then held still again
for the aunt whose phone had been aimed the wrong way.

"Farther," Delores Fountain-Mabry directed, from the walk.
"I want the door in it. All of the door."

"It's a door," said Odell Fountain.

"Take the picture, Odell."

Nobody said why this year needed so many.
The reason stood out at the cold edge of the lawn all evening
and did not come up the steps.

Verna Poteat went inside around ten,
when the carillon came faint across campus under the fiddle,
and stood at the third step of the hall staircase
with one hand on the rail.
The third step was the kneeling step.
A century of Ashford proposals had started on it,
and a fair number of the faces in the couple photographs
going up that wall
had said yes looking down at somebody kneeling there.
Verna did not kneel, and did not explain, and did not move.
She stood through two whole tunes with her hand on the rail.
When she came back out she took a plate
and told Delores the cake was too sweet, same as every year.

Then a third of the rail went dark all at once,
corner post to steps,
and Cal came off the rail with his keys already out,
because a dead circuit was the one conversation
he was still welcome in.
He didn't get six steps.
Peanut Kyzer was down on one knee at the corner post
with a bar towel over his shoulder,
running the cords through his hands,
and by the time Cal cleared the cake table
Peanut had found the doubled-up outlet,
split the strings across two,
and taped the run flat to the post
with nothing left for a heel to catch.
The lights came back before the fiddler finished the verse.

"Two strings on one plug," Peanut said, standing.
"Same corner every year.
The Mule runs twice this footage every December,
inside and out.
You split the load
or you spend your whole night on your knees out here."

"I had it," Cal said.

"You had the walk over. I had the towel."

"Where'd you learn load?"

"Christmas," Peanut said.

Cal laughed, one short honest note,
the first he'd spent all night,
and Peanut nodded like a man accepting payment
and went back to the punch bowl.
Cal stood there holding keys
to a house whose door was wide open,
beside a repair that hadn't needed him.
There was still a paper across town
that wanted his name by Friday.
For one breath he felt it,
out past the reach of the string lights.
Then the fiddle picked the pace back up, and he let it go.

Marisol worked the porch and the lawn all evening
and came nowhere near him, which was the agreement,
and the agreement held, same as it had held for weeks.
She had her hair down for once,
dark and loose to her shoulders,
and a good dress, deep green,
that made it look like somebody had hung the lights
with her in mind.
He watched her run a plate out to the band between tunes,
pin a corsage back together with a cake pick,
get thanked for both, and wave both thanks off.
Twice her orbit carried her within ten feet of him,
and twice she smiled,
and both times the smile went out just past his shoulder
to some point on the dark lawn behind him,
polite as a stranger, exact as a surveyor.
He knew that address.
Every smile she had sent his direction since the day they met
had been delivered to it, every single one,
and he had never once meant to keep the count,
and he had kept it anyway.

A little before eleven
the fiddler stood and called the last set.

All night the town had been telling the House goodbye
without saying the word.
All season the House had kept its door shut after dark,
and tonight of all years it stood open to the whole county.
Cal looked at that a long moment.
He wasn't caught, and he wasn't careless, and nothing slipped.
He had simply, somewhere between the cake and the last set,
stopped wanting the secret more than he wanted the dance.

He set his cup on the rail and crossed the porch.

It was thirty feet, and the town made it longer.
Talk shut off in rings as he came.
Earlene set her cup down where she stood.
Aisha Cole reached out and turned Coach Merritt,
still in his game-day windbreaker with his voice about gone,
around by the sleeve.

"Coach. You want to see this."

"See what?" said Coach Merritt, and then saw.

Marisol stood by the cake table
with a stack of used plates in her hands
and watched him come the whole way.

"Cal Sutter," she said.
The plates went down on the cloth with a click.
"In front of everybody."

"In front of everybody," Cal said.
"I'd like the last dance, if you'll have me."

Up on the steps the fiddler,
who had spent his whole working life
waiting to be useful in exactly this way,
took the tune down slow without being asked,
easing it back to a speed two people could begin at,
and the bass came down after him.

And Marisol smiled at him.

Straight on. Dead level.
Not past his shoulder, not out to the lawn,
not to the crowd going quiet around them.
It came the whole distance and stopped where he stood,
aimed at him alone in the middle of everybody,
and Cal, who knew better than any man alive
exactly how long that had taken,
stood in his good jacket and took all of it.
Something under his ribs missed its footing
and came down surer than before.
The town saw a woman smile at a man.
The man knew what had actually arrived.

"I thought you'd never ask," she said,
clear enough to carry,
and gave him her hand.

His hand at her waist, her hand in his,
nothing the town couldn't photograph,
and the town photographed it.
Phones went up all along the rail, warm and bright,
Earlene shooting one-handed
with her cake plate still level,
Delores narrating the framing to Odell
in a whisper you could hear at the punch bowl,
Tick Moran getting three for the station.
Not one soul on that porch looked away,
and not one looked sorry.

"You know what you just did," Marisol said, near his ear.

"I do," Cal said. "Been meaning to for a while."

"How long a while?"

"Since a Saturday in August."

"They'll talk all winter," she said.

"Then we'll give them the whole tune to get it right."

Couples came onto the boards
until the porch carried every pair Ashford had.
Eli Fountain, still saving his words from the parade,
steered Birdie Pham through a slow turn
and gave Cal one nod as they passed.
She was light on the boards and Cal was steady on them,
and between the two of them
it came out to one pretty fair dancer.
The fiddler took the tune around once more, slower yet,
and the whole porch moved with it,
boards giving under every couple and rising again,
giving and rising, a hundred Octobers deep in the grain.

The music went out through the open front door,
down the lit hall and up the staircase,
past the wall of everyone who had ever said yes in that house,
and under all of it the porch rose and settled,
rose and settled, carrying the whole town at once.
It held.
