# Chapter 21 — Under the Home Stands

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate C. Variance card: D1.

---

Homecoming reached her before the daylight did, and it came by ear.
The tower was going long past any hour count,
on and on into the early blue,
and under the bells, blocks off, a brass section
ran the same eight bars until everybody in it landed together.
Somewhere a public-address voice said *test, test*
to an empty stadium, gently, twice,
like a man waking a baby he liked.

She poured coffee and read the text again.
It had come in at one in the morning, two nights back,
lighting up the dark hall of Hartwell House
at the single worst-timed moment of her adult life,
and she had been rereading it ever since with her whole heart.

MOM WHERE DO MOMS PARK SATURDAY

LOVE YOU BIG. THIS IS URGENT

Twelve thousand parents in the Grapevine,
and she had answered the parking question for every one of them,
some twice, some in what was by now practically song.
Her own child had sat on his through an entire day
and then fired it off at one a.m. like a flare over open water.
She'd answered inside a minute, because she was a professional,
and signed off LOVE YOU BIGGER, in caps, so it would register.

By nine, Millrow was a sound with a street attached.
The band came off the brick in pieces, horns first,
then the flat crack of snares turning a corner,
then all of it at once as the front rank swung into view.
Candy hit the pavement in handfuls ahead of the first float
and the curb kids went for it like gulls,
and every third porch had the radio up,
Tick Moran on WDSS calling the parade like a horse race.
"Here comes the ag council float," said Tick Moran
from four porches at once, "and folks, they have outdone
whatever it was they did last year."

Birdie Pham had backed the Zinnia Wagon up to the House gates
and dropped the tailgate on a market's worth of mums,
gold and rust and a purple you could hear.
Marisol hauled water for her, four trips to the church spigot,
a bucket in each hand, shoes soaked through to the socks,
done right, because half-watered mums don't make it to noon.

"Look at her go," Verna Poteat said from a lawn chair,
to the street in general. "I'd pay money to watch you work."

"Water's free," Marisol said, and went back for more.

"One in the morning," she told Birdie,
coming back with the last two buckets.
"The boy sat on that question through an entire day
and launched it at one a.m."

"What'd you tell him?"

"Where to park. And that I love him bigger.
In caps, so it counts."

"In caps is legal tender," Birdie said,
and rang a bucket down onto the tailgate.

Sonny Dillard came through the crowd against the grain,
holding a plastic sleeve out ahead of him with both hands,
high, like a thing that might go off.

"Good bones," he said, instead of hello. "Bad wiring."

"You've been saying it since before I moved here."

"Been saying it since I had hair." He turned the sleeve around.
"Now it's got a stamp."

The page inside was typed and plain, no poetry in it anywhere,
the House's wiring written up run by run,
and in the corner a seal was pressed into the paper itself,
cold as a courthouse floor.
She read it twice.
It said what Sonny had always said,
except this copy could not be argued with across a desk.

"It's real," she said.

"It's real. Question is what anybody does with it,
trustees coming the first Tuesday of November."

"Frame it," Marisol said. "After I borrow it."

"Borrow away. I made copies.
I have waited years to make copies."

"So what do we do," Verna said, "pass a hat?
I've seen the hats in this town. Keep your hat."

Birdie reached into the cab, came out with a glass jar
of tailgate change, dumped the change into her apron pocket,
and set the empty jar on the tailgate hard enough to ring it.
"Jar," she said.

Marisol tore the top sheet off Birdie's order pad,
wrote HARTWELL HOUSE across it in grease pencil,
and taped Sonny's stamped page flat to the side of the truck
where the whole curb could read it for itself.

"The first thing anybody can say against the House
is the wiring," she said. "So we say it before they do,
and we fix it before anything pretty gets a dime."

She wrote it at the top of the list, *Rewiring — first*,
and under that her own name, plain,
first of however many, one voice out of twelve thousand,
no badge anywhere on it.

"Put the store down," Sonny said. "Good for a ladder
and every Saturday I'm above ground."

Verna licked a thumb and counted motel money into the jar.
"That's a week of soap. Don't ask what soap runs.
It would depress the whole parade."

Ray from Delmar's leaned over the tailgate, read the list,
and dropped in a folded bill.
"We're closed Mondays," he said. "So's the House. Twins."

The band went by.
The jar took quarters and folding money both,
and the list grew names she'd known a year
and names she'd known only as avatars asking about parking,
here in the flesh, in team colors, holding corn dogs.

Her phone had been going all morning, parking, parking,
one lost kid found before the post even finished posting,
and in a scroll between questions
the top of the town feed read CONDEMNATION — FINAL,
caps, no name on it that she caught,
and then a cymbal line crashed past four feet from her head,
and when her eyes came back down the feed had moved on
and the words were not anywhere in it.
She stood a moment with her thumb still, in all that noise.
Then a woman two strollers deep asked her where to park,
and she answered, because she always answered.

Eli Fountain turned up at the Wagon mid-morning,
walking like a man who had rehearsed the walking.

"Birdie."

"Eli."

"How many buckets of mums you got left?"

Birdie looked down the tailgate. "Nine."

"I'll take all nine."

A float went by playing something with the melody missing.

"For what?" Birdie said.

"The end zones," Eli said.

"The end zones."

"Decorative," he said, and then, committing with his whole chest,
"It's homecoming. The grass expects it."

Birdie started laughing on "decorative"
and did not stop while she counted his money,
did not stop while she loaded nine buckets onto his cart,
and was still going when the tail of the parade
had moved a block and a half on down Millrow,
her laugh carrying over the last snare line,
audible, people would claim, from the church steps.
She wiped her eyes with the heel of one hand.
"The peach man never bought a bucket," she said, to nobody.
Eli drove off sitting very straight, riding low on nine buckets.

At noon, in the shade of the House gates,
she looked for the words again on purpose,
scrolling back farther than the morning went.
Nothing. No one around her said one word about it,
so far as she could hear, and there was nothing to point at,
and the day went on being the best kind of loud.

She went up the hill with the last of the crowd
and heard the game long before there was anything to see,
the sound coming down through the streets in slow swells.
She had promised the parents' tent by halftime,
and with twenty minutes left in the half the west concourse
was a solid wall of people being patient at each other.
She was weighing the long way around the hill
when a green polo passed her at arm's length, not looking,
and a voice she knew said, conversationally, to no one at all,
"Service door past the second pillar. Cuts under.
Comes out at the tents."
He didn't stop. She didn't turn.
She counted ten, walked to the second pillar,
and found the door propped on a wedge of scrap pine.

Under the stands the game stopped being a game
and became sound entire, ninety thousand people overhead,
their voices coming down through plank and steel
until she could feel the score in her back teeth.
Light fell through the bleacher slats in long stripes
that shivered whenever the crowd moved.
Cal was ahead on the walkway at a maintenance man's pace,
and the collar of the polo pulled across his shoulders
when he looked up into the underside of all those people,
and she noticed, and let herself.

"Mind the cable run," he said, without turning.
"Left side's mine. Right side's original,
and the original was wrong."

She came level with him where the walkway widened.
Strangers, by agreement. Daylight at either end,
and nobody under here but the two of them and the noise.

"How much of this is yours?" she said, meaning the steel.

"Cross-bracing on this run, a dozen seasons back.
Those stringers two springs later.
That rank of bolts has been through my hands twice apiece."
He set his palm flat on an upright,
easy as a man checking a horse.
"Twenty years of Saturdays up there
thinking the ground is the ground. It isn't. It's this.
Somebody keeps it true."

She watched the hand on the steel and not the steel.
Forty-three years old, and the most interesting thing
at a ninety-thousand-seat homecoming
was one flat hand holding still on a girder.

Then the crowd noise tightened.
She heard it before she understood it,
the whole register climbing,
ninety thousand voices finding one vowel together,
and the roar landed, and everything above them let go at once.
Stomping rolled the length of the stands like falling freight,
dust sifted down through the stripes of light,
and the steel breathed with the crowd,
easing and taking up, easing and taking up.
The deck over their heads moved, truly moved.
She stepped in against the nearest upright,
and he had come to the same upright from his side,
his hand already on it, hers landing a bolt's width away.
Neither hand moved.
The roar arrived through the soles of her feet,
up the steel, into her palm, and for the length of it
there was no county, no feed, no stamped page taped to a truck,
just the noise, and across one girder
the man who kept all of it standing.

The radio on his belt cracked through it.
"Cal, you copy?" Ronnie's voice, half drowned.
"Bay three. Breaker's down at the west gate
and the half's coming."

Cal took his hand off the steel.
"Copy," he said. "Coming."
He looked at her once, and neither of them said
anything with a name in it,
names being the one thing they had agreed not to have.

"Straight on," he said. "Daylight at the end."

"Go," she said. "Keep the ground the ground."

They walked the last of it side by side,
because the walkway went one place, and pretending otherwise
would have looked stranger than walking,
and they came out through the far door into full sun
with a clean yard of air between them the whole while.
Nobody watching would have seen a single thing worth telling.
The band was staging at the tunnel, forty snares idling,
the hill was sound stacked on sound to the top of the sky,
and that yard of air was the loudest thing on it.
Then he turned for the west gate, and the crowd closed
behind him, and she carried the quiet of it to the tent.

The parents' tent was twelve conversations deep,
and she got pulled into four of them at once,
which was its own kind of music.
Halftime broke overhead and the band came down through canvas,
brass first, drums under, the tune arriving off the stone
half a second behind itself.
Her phone buzzed against the table. Parking.
She answered it the same as the other twelve thousand times.
Then, thumb still on the glass, she scrolled the town feed
back through the morning, to where the cold thing had been,
and it was not anywhere, not deleted-looking, simply gone,
a note nobody else had heard played.
She was still scrolling when the crowd found its next roar,
far off first, then in the tent poles, then up through her feet,
and she stood inside it with her thumb moving on the glass.
