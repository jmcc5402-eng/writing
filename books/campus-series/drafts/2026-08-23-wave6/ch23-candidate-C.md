# Chapter 23 — Millrow, After

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate C. Variance card: D3.

---

They went out by the side steps of Hartwell House
with the band still playing behind them.
The music followed them a block down Millrow and gave up.
Nobody stopped them.
Everybody saw them go.

Cal had her hand.
He had taken it on the porch in front of the whole town,
and he had not given it back,
and she had no plans to raise the matter.

The night was warm.
The street smelled like cut grass and somebody's late charcoal.
Their shoes were loud on the brick.
He didn't talk.
He started to, twice.
She heard him pick a sentence up both times
and set it back down.

"You can say it," she said. "Whatever it is."

"Working on it," he said.

Behind them the dance was letting out.
Car doors, laughter, somebody's horn.
Every porch they passed had somebody on it,
and every somebody watched them go by
and pretended not to, badly.

"They're all looking," he said.

"They are."

"Figured I'd mind more."

"You want to give them something to report?"

"I gave at the dance," he said.

Millrow was a short street.
Everyone said so, and it was true,
and there was not much runway in it.
Porch lights burned all down the block.
A dog considered them from behind a fence and let them pass.
His nerve ran out near the end of the street,
and the pavement ran out right behind it.

"Those porch boards," he said, at the corner.
"Back at Hartwell. That's quarter-sawn oak.
You cut the log that way, the board won't cup on you.
Won't hardly wear, either. Nobody mills it anymore.
Too slow. Too much waste."

"Cal Sutter," she said.
"That is the most romantic thing anybody has ever said to me."

He looked over to see if she was laughing at him.
She was not.
That was the terrible part.
He had said quarter-sawn oak to a divorced woman in the dark,
and it had worked all the way down,
and she was going to have to live with that.

"I know some things about heart pine too," he said.

"Don't spend it all in one night."

Her house sat a few blocks past the end of Millrow,
behind a low gate, under the live oak.
They walked the last of it slow, on purpose,
both of them, neither one admitting it.
She had smiled at him on Hartwell's porch
with the whole county watching,
and there was no unringing that bell.

At the gate he let go of her hand and took off his cap.
He didn't wring it or fool with the brim.
He took it off like a man in a picture show
made before either of them was born,
and he held it at his side and looked at her.
Under the porch light his hair was pressed flat
where the cap had been.
She looked at that longer than it deserved.
She was allowed to now.
Moths ran their circles at the porch light,
and under the live oak the dark came down close.

"Ma'am," he said.

"Don't you dare 'ma'am' me at my own gate."

"Marisol," he said, and that was worse.

She was laughing when he leaned in,
and he was laughing too,
and their foreheads hadn't even touched yet.
Then they did.
Then the laughing stopped.

The kiss was slow.
It was sure.
There was no question anywhere in it.
They had settled the question weeks ago, in private,
with the county none the wiser.
The only new thing tonight was the porch light on
and the street allowed to watch.
His hand came up and held the side of her face
like something worth being careful with.

She unlatched the gate without looking at it.
They went up the walk with his cap still off.
She got her own front door open on the second try,
which was his fault, and he did not apologize.
In the dark of the hall she pushed the good jacket
back off his shoulders, and it landed on the floor.

---

Sunday came up bright.

Her phone had been buzzing since seven.
She came downstairs, started the coffee,
and let the phone lie on the counter and buzz.
The kitchen was full of sun.
She drank the first cup standing up,
next to the calendar with its June date circled,
and picked up the phone with the second.

The parents' group was on fire.

Twelve thousand members,
and it appeared that every one of them
had either been at the dance
or knew somebody who had,
and somebody had a photograph of the last one.
There she was, in his arms, lit gold by the porch bulbs.
Three people had asked if the man was single.
One of them had asked twice.
The new moderators had asked everybody, twice,
to please stay on topic.
The topic, four hundred replies deep,
was now the man's shoulders.
She read the first hundred comments
with her hand over her mouth.
Then she got dressed and walked to the square,
because some things should be read aloud in a booth.

The Checkerboard never closed,
so on Sundays it filled instead.
The griddle was loud and the coffee was fresh
and the Liars' Table was seated at full strength.
Tick Moran had the outside chair.
Tunk Ferrell had the syrup.
Peanut Kyzer had opinions about both.
Earlene Tatum held the near booth,
positioned, as ever, to hear everything twice.

Cal was in the back booth with coffee,
off-shift cap on, forearms on the table,
taking up his whole bench like a man
with nowhere better on earth to be.
He stood when she came in.
Tick saw him do it.
Nobody at the Table said a word,
which meant they were saving them.
She could tell by the quiet.
It was the particular Checkerboard quiet
of eight people all not mentioning the same thing.

She slid in across from him
and set her phone on the table like evidence.

"The photograph is everywhere," she said.

"I heard."

"Twelve thousand members, Cal. They're on the case.
Do you want the leading theories?"

"I want every one of them."

"Theory one. You are the new strength coach."

"I'm not."

"They know. A whole thread worked it out.
Theory one has been retired with honors.
Theory two, and I need you to take this with dignity.
You are a visiting country singer."

Cal drank his coffee.

"They believe you're in town to shoot a video.
There is a faction. They have screenshots of the jacket."

"It's a good jacket," said Cal.

"I'm aware," said Marisol.

"Theory three." She cleared her throat and read it straight.
"'Pretty sure that's the tall one from facilities.
Asking for a friend.'"

"Told you it was facilities," Tick said, to his eggs.

"There is also a side argument," she went on,
"about whether you can even be the facilities man,
because the facilities man is in trouble with the county,
and a man in trouble with the county
would not dance that slow right out in the open."

"Sound reasoning," said Cal.

"He can't sing," said Peanut Kyzer, to the Table, with authority.

"You've never once heard him," said Tunk.

"I stand by it."

"The county faction is losing badly," Marisol went on.
"The friend faction has momentum
and a zoomed-in picture of your shoulders.
The friend has follow-up questions.
The friend would like to know if the shoulders are recent."

"Recent," said Cal.

"Her word. I'm only reporting what I read."

He listened all the way through.
That was the thing she kept walking into with him.
He didn't check the door or the clock or his phone.
He took every theory like testimony
and let her get all the way to the end.

"You understand I can't do a thing about any of it," she said.
"They took my badge weeks ago. I'm a civilian now.
I read it like everybody else."

"How is that?"

"All of the news, none of the paperwork. I recommend it."

The phone buzzed in her hand.
Kat, this time, in capital letters,
with margaritas attached and a demand
for every detail in chronological order.
Marisol turned the phone face down.
That one deserved a real answer, and not from a booth.

She raised a hand toward the counter instead.

"Verna. One buttermilk, a whole slice,
and set it in front of him. I'm buying."

Verna Poteat cut the slice and named the price.
Pie rates at the Checkerboard were set by Verna,
reviewed by Verna, and appealed to Verna,
and nobody in the history of the place
had ever won the appeal,
and nobody tried now.
The griddle was going and Verna had her hands full of Sunday,
so Marisol fetched the plate herself,
took the coffee pot along on the way back,
and topped off the whole Liars' Table without being asked.

"You don't work here," said Verna.

"I owe a debt, Verna. I'm settling my affairs."

She set the plate in front of Cal
and laid the fork square on the napkin.

"Delmar's porch," she said, to the booth,
to the Table, to the room at large.
"Weeks ago. I lost that bet fair,
and this is the pie, and every one of you is a witness."

"Witnessed," said Tunk Ferrell.

Cal looked at the pie.
Then at her.
She had owed it since the porch
and had let it ride all this time,
and neither of them had ever said what they were waiting on.
There was nothing left to wait on.
The pie had kept. It had kept just fine.
He picked up the fork.

"Verna," he said. "It's a fine pie."

"I know it," said Verna.

"Well?" said Earlene Tatum, from the near booth,
not even pretending. "Which is it, then? Coach or singer?"

"Earlene, I am not at liberty.
I'm a private citizen who lost a bet to a country singer.
The friend can send her questions through the group."

And Cal smiled.

Not the quick one.
Not the once-and-done, back-in-the-pocket one.
It opened all the way and it stayed open,
the door thrown wide on the lit room,
and the whole Liars' Table watched it happen,
in front of God and the eggs.
Something came loose under her ribs and stayed loose.

He set down the fork.
"Ask your friend," he said, "whether she'd like my number."

The Liars' Table looked over.
All of it, at once.
In Ashford that counted as a press release.
By supper it would be countywide,
and neither of them moved to stop it.

"She has it," said Marisol. "She's had it for weeks.
She's been unbearable about it."

"Sounds like a good friend."

"She's all right."

He ate the pie down to the shine on the plate.
She drank half his coffee because hers had gone cold,
and when it was done she paid Verna at the register.
Her money. Her debt. Paid where everybody could see.
Verna rang it up and looked at her
over the top of the register for a long moment.

"About time," said Verna.

"Verna, I paid the minute the pie existed."

"Wasn't talking about the pie," said Verna.

Then he walked her out.
Past Tick, who lifted two fingers off the table.
Past Tunk, grinning into his syrup.
Past Peanut Kyzer, who had stopped chewing altogether.
Past Earlene, who already had her phone to her ear.
His hand was at her back the whole way,
light and certain,
and every face in the Checkerboard was grinning,
and she would not have traded one of them.

The bell over the door rang behind them.
They stood on the sidewalk in the sun.
The square was Sunday-quiet.
The tower rang eleven.
