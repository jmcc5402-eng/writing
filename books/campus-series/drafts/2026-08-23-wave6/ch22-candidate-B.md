# Chapter 22 — The Porch Holds

POV: Cal, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate B. Variance card: D7.

---

Halfway through the second reel the porch was full of couples
and carrying every one of them,
and the string lights along the rail jumped on the downbeats.
The fiddle band had the steps.
The cake tables had the deep shade under the live oaks.
The boards gave a little under the reels and came back,
which is what they were for.
And at the top of the walk the front door of Hartwell House
stood open to the dark for the first time all season,
the hall lit gold behind it,
laying a long stripe of light down the steps
and out across the grass.

Nobody said one word about the door.
Everybody came up the walk a little slower because of it.

Cal had the north end of the rail to himself,
which on a porch that full took the whole town cooperating.
The moat was polite. It moved when he moved.
It offered him a lawn chair nobody sat in
and a clear view of everything he wasn't part of.
He had come anyway.
Staying home tonight would have been a sentence said out loud,
and he was through saying it.
So he stood where the county could look at him
under the same lights it had strung on the House it loved,
and he ate his cake slow, and he let them look.

He had worn the good jacket.
It still fit through the shoulders,
though it had been a long while
since he'd had a reason to find that out.
The keys to the House rode in his shirt pocket under it,
and on the big downbeats he felt them tick against his chest,
faint as a watch.

Through the open door he could see the staircase wall,
generations of framed couples climbing it two by two,
and out here the same faces, older, in motion,
turning past the rail with cake on their breath.
Verna Poteat stood on the third step of the hall staircase,
her hand on the rail, not going up, not coming down.
A century of Ashford men had gone to one knee on that step.
Verna stood on it a long time and said nothing about anything.
Cal looked away first.
Some kinds of standing are load-bearing,
and it's rude to watch.

More photographs were being taken than any one night needed.
Delores Fountain-Mabry posed groups of four and six
against the rail and checked every frame
before she would let a group go.
"Squeeze in. Act like you like each other."

"We do," said Tunk Ferrell.

"Camera can't tell," Delores said, and took three more.

Tick Moran worked the rockers with his recorder,
collecting old-timers for WDSS two minutes at a time.
Ratchet had stationed herself under the near cake table,
where nobody was enforcing anything,
and Coach Merritt was retelling the afternoon
to anyone who slowed down,
and it got better every time he told it.
"I'm just saying the boy could fly," Merritt said.
People kept stepping back from the dancing
to look up at the House between tunes,
and then stepping back in without saying why.
No one named the thing that kept circling the porch.
It was simply there, all evening,
the cold that comes off a window glass before you touch it.

Earlene Tatum crossed the moat with two plates of cake
and no visible respect for the border.
"Coconut," she said, "or Delores's chocolate,
which has a sheen I'm not qualified to explain."

"Coconut."

"Coward." She handed it over,
then set her back against the rail beside him,
facing the band, taking her time about it,
so the entire porch could see where she'd chosen to stand.
"You know this is a dance."

"I heard the fiddle. Put it together."

"Mm." She forked a bite off her own plate.
"Cake's better on the move. That's all I'll say about it."

"That's never all."

"It is tonight."

Cal laughed once, short, down at the plate.

Peanut Kyzer came through on her wake
with a thermos everybody understood.
"Sutter. You want weather in that cake?"

"I'm set, Peanut."

"You look set." He moved off down the rail,
improving plates as he went.

Odell Fountain stopped at the rail,
looked at the band, looked at Cal.
"Big crowd," Odell said, and moved on.

Tick Moran came by with the recorder held low,
which for Tick was a courtesy.
"Two minutes for WDSS, Cal? Homecoming memories,
whatever you've got."

"Not tonight, Tick."

"Fair enough." He didn't move.
"Off the record. Door's open."
He nodded up the walk. "First time all fall."

"I know it," Cal said. "I keep the keys."

"That you do." Tick drifted on toward the rockers.

Somewhere in town, Boyd's page was waiting on his name,
due the Friday before the trustees met,
and tonight was not Friday's to spend.

---

Eli Fountain was already out on the boards
when the waltz set came around,
dancing with Birdie Pham in the strict technical sense,
frame correct, face solemn as jury duty,
while Birdie steered the both of them
and let him believe otherwise.
When the tune closed he kept her hand.
"There's another one coming," Eli said. "Tune."

"I know how music works," Birdie said,
and didn't let go.

Down at the cake tables Ray had an audience going.
"—and the second mule wouldn't go either," Ray said,
and the table went up, and the band played over it.

Marisol had kept to the far end of the porch all evening,
by the agreement,
which was costing more to keep every hour it ran.
Her hair was down, dark and loose past her shoulders,
her good dress moved when the boards did,
and she was giving the cake table
everything Ray's story deserved,
sending every smile out over the yard,
past every ear on the porch,
the same delivery she had used on Cal
since the day they met.
He knew the flight path by heart.
He had kept the count for months
without ever writing a number on it.

Off across campus the carillon rang ten.
Nobody went home.

Earlene came back for his empty plate.
"You planning to hold that rail up all night?"

"Rail's holding me up."

"Mm." She took the plate.
"Wasted arrangement all around, if you ask me,"
she said, and left the space beside him empty.

The band called the last set.

---

Cal was three boards past the moat
before the nearest conversations quit.
He had stopped wanting the secret
somewhere in the middle of the evening.
He hadn't lost his grip on it. He had set it down.
And once the wanting quit,
the crossing was only walking,
twenty feet of heart pine in front of the whole county,
the jacket sitting square across his shoulders,
the keys ticking on his chest.

The porch did not go quiet all at once.
Word just crossed it faster than he did.

She saw him coming the whole way
and stood her ground by the cake table,
chin level, letting the town watch her wait.

"Marisol."

"Cal."

"I'd like the last dance," he said,
at plain Saturday volume, no more and no less,
"if it isn't spoken for."

Up on the steps the fiddler,
who had waited years to be useful in exactly this way,
leaned the tune down into three-quarter time
without one soul asking him to.

"It isn't spoken for," she said. "It's held."

"For anybody in particular?"

"For whoever crossed first."

"Then I'm glad I walked fast."

"You didn't," she said. "You walked like church."

And then she smiled at him.

Every smile of hers he had ever stood near
had been in transit — bound past his shoulder,
over his ear, out to the town at his back —
and this one came straight on.
Dead level. Aimed.
It did not bend at the last foot of air,
and it did not go by.
It crossed the lights and the twenty feet and arrived,
all the way, his.

The town saw a woman smile at a man
before the last dance of the night,
which is what there was to see.
Cal stood inside it while it landed.
His breath took a knee.

Her hand came up into his hand, small and warm.
His other found her waist over the good dress,
and there was nothing between them
the town couldn't photograph,
so the town photographed it.
Phones came up all along the rail,
screens glowing warm as jar candles.
Delores directed. Aisha Cole got up on a chair.
"Hold still, the light's doing something," Aisha said,
and nobody held still, and she got it anyway.

They turned with the tune,
and the porch light moved across her face
and came back around.

"They're taking pictures," she said, near his collar.

"Then we'd better dance well."

"You could have warned me."

"Didn't know for certain till I was walking."

"Yes you did."

"Halfway across, then."

She was quiet through one full turn of the porch.
"Whole town, Cal."

"Whole town."

"The jar did good today," she said.
"People kept putting in twice. On purpose."

"Sounds like this town."

"It does," she said. "It sounds exactly like it."

"About time," Earlene said from the rail,
to nobody and to everybody.

"Your friend will take credit," Marisol said.

"She'll have the whole thing claimed by Sunday."

The fiddler stretched the last tune
to fit every couple Ashford had,
Eli and Birdie, Coach Merritt and Delores,
Tunk Ferrell counting quietly to three,
and Verna Poteat came down off the third step at last
and stood in the doorway light to watch.

Under all of them the boards gave and held,
gave and held,
the same easy give they had offered
every October anybody could name,
while the hall light lay steady out the open door
and the last tune went around again.
The porch held.
