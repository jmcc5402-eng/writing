# Chapter 22 — The Porch Holds

POV: Cal, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate C. Variance card: D5.

---

Hartwell House put its dance up at sundown,
in the order the dance had always gone up.
String lights first, looped along the porch rail
by Odell Fountain and two nephews
whose breath showed while they argued the sag.
"It dips in the middle or it isn't our porch,"
said Odell, and the sag stayed.
Then the cake tables under the live oaks,
boards over sawhorses, white cloths clothespinned down
against a small traveling wind,
Delores Fountain-Mabry's four-layer setting up firm and glossy
the minute it left the warm of her kitchen.
Then the band, a fiddle, two guitars,
and a borrowed upright bass, taking the front steps
for a stage, the fiddler tuning
with his coat still buttoned to the chin.

Birdie Pham had brought marigolds for the tables,
rust and gold in canning jars down the whole white row,
and she squared each jar as she passed
like the row would be graded.
"Touch the jars, not the flowers," said Birdie, to a kid,
who touched neither.

By full dark the lawn held most of Ashford,
wool and flannel and one man in a suit,
cider going around with steam standing off every cup.
Coats came off the young ones inside an hour of dancing
and went over the porch rail in a row,
and the oaks dropped a stiff leaf now and then
onto the cloths for Delores to flick away.

And the front door of Hartwell House stood open.

All season that door had been shut by dark.
Tonight it stood open onto the lit hall,
lamplight down the front steps in one long panel,
and the town went up and came back out
with paper plates and second helpings,
easy, as though it had never once been otherwise.
Nobody remarked on it,
which was how Cal knew that everyone had seen.

Cal had come on foot, in the one good jacket he owned.
Wool, old enough to have come back into style,
still square across his shoulders,
and tonight, at last, it was cold enough out to wear it.
The House keys rode in his shirt pocket under the wool,
and he felt them there when he breathed.
He had considered staying home,
right up until he understood what staying home would say,
and how clearly, and to every ear in the county.
So he had shaved, put on the jacket, and walked over.

The town arranged itself so that the stretch of rail
where Cal stood stayed simple to avoid.
Nobody was rude. Rude would have required looking at him.
Folks just found other places to need to be,
and the string lights went on shining anyway,
on the House the county loved
and on the man the county had decided against,
the two of them strung on the same wire.
Cal held his corner and drank his cider slow
and watched the dance he had watched from this lawn
most of the years of his life.
He had fixed half of what a person could see from that rail.
The gutter run. The rail itself, two summers back.
The county could believe what it liked about his signature;
the House knew his hands.

There were more photographs that night
than any one night could use.
Delores marshaled the Fountains onto the steps.
"Oldest to youngest," said Delores.
"Now the other way. Now with the band,
before this cake goes. Smile like it's paid for."
Aisha Cole worked the rail with her good camera.
"One more," said Aisha, who had said it a dozen times
and was believed every time.
Couples stood where couples had always stood,
at the turn of the rail under the lights,
and held still a little longer
than any camera needed them to.
Ray stood for his and said, "Good cake this year."

Nobody said the word that was out there in the dark
past the reach of the string lights.
It stood at the edge of the lawn all evening,
right about where the cold began,
and the whole town danced with its back to it.
They said other things instead.
That the band was the best in years.
That the cake had outdone itself.
That somebody ought to get a picture
of exactly how the lights hung,
in case anybody ever needed to know.

Through the open door Cal could see the hall staircase
and the wall of couples going up it in frames,
generations of them, dressed for every decade.
Verna Poteat stood a long while at the third step,
one hand on the rail, saying nothing.
Everybody on that porch knew the third step.
A century of Ashford had gone down on one knee there.
Verna kept her back straight and her own counsel,
and after a while she came out
and took a plate of cake like anybody.

Across town there was a piece of paper
that wanted Cal's name on it
by the Friday before the trustees sat.
He left it out in the dark with the rest of it.

Earlene Tatum crossed the lawn with two ciders
and set up at his elbow like she'd been assigned the post.

"Sutter."

"Earlene."

"You want to know what they're saying about you tonight?"

"Not much," Cal said.

"Good, because it's dull, and I hate repeating dull."
She pushed the second cider into his hand.
"Drink that. A man at a party with empty hands
looks guilty even when he isn't."

"And when he is?"

"Guilty stays home," said Earlene.
"I've come to this dance my whole life,
and guilty never once showed up. You're here."
She knocked her cup against his.
"You look decent, by the way. Jacket fits."

"It's old."

"So is the House," said Earlene,
"and old and still fitting is the best thing
we grow around here."

She stayed at his elbow through two whole songs,
drinking cider in plain view of everybody,
which from Earlene was a speech.

"You planning to hold this rail up all night?"

"I hadn't decided."

"Decide better," said Earlene,
and moved off toward the cake.

Peanut Kyzer came by with a paper plate
riding three kinds of cake.

"Mule's open after," said Peanut.
"First one's mine, if the band plays anything
a man can survive."

"They've killed nobody yet," Cal said.

"Night's young," said Peanut. "You dancing tonight?"

"Ask the band," Cal said, and Peanut went on, satisfied.

Coach Merritt danced with Delores like a man
being marked on form, elbows correct, chin high,
and Tunk Ferrell danced with whoever was nearest
when the music started,
which everyone knew to expect
and positioned themselves accordingly.
Down by the steps, Eli Fountain stood beside Birdie,
holding her coat folded over both arms
with the concentration of a color guard.
Whatever Eli wasn't saying this month,
he wasn't saying it right next to her,
and Birdie was letting him not say it.
The whole town was pulling for them anyway.

Marisol Pruett was at the cake tables.
She had run the pledge-jar post at the parade
all that morning, and her right hand still carried
a gray shadow of marker ink;
she cut and plated with it in even, certain strokes,
dark hair pinned up off her neck against the night air,
a good dress on her the deep red of the oak leaves
that had been coming down all week.

She smiled all evening.
At Delores. At the band between numbers.
At every kid who came through the cake line twice
and believed he was getting away with it.
And every smile that came anywhere near Cal
went past his ear,
the same place every smile of hers had gone
since the day they met.
He knew how many that made.
He had known the number for months,
and he kept it where he kept the House keys.
Close. Out of sight.

"Last set, Ashford," the fiddler called from the steps.
"Find who you came for."

Couples began sorting themselves out of the crowd.
The lights burned steady.
Marisol was laughing at something Delores said,
with her whole face, at somebody else, again.

Cal set his cider on the rail.

Between the rail and the cake tables,
the secret stopped being worth what he was paying for it.
No slip. No nerve giving out.
He just quit wanting it,
the whole careful apparatus of not-looking,
and the moment he quit wanting it,
nothing on that porch was holding it up but him.
So he put it down.

He crossed the porch — past Earlene's cup stopping halfway,
past the cake tables, past four conversations
quitting on the same word — and stood
in front of Marisol Pruett
with the lights on him, the cold at his back,
and his hands easy at his sides.

"Marisol," Cal said.

She turned with the cake knife still in her hand.

"They're about to play the last one," he said,
loud enough for the porch and not one person more.
"I'd like it to be with you."

"Lord," said Earlene, somewhere behind him, softly,
to her cider.

Up on the steps the fiddler,
who had waited years to be useful in exactly this way,
slowed the tune without being asked.

Marisol laid the knife down on the white cloth.
She took her time about it.
Then she looked up, and her smile came straight on.

Not past his ear.
Not at the crowd, with him permitted somewhere in it.
Level, dead center, the whole distance closed,
aimed at Cal Sutter and arriving,
in front of every soul the county had.
The porch went on with its lights and its fiddle
and its cake and its cold,
and one thing crossed all of it
that had never once come all the way to him before,
and he stood still and let it land.

"In front of everybody," Marisol said.

"In front of everybody," Cal said.

"Well then." She gave him her hand.
"It's the last one. I'd hate to waste it."

His hand at her waist. Her hand in his.
Nothing the town couldn't photograph,
and the town photographed it.
Phones came up along the rail, held high and steady,
little bright windows with the two of them inside,
Tick Moran turning his sideways for the wide shot.
"Hold still," Aisha called. "No, keep moving.
You're better moving."
Nobody whispered.
It was all out in the open, where he had put it.

They turned with the tune,
and the old boards sounded under every step.
Her breath showed when she spoke, close to his jaw.

"The whole county is looking at us," Marisol said.

"I wore the good jacket," Cal said.

She laughed, bright and out loud,
right up against the music,
and the nearest couples caught it
and passed it down the rail without knowing why.

"Where'd you learn to do this?" she said.

"A porch a long time back," Cal said,
and she didn't ask, and he was glad twice over.

"Everyone is going to ask me about you tomorrow."

"What'll you tell them?"

"Everything," Marisol said. "Slowly.
One person at a time."

Her hand moved on his chest and found the shirt pocket
through the wool, the House keys under her palm,
and she let it stay there for the rest of the song.

Out across the dark campus the carillon struck the hour,
small with the distance, every note arriving clean,
the air carrying farther tonight than it had all year.
The band went around once more,
because nobody on the steps wanted to be the one to stop.
Under the dancing the porch boards gave and held,
gave and held, under Eli and Birdie,
under couples already framed on the staircase wall
and couples nobody had thought to photograph yet,
under every couple Ashford had,
under Cal Sutter and Marisol Pruett
in the middle of all of them,
a hundred years of Saturday nights deep in the wood,
and the porch held.
