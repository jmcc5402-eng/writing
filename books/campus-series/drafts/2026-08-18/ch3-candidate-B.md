# Chapter 3 — The Second Guest Book

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
FIRST DRAFT — Candidate B. Variance card: D1 (sound-forward).

---

Marisol heard the market before she saw it.

Away Saturdays turned Ashford's sound inside out.
No drumline, no tide of ninety thousand coming over a wall.
The whole town's noise packed itself instead under one long shed roof
at the Doss County Curb Market and got specific.
Card readers chirped their approval notes down the aisle,
taped to folding tables and clothespinned to stakes.
The cold-brew tent ran its grinder in short bursts, like a pit crew.
At the far end the boiled-peanut kettle kept its low old boil,
the one sound in the building with seniority.

And the game. The away game played on three propped phones,
one against a watermelon, one on a tackle box,
one leaned against a jar of pickled okra,
and no two of them agreed about when anything happened.
The okra phone ran fastest,
so the camp chairs at that end always roared first,
and the roar traveled down the shed in installments,
tackle box, then watermelon,
the same good news breaking three times like waves off one boat.

Her pocket kept its own time under all of it.
The parents' group was running an away-day thread called
WE RIDE AT DAWN, CARPOOL EDITION,
and the market had a thread of its own,
which she'd been added to in her third week in town
without anybody asking her first,
which in Ashford was a sacrament.

Birdie Pham had the good corner stall, tailgate down,
buckets of zinnias and sunflowers and mums drinking in rows.

"Zinnias," Marisol said. "The loud ones."

"All my zinnias are loud." Birdie pulled a bucket forward.
"These are the ones the neighbors complain about."

"Those. All of those."

"Cash or tap?" Birdie said. "Reader's on the tomato stake."

Marisol tapped, and the reader sang its two cents,
and behind her the roar came down the shed again in thirds.

"You hear the game like that all morning?" she asked.

"Like an echo with opinions," Birdie said. "You learn to wait
for the okra end. They're living in the future down there."

A shadow passed the pepper table. Birdie did not look up.

"Fourth lap," she said.

Marisol did not have to look either. She looked anyway.
Eli Fountain was moving down the aisle
with the unhurried stride of a man on official business
who could not have named the business under oath.

"He's been orbiting since nine," Birdie said,
wrapping wet stems in newspaper.
"Me, the peppers, and the peanut kettle. Big loop."

"You could wave him in."

"Air traffic control waves people in," Birdie said.
"I sell flowers."

He docked on the fifth pass.

"Birdie."

"Eli."

He surveyed the buckets with a professional frown,
a man consulting a wine list in a language he almost read.

"I'll take the mums," he said.

"Which mums?"

"All of them."

Birdie's face didn't move, but the pause did.
"You want nine buckets of mums."

"They're a hardy fall bloomer," Eli told the buckets.
"Good root structure. I've got bare spots by the north gate
that could use the cover."

"You want nine buckets of mums for erosion."

"Erosion is a real problem," said Eli, with dignity.

"Tap's on the tomato stake," Birdie said,
and her mouth had surrendered a quarter inch at one corner.

The reader chirped. It was the loudest thing
either of them was prepared to say about it.

Marisol stayed to watch him ferry mums
to a groundskeeping cart two buckets at a time,
and Birdie watched too, making no change of expression whatsoever.

"Erosion," Marisol said.

"Before the mums it was a flat of marigolds," Birdie said.
"For 'the visual.' I'm going to be rich by Thanksgiving."

She heard Cal Sutter before she saw him.
Keys, a whole ring of them riding a belt loop,
coming down the aisle half a beat slower
than everything else in the shed.
She turned and gave the peppers her full attention,
which left her ears on him, which was better anyway.

He had Verna Poteat's market basket in one hand,
carried at exactly the height that saved Verna reaching for it,
and when the corner table started to walk on its bad leg
he caught the edge with two fingers, without setting anything down,
and held it level while the vendor reset the shim,
then moved on as though the table had straightened up
out of respect.

At the tomatoes the keys went quiet a moment.
He paid for Verna's basket before her wallet cleared her purse,
took the scolding for it with half a nod,
and proceeded down the aisle in the visible belief
that nobody in the shed was paying him any attention.

The shed was paying him attention.
The jam table tracked him its full length
without once breaking conversation.
Marisol gave him the same consideration she gave the weather,
which is to say constantly, and with plans.

"He's still got my wrench," she said.

"The whole market's heard about the wrench," Birdie said.
"Verna tells it like a war story. There's a version now
where you chased him."

"There's a version where I didn't?"

The keys faded past the peanut kettle and out the far end,
and the shed sounded ordinary again, which annoyed her.

Her phone buzzed once, alone.

She'd have missed it under the shed noise,
except that it buzzed wrong. Not the carpool thread's popcorn.
One message, no follow-up, like a single knock.

She had the picture open before the sound reached
the rest of the market. Then it reached the rest of the market,
chimes and buzzes breaking out down the aisle in patches,
like rain starting on a tin roof.

The picture was a rendering. Computer-clean, brochure-green,
a tidy lawn rolled out flat on Millrow
exactly where Hartwell House stood.
Not the House moved. Not the House saved.
The House erased and grassed over,
with a caption in polite real-estate serif:
"Pending trustee approval at the November board meeting."

The okra phone roared about something. Nobody roared back.

"Where'd this come from?" the jam table said.

"Nobody sent it," said the pickle man. "Everybody forwarded it."

"That's Millrow. That's the House."

"It says pending. Pending means it isn't."

"Pending means it's moving. That's what pending means."

"Whose lawn is that? There is no lawn on Millrow."

"That's the point, honey."

"The board's got students to house. Land is land."

"Say that louder. I want Verna to hear you say it."

Earlene surfaced at Marisol's elbow, reading over her arm.
"November board meeting," she said, flat as a road closure.
"Trustees. That lawn's been drawn by somebody
who never once stood on that porch."

Marisol had never been inside Hartwell House.
A year in town, and she knew it as the porch
you slowed for on Millrow, the place where half the couples
in this shed had started.
She looked at the tidy nothing in the picture,
and her thumb pressed the screen white at the edges.

Verna Poteat had her glasses out of her purse
and somebody's phone at arm's length.
The aisle quieted itself around her without being told to.

"I got engaged on that porch," Verna said.
"Both my sisters got engaged on that porch.
Half this shed got engaged on that porch."
She handed the phone back.
"Nobody in the history of this town
ever got down on one knee in a lawn.
You know what a lawn has in it? Chiggers."

The laugh went down the shed the same as the game had,
in installments, okra end last for once,
and it helped, and it fixed nothing,
which is what a laugh is for.

She ran her errands with the zinnias riding shotgun
and the market thread going off in the cupholder
like a slow storm.
In the pharmacy lot, between the dry cleaning and the bank,
she opened the parents' group queue.
Four hundred and some member requests, backed up since August,
new families wanting in before the season got any older.
She waved them through, approve, approve, approve,
thumb on autopilot, four hundred people in four minutes,
brisk as stamping hands at a gate.
It was the most useful thing she'd done all day,
and it took less thought than the zinnias had.

She'd run this group for years before she ever had
an Ashford address, a team mom at long distance
since her boy's freshman fall,
twelve thousand parking questions answered
from two states over.
When the dust of the divorce had finally settled,
moving to the town where her son and half her mornings
already lived had simply made sense.

She put the phone face down on the passenger seat,
under the zinnias, and went and got the dry cleaning.

She heard Delmar's before she made the turn onto Millrow,
the screen door doing its two-note slam, spring and frame,
steady as a metronome with the after-market crowd,
and under it the smoker ticking like a parked engine.
The argument had beaten her there.

"It says pending," a man at the counter was saying.

"Everything says pending till it's done," Ray said,
working the register without looking at it.
"My roof said pending for two years. Then one Tuesday
I didn't have a roof."

"So who do we call? Who do you even call about a picture?"

"You don't call about a picture," Earlene said,
from her stool by the pie case.
"You count. That's all a board is.
A headcount that went to college."

The register drawer shot home and did not open again,
because Ray had come out from behind it.
He stripped last week's meat order off the clipboard
that lived by the register,
clipped a fresh legal pad in its place,
tied the counter pen to the clip
with a length of butcher's twine,
and set the whole rig down by the toothpicks,
where nobody in the building could pretend not to see it.

"There," he said. "Anybody thinks that House ought to
keep standing where it's stood, put your name down.
Headcount starts now."

He took the pen and signed the first line himself, unasked,
in letters you could read from the door,
then dropped the pen on its twine and went back to the register.

"What do we call it?" somebody asked from the pie end.

"It's a guest book," said Verna Poteat,
who had beaten everyone to Delmar's
and was two ribs into a plate.
"The House already has one."

"You can't even get inside the House, Verna."

"The porch," Verna said, patient, terrible.
"Every yes ever said on that porch is a signature.
This here's just the second guest book."

The market thread had a picture of Ray's rig
inside ten minutes, and the name came back
from six directions in the same hour, and it stuck.
The House's second guest book.
By dusk the line for the pen was four deep,
and the screen door kept the count,
two notes for every name.

Marisol took the pen when it swung to her on its twine.
Both columns of the first page were full already,
names she knew from the tent and names she didn't yet.
She added hers, passed the pen behind her without looking,
and heard it taken.

She stayed for a plate she hadn't planned on,
at the end of the counter, in the noise,
and let the town be loud around her.
She was in the book now.
