# Chapter 3 — The Second Guest Book

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate: C. Variance card: D7.

---

The peach man wanted three dollars a basket,
and Marisol had him down to two twenty-five
when the away game gasped on three phones at once.

The phones stood propped down the center aisle
of the Doss County Curb Market's long shed,
each leaning on its own jam jar,
each running the broadcast at its own idea of now.
The near one was honest.
The middle one ran four seconds behind and knew shame.
The far one ran a full play ahead,
and its owner had turned prophecy into a personality.

"Incomplete," he called, arms crossing,
before the near phone had even snapped the ball.

"You are ruining live sports for this entire shed,"
said the woman in the cold-brew tent,
which shared six feet of gravel with the boiled-peanut kettle,
oat milk and brine keeping formal diplomatic relations.

"Prophets are never loved at home," the man said.
The near phone snapped the ball. The pass fell incomplete.
The whole shed booed him, and he accepted it, glowing.

"Two twenty-five," the peach man said to Marisol,
in the voice of a man signing away the family land.
"I hope you sleep at night."

"Like a queen." She tapped her card on the reader
taped to his folding table. The tape was structural.
The reader chirped anyway.
"Your peaches bruise on the north side, by the way.
You should be paying me a consulting fee."

"Get out of my stall."

"Buying flowers first. Don't cry where the customers can see you."

The market's group chat had been going since seven,
eighty-some regulars in a thread named SHED HAPPENS,
currently split on whether the middle phone's lag
counted as time travel and, if so, whose fault it was.
Marisol scrolled it one-thumbed on the move,
past a card table taking tap-to-pay for tomato seedlings,
past two kids running delivery-app pickups stall to stall
for tips, a whole gray-market economy in scuffed sneakers.
One year in this town, and she had a seat on this exchange.
She knew which vendors padded the scale,
and which ones slipped an extra peach to anybody
who asked after their mother.

---

"Fifteen," Birdie Pham was saying,
snapping the rubber band off a bunch of zinnias
and rolling them into a cone of brown paper.
"Last zinnias of August. Scarcity pricing."

"You have four buckets of them," Marisol said.

"Three buckets and a display model. Fifteen."

"Twelve, and I keep telling people your truck's the reason
my tent looked civilized."

"Twelve, and you take the sunflowers nobody wants."

"Everybody wants sunflowers."

"These came up short. They're insecure about it.
They need a confident home." Birdie was already wedging them
into the cone. "You have confident arms."

"That is the nicest thing anyone's said to me all week,"
Marisol said, "and a man kept my wrench last Saturday,
so the bar was on the floor."

Behind them, Eli Fountain passed the flower truck
for what Marisol could personally certify was the third time,
carrying his own elbows, studying the middle distance.

"Eli," Birdie said, without turning around.
"Three laps. Buy something or start paying rent."

Eli stopped. He looked at the truck
as if it had pulled up that second and surprised him.

"I need," he said, and his eyes went down the tailgate
with rising panic, over zinnias, sunflowers, mums,
not one thing on that truck a groundskeeper needed,
"mums. A bucket of mums."

"A bucket," Birdie said.

"For the soil. They fix nitrogen."

"They absolutely do not."

"Then they'll be ornamental," Eli said, with dignity.

Birdie named a price. He paid it without hearing it,
tapping his card twice because the first tap didn't take,
and then stood there with the bucket of bronze mums
gathered against his chest in both arms,
a man who had achieved his true objective
and had no plan for the remainder of his life.

"The rye's holding its color," he offered.

"I know," Birdie said. "You told me Thursday."
But she said it while she tucked one stem deeper
into his bucket, straightening what didn't need straightening,
and neither of them stepped apart.

Marisol took her flowers and left them to it, which cost her.
She'd have paid real money for a chair.

---

Verna Poteat's basket was already riding on Cal Sutter's arm
when Marisol spotted him one aisle over,
moving through the crowd at the speed of a man
running quiet errands in his own house.
Verna steamed alongside at half his height, directing.
Whatever she said, he adjusted course.

At the seedling table a stack of flats started to slide,
and he set one hand flat on top of it, no hurry,
still listening to Verna,
and the stack reconsidered and stood down.
That was the thing from under the canopy,
the thing Marisol had elected to stop thinking about,
unsuccessfully. The effort never showed.
Things near him simply began behaving.

He picked tomatoes with actual attention,
turned each one over once, paid,
and took the bag in the same hand as the basket,
keeping his right free for whatever the market
might next need caught.
He believed, she could tell, that he was invisible.

SHED HAPPENS knew better.

*green polo at the tomatoes*

*somebody tell the tomato table to be cool*

*he bought six. what does one man need with six tomatoes*

Marisol contributed nothing to the record.
She stood holding flowers with an expression
she was glad nobody was logging.
The man still had her wrench.
The debt was accruing interest,
and she intended, at a time of her choosing, to collect.

---

Her phone went off against her hip
while both arms were still full of paper cone.
By the time she'd shifted the flowers and dug it out,
it had gone off four more times,
and the shed's whole soundscape had changed pitch,
a hundred pockets buzzing at once,
the broadcast still talking to itself on three phones
that nobody was watching anymore.

The picture was already in the parents' group,
already in SHED HAPPENS,
already in a text from Earlene with no words attached.
Nobody could establish where it had started.
Nobody had sent it. Everybody had forwarded it.

It was a developer's rendering, computer-clean,
sunlight at a flattering angle,
and it showed a stretch of Millrow she'd have known anywhere,
except that where Hartwell House stood
there was only a tidy green lawn.
Not the House moved. Not the House saved.
Grass, mowed in cheerful diagonal stripes,
as if the House had never happened to the street at all,
with one line of gray letters underneath.
*Pending trustee approval at the November board meeting.*

The shed went up like a struck hive.

"That's the House." The peach man was out
from behind his own table. "That's Millrow.
They have drawn grass over the House."

"Who's 'they'?" the cold-brew woman demanded.
"There's no name on it.
Who sends a thing like that and doesn't sign it?"

"Trustees," somebody read off a screen.
"Pending trustee approval. Whose trustees?"

"It's a rendering," said the prophet with the fast phone,
who for once knew nothing ahead of anyone.
"Renderings are proposals. My cousin makes renderings.
Half of them never happen."

"And half of them do," Earlene said.
She had materialized at Marisol's shoulder
wearing the flat calm she used for weather and road closures,
but her whole hand was gripping her phone.
"November's ten weeks off,
and somebody already paid for that picture.
You don't pay for a picture
of a thing you're not fixing to do."

The argument broke into overlapping waves.
Marisol stood in the middle of it,
zinnias going warm in the crook of her arm.
She had never once been inside Hartwell House.
A year in town, and it had simply always been there,
the deep porch on Millrow where every couple in Ashford
had gotten engaged since before the roads were paved,
a house that belonged to everybody and to no one in particular.
She had been assuming, she realized,
that there would be time to be invited.

"My second husband proposed to me on that porch,"
a woman near the kettle was saying, hand at her throat.
"That porch is my whole marriage."

"Mine proposed to me on that porch too,"
said Verna Poteat, back at the center of things
minus Cal and minus her basket,
"and he turned out sorry as a two-dollar umbrella,
so I don't blame the porch. The porch did its part."

The laugh that went up was ragged, but it went up,
and it let the shed breathe.
Under it Verna added, not softly,
Verna having no setting that was soft,
"But hear me. I have buried a husband,
and I have never yet had to bury a house.
I don't aim to start in November."

"Okay." Marisol heard her own voice come out
in the register she used on twelve thousand parents at once,
and the nearest wave of argument broke around it.
"Whose board? Whose trustees?
Somebody knows who holds that house,
and until we do, we're all just yelling at a picture."

"First sensible thing said in this shed all day,"
Earlene said. "It's a courthouse question,
and the courthouse is closed till Monday.
What's open is Delmar's."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning when this town gets bad news on a Saturday,"
Earlene said, already turning, "it goes where the smoke is."

---

Ray had the register clipboard stripped bare
before the market crowd finished coming through his door,
delivery-app tickets shucked off in one motion
and spiked where he couldn't lose them.
He rubber-banded a fresh legal pad to the board,
tied a pen to the clip with a length of butcher's twine,
tested the knot with one hard yank,
and set the whole rig on the counter
beside the tap-to-pay stand.

"What is that," Earlene said, "and can it hold a crowd?"

"It's a place to put your name," Ray said,
"if you're of the opinion the House stays standing."
He uncapped the pen and wrote RAY, DELMAR'S PIT BAR-B-Q
on the first line, in letters legible from the door,
unasked, and turned the board to face the room.
"Because I am of that opinion. Sliced or chopped, next in line."

The line for the pen got longer than the line for the meat.
Somewhere in mid-afternoon, Verna Poteat,
waiting her turn with her checkbook out
for reasons nobody could determine,
looked at the filling page and said,
"Well. The House has itself a second guest book."

By dusk it wasn't Ray's clipboard anymore.
It was the second guest book, that fast,
the whole town using the name
as if it had come down with the deed,
and two pages had been added,
and the twine had been retied twice.

Marisol signed in the late afternoon,
brisket order in, zinnias holding on
in the shade of the car outside.
She wrote her first name large.
She had never set foot past that porch,
and she signed like a woman who fully intended to.

"You're the parents' group," Ray said, watching the pen.
It wasn't a question. "Twelve thousand of them."

"On a slow week."

"November board meeting. Ten weeks.
A town this size, twelve thousand people with opinions."
He handed her the bag, heavier than she'd paid for.
"I'd keep my Saturdays open."

"Ray," she said, "my Saturdays are the best thing I own."

---

The member queue was at four hundred and change
when she got to it, parked outside the grocery
with the air conditioning arguing against the evening.

The rendering had done what pictures do.
Everybody who had ever loved anybody in Ashford
suddenly wanted into the parents' group,
aunts and alumni and cousins from two states over,
requests stacking faster than she could read the names.

She didn't read the names.
She ran her thumb down the queue, approve, approve, approve,
brisk as dealing cards, four hundred new members
in four minutes flat, because the town was lit up
about the House, and a bigger room was a louder room,
and a louder room was exactly what November required.
This, after all, was the thing she was good at.

She'd run this group for years before she ever had an Ashford
address, a team mom at long distance since her boy's freshman
fall, twelve thousand parking questions answered from two states
over. When the dust of the divorce had finally settled, moving to
the town where her son and half her mornings already lived had
simply made sense.

Twelve thousand and four hundred, now.
She posted the photo she'd taken of the second guest book,
typed *Delmar's. The register. Bring a pen,*
pinned it, and pulled out of the lot.

She took Millrow home on purpose.
The House stood where it had always stood,
porch deep in evening shade,
entirely uninformed about November,
and somebody had already propped a bucket of bronze mums
against the bottom step.
Marisol laughed the whole length of the fence,
gave the horn one light tap as she passed,
neighbor to neighbor,
and drove home with every window down.
