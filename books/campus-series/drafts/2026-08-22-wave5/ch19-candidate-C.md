The storm crossed Ashford at two in the morning
and was slow about leaving.
Marisol lay awake in her own dark house and listened,
rain in one long push, wind going over the block twice,
one pecan-sounding crack from somewhere streets off
that she felt in her back teeth,
and then, near four, the whole argument dragging away east,
still grumbling.
The power blinked once and held.

Monday mornings were hers till noon, by her own arrangement,
and she had planned to spend this one slow.
She was up at five with the plan abandoned
and her boots by the door.

The square, when she reached it, had its lights on in the right
order.
The Checkerboard's griddle had been going since twenty to six,
the exhaust fan droning over the alley,
and the Courier's upstairs window burned over the old five-and-dime.
Standing in line she heard the tower try itself at six,
three notes, the scale, quitting where it always quit,
and off past the square Verna's light came on at the quarter hour.
Storm or no storm, the town had not moved its clock one minute,
and that steadied her more than the coffee was going to.

The Liars' Table had the storm divided into stories already,
who lost shingles, whose trampoline was in whose pasture,
and Earlene Tatum waved her over without getting up.

"Limb's through the porch roof at the House," Earlene said.
"East end. Big one. Sonny was up on it before the rain quit,
and the crew trucks are coming at half past."

"How bad?"

"Porch took it. House didn't." Earlene turned her mug a half turn.
"Delores is calling it a mercy, Verna's calling it a warning,
and both of them will get a full week out of it either way."

Marisol was at the register before her own coffee arrived.
"To go," she said. "Twelve."

"Twelve," Earlene said behind her,
in the tone she used for arithmetic that checked out.

"A crew runs twelve."

"I know what it runs," Earlene said.
"Go on, before it's all tarp and no story."

Two trays of six, hot through the cardboard,
steam standing off the lids in the cold of the lot.
The crew trucks passed her on Millrow at twenty-five past exactly,
right on the town's clock and headed the wrong direction,
diesel and headlights in the gray, and she fell in behind them.

***

Millrow at first light was rain-washed and loud with birds,
wet pecan leaves shoaled along both curbs,
and the House stood behind its two oaks
with a limb the size of a johnboat through the east end
of the porch roof.
The supper's string lights had ridden the storm out
without losing a bulb; the porch roof had been less lucky.
The mum on the bottom step sat under a lapful of wet leaves,
unmoved, unclaimed, still known to everybody.

Sonny Dillard was already on the roof in his off-shift clothes,
walking the limb end to end with a lumber crayon,
marking it every few feet and cutting nothing yet.

"Bind's here, and here," he called down, tapping his marks.
"She's loaded like a spring.
Cut her out of order and the butt end rolls through that lattice.
Nobody touches a saw till I'm done being boring."

"Boring's the reason he's still got ten fingers," Dewayne said,
from the tarp pile.

"Nine and a half," Sonny said, and went on marking.

The chainsaw caught on the second pull and settled in,
and Sonny took the limb apart from the top down,
small and boring, exactly as promised,
every piece coming off the roof the size a man could carry,
and not one thing rolled anywhere.

Under the porch, Ronnie out of bay three had two screw jacks
set on cribbing and a string line pulled corner to corner.
He gave the near jack a quarter-turn and watched the line.
Another quarter. Watched the line.

"How far out is she?" Cal said, from the steps.

"Inch and three-quarters at the post," Ronnie said.
"She'll come back. Sills held. It's the roof that quit."

"Roof had help," Sonny called down.

Marisol came up the walk with the trays,
and the crew made a slot for her without stopping.

"Coffee," she said. "Twelve. Somebody tell me where to stand."

"Out of the drop zone and next to me," Dewayne said.
"In that order."

Cal came around the tailgate of his truck
with a pry bar in one hand and rain still in his hair,
flannel shoved back off his forearms,
the T-shirt under it doing honest work across the shoulders,
and his face, in that scrubbed gray light,
had no business being this good a reason to hold a tray steady.

He took a cup off the tray by the rim, under her fingers,
the weight of it passing from her hand to his,
and nothing about it would have held up as evidence anywhere.

"Appreciate it," he said, to the tray.

"It's coffee," she said, to the porch. "Try to be worth it."

The look that went with the cup ran long,
gray morning, steam off the lid,
long past what a job site had any use for,
and Ray's truck came grinding up Millrow with the window down,
Ray hollering before the brake was set.

"Storm plates! Biscuits and two kinds of gravy,
nobody pays, and nobody argues with me on my own tailgate."

"It's my tailgate," Cal said.

"It's a catering site now," Ray said. "Get a plate."

***

She did not leave.
There was a version of the morning where she set down the trays,
said a public word about the porch, and went home useful.
She put her tote in the car and her hands in gloves instead.

The brush line ran from the porch to Sonny's trailer,
and she got in it, and inside twenty minutes she had rebuilt it,
turned it so nobody walked the wet bricks backward,
staged the big wood where Sonny's truck could reach it,
and the line quit bunching and started flowing,
and nobody said one thing about it,
which was how she knew it took.

By eight she had the names.
Ronnie out of bay three, who ran the jacks.
Dewayne, who ran the tarp lines and the conversation.
Mack, who ran the wheelbarrow and agreed with nobody.

The opinions came free with the names.

"Worst tree God ever made," Dewayne said, feeding the line.
"A pecan will drop a limb on a windless day out of pure meanness.
This one at least had the manners to wait for weather."

"Best smoke in the county," Ronnie said, from under the porch.

"Didn't say it wasn't useful. I said it was mean.
Plenty of useful things are mean. My uncle's mean."

"Your uncle ain't useful," Ronnie said.

The laugh went down the brush line and into the wheelbarrow.

"It's heavy, is what it is," Mack said.

"You bring coffee to every tree fall in the county?" Ronnie asked
her, over the string line.

"Only the porches I'm fond of," Marisol said.
"The coffee's so y'all let me stay."

"Stay's earned," Ronnie said. "Coffee helps."

Ray's biscuits went around at half past eight,
and Dewayne ate his standing on the tailgate of Cal's truck
like a man giving a toast.

"Row barns get new roofs this winter," he said. "It's in writing.
Pending, but in writing.
First dry roof that shop's seen since I hired on."

Marisol handed him a second biscuit and kept the rest to herself.

The tarp went over the roof hole at nine,
and Cal went up the extension ladder with the last grommets.
She stepped to the ladder without being asked
and put her weight on the rails,
which any of the twelve would have done,
and he came down the last rungs with his hand closing the rail
just above hers, boot landing beside her wrist,
labor by every measure a witness owned.

"Two more grommets on the west edge," he said,
which on a job site was thank you.

"I'll allow it," she said.

***

She carried the empty trays back to his tailgate at ten,
and the clipboards were lying by the toolbox,
the top one work orders, grease-thumbed, half checked off.
The corner of a second one showed underneath.
She moved the top one an inch. She should not have, and did.

Twelve names, in pencil, in his hand.
First and last names, no part numbers, no dates,
no column for anything a store sold.
She read down as far as the fourth name,
a name she had heard hollered down the brush line all morning,
and quit reading.
She knew what the list was.
He had told her at midnight in a lit shop
what stood behind all of this if Boyd's money didn't land,
and she had carried the number around all week
like a stone that fit her pocket.
A number was one weight.
Names, in pencil, in his own hand, were another,
and her chest took the second one whole.

She set the top clipboard back square, exactly as found,
and went and hauled brush until the weight worked loose.

***

By noon the porch was coming level.
Ronnie ran his two jacks a quarter-turn about,
calling the string line low, patient as a tide chart,
a new header went in over the east bay,
and the tower counted noon down the hill
while the string came true.

She watched it settle, the last coffee going cold in her hand,
and the thought she had been circling all week
came all the way in and sat down.

Cal was at the corner post with a handful of eightpenny nails.

"Look at what today took," she said, low, for two people.
"A sawyer before breakfast. Carpenters all morning.
A roofer by Thursday, a mason under that pier before spring,
painters when the weather turns.
Every trade Boyd keeps promising this county his decks will hire,
and this house needs all of them, all winter, starting now."

He thumbed a nail out of his palm and didn't drive it.

"What if the restoration is the winter?" she said.

He looked at the porch the whole length of the question,
and then at her.

"Keep that one inside the property line a while," he said,
and it did not sound like a no.

Sometime after that the crew quit narrating her.
Mack handed her the next load without checking her grip first.
Ronnie hollered "brush line" in her general direction
same as at anybody in gloves,
and when the water jug came around it came to her in her turn,
not first, not offered twice,
which from that crew was the highest compliment on offer.

And on the east lattice, above the sawhorses and the wreckage,
the wife's rose has opened — October, holding to form,
the storm having failed to take a single bud —
and Marisol tells him so in his own doorway:
"She's still winning."
He drives the next nail a little slower,
and nobody on the crew learns why.

***

She left when the tarp was tied off and Sonny's truck
had taken the last of the limb down Millrow,
and she drove home with a window cracked to the scrubbed cool
and sawdust riding her hair the whole six blocks.

It was the hall mirror that told on her.
She had walked past it and come back, which she never did.
Sawdust from crown to collar, pale gold in the dark of it,
a scratch across one knuckle, pecan sap on one sleeve,
and no knot left anywhere in the plan.
She turned her head one way, then the other,
and gave the whole business a fair inspection.
She had spent a year deciding things on purpose
and in her own favor.

"Keep it," she told the mirror,
and wore the sawdust till supper.
