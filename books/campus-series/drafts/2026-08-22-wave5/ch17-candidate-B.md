The last run of string lights got tied off a little after six,
eave to oak to porch post, no gap left anywhere,
and the House's lawn came up gold from fence to fence.
Two Tuesdays ago Marisol had climbed down from half a job
and left that gold burning on empty grass.
Tonight there was no empty grass to be had.
Forty tables. Four hundred chairs.
Casserole steam and fried chicken under the live oaks,
a fiddler tuning on the porch steps,
and above all of it the House, dark and tall,
front door pulled to for the night,
standing over the brightest lawn in the county.

Delores Fountain-Mabry had said it over sheet cake at Mt. Nebo.
Put the whole town at one table,
and make November look it in the eye.
The town had taken her at her word,
and Delores stood behind the dessert table directing everything
at reading-circle volume, thirty years of elementary school
in her spine, obeyed from forty feet.

"Sheet cakes here. Pies with Verna.
If it wobbles, it's a jello salad, and it goes to the end."

The sign-up app had assigned the county its sides.
The county had read its assignments carefully
and brought casseroles.
Delores triaged them off the card tables without looking down.
"Sweet potato travels. Far end.
Hash brown to table six, they've got growing boys.
If one more broccoli-rice comes up this walk,
it goes home with Peanut."

"I'll take it," Peanut Kyzer said, from the drinks table
at the boxwood end, where he had three coolers,
a rented urn of sweet tea, and a system.
Jars stood ranked by size. Seltzers slept in the ice.
"I learned about running dry in an establishment
I won't name on a church-adjacent lawn. Not twice."

Odell Fountain had the tables,
every one of them borrowed off a fellowship hall,
and he set them to the map he kept entirely in his head.
"Providence Primitive along the walk.
They go home first and I won't hear about it after.
Mt. Nebo under the oaks. Their legs sit level on the roots."
He had carried them off the trucks one under each arm,
and he knew which was whose by the paint on the legs,
in the dark, without a label anywhere.

Marisol ran the cake tables,
which Delores had assigned her in August language.
"Steady hands, no doubles. It's still an honor. Still act like it."

She cut corners with icing on two sides for anybody under twelve
and worked the line by name, and the line worked her back.

"That dress is out past what a cake table calls for,"
Earlene Tatum said, coming through for seconds.

"It's washable," Marisol said, and moved the line along.

The tape over the extension cord kept curling up
at the cold end, and twice she knelt in the good dress
and pressed it flat before somebody's grandmother
could find it with a shoe.
Nobody saw her do it either time,
which was how she knew it counted.

The evening cooled fast once the sun quit.
By full dark the lawn had gone to jackets,
the fiddler had graduated from tuning to waltzes,
and the parents' group was passing around somebody's photo
of the lights with four hundred comments under it,
two states' worth of kitchens saying it looked like home.
The photo had no badge on it anywhere. Neither did she.
She'd quit checking.

Across the lawn, across the boxwoods,
Cal Sutter sat at the far end of the longest table.

She had known where he was since he got there,
the same as she knew where the porch was.
October had dressed him better than August ever managed,
canvas jacket open over flannel,
and the string lights had found the gray at his temples
and were calling it silver.
Nobody sat within a chair of him on either side.
The town had left a moat around a man
it used to leave a path to,
and he ate Delores's supper in the middle of it,
back straight, because staying home
would have been a sentence said out loud,
and he had come.

What she wanted was small and impossible.
She wanted his eyes for the length of one slow breath.
That was all.
The terms were hers; she'd written them herself at midnight
in a lit shop with her keys still in her fist,
and the terms said the width of a lawn and not one name.

He looked up from his plate,
over the heads and the boxwoods and the whole gold middle
of the supper, and found her already there.

Neither face moved. That was the discipline.
His eyes and hers with a town between them,
boxwood and buttercream on the cooling air,
one long breath and most of another,
and then Delores said "cake knife" at reading-circle volume
from two tables over, and Marisol turned,
and the lawn came back up around her.

It settled nothing.
She carried it back to the cakes anyway
and was rich the rest of the hour.

***

Rex Boyd arrived at full dark.

November came up the House's front walk in a canvas jacket
that had never once seen mud,
shaking hands at volume one the whole way,
and the tables turned toward him one at a time.

Tick Moran was live off the porch rail,
WDSS 1340 on a card table with a mixer and one good microphone,
narrating the pies to the county between waltzes.
"Update from Millrow. The buttermilk is gone.
The chess is going. Y'all missed the buttermilk."

Boyd borrowed the microphone like a man borrowing
a neighbor's hose, easy, and already forgiven for it.
"I won't keep anybody from that dessert table long," he said,
warm as supper.
"I've been to a lot of nice evenings in a lot of towns
that don't know how to do this. Look at this lawn.
This is the town the whole project is for.
So raise whatever Peanut gave you.
To Ashford. To keeping her safe.
And to everything that's coming. To progress
with a porch light on."

The applause came up easy, because it was a supper
and he'd kept it short.

Then, beside the cake table, Coach Merritt made his mistake.

Merritt was built like the far end of a weight room,
straight from practice with the whistle still on his neck,
and he said it loud enough to lend out.
"Doc'll tell you. Safety's the whole point.
She writes that word on half my roster's paperwork.
Cole. Back me up."

Aisha Cole set down her jar with a click Marisol knew.
"Back you up on what, exactly."

"Progress. New lights, new training room. You of all people."

"Do not hang my license on your lawn plans, Merritt."

"It's a compliment."

"It's a permission slip," Aisha said, "and I'm not signing it.
I keep people breathing. I don't do real estate.
You want the word safety, you can have it at my office,
in writing, about a hamstring."

"You have fought me on every call since June—"

"Because you have been wrong on every call since June."

The fiddle stopped mid-tune, bow still up.
Forty tables went from waltz to courtroom
without moving a chair.
The two of them stood toe to toe by the sheet cakes,
a doctor and a coach at full wingspan,
and Marisol stepped in beside Aisha and said nothing at all,
which said the only thing it needed to.

"Well," Tunk Ferrell offered, into the quiet,
"the fiddle needed the rest."

Nobody laughed. The quiet stayed where it was.

Delores arrived with recess in her voice.
"You two are one raised fork from a folding-chair league,
and not at my supper.
Coach, the pudding is that way. Walk toward it.
Fiddler. You were paid in pie. Play."

The fiddle came back. The coach went toward the pudding.
Aisha stayed exactly where she was, at Marisol's shoulder,
breathing like the argument had been cardio,
and picked her jar back up.
"He gets under every nerve I own," she said.

"How tall is he again?" Marisol said.

"Cut the cake, Pruett."

Across the boxwoods, Cal had watched Aisha's whole stand
from the middle of his moat,
and for the space of a breath his eyes came to Marisol's
and went back to his plate.
That one helped too.

Boyd let the whole weather system pass.
Then he came in over the top of it, volume one, never louder,
the recovery so smooth you had to know him to know it was one.

"That's this town for you," he said into the microphone,
fond as a family story.
"Everybody in it cares out loud.
And while I've got this back, let me say the thing
I drove over to say.
None of what's coming gets done without partners.
The university knows it. I know it.
So I want to thank our facilities director, publicly,
for his partnership.
There he is. Y'all know him.
Whole campus runs through that man's key ring."

The applause came up patchy.
Some of it was polite and some of it never started,
and at the end of the longest table Cal sat still
in the middle of his moat with every string light on him,
and did not stand, and did not wave,
and let the word partnership be hung on him
in front of four hundred chairs.

Marisol kept her eyes on the cake knife.
Here was what the lawn did not know and could not be told.
There was a page across town that had wanted his name on it
by a Friday, close of business,
a Friday the rest of this lawn had never heard of.
That Friday had come and gone and taken nothing with it.
And this was Boyd's answer, this toast,
gracious in front of the whole county,
gratitude aimed the way only a patient man can aim it.
Three people on the grass knew what the toast was for.
The town, applauding or not, was not one of them.

Delores took the microphone back with a schoolteacher's thanks,
and then held it out across the dessert table.

"Marisol kept every list this supper ran on.
She's better at names than the rest of us put together.
Come say the thank-yous, and then we'll cut these cakes."

"You're live, sugar," Tick said, off the rail.
"1340 and every kitchen in the county."

Marisol took the microphone.
Her pulse went loud in her ears and stayed there.

The thank-yous were easy.
Delores, for the whole idea of it.
Odell, for forty tables that would all get home.
Peanut, for not running dry.
Birdie, for flowers nobody had a budget for,
zinnias down every table, last of the season.
The pies, by name, because pies in this county had names.
The lawn applauded itself, warm and fed,
the fiddler playing small under her voice,
and it would have been the easiest thing in the world
to hand the microphone back.

Delores had not asked for a supper.
She'd asked for a town that could look November in the eye,
and November was standing by the porch rail in a good jacket,
admiring what he'd built out of somebody else's evening.

"One more thing," Marisol said.
"Mr. Boyd thanked our facilities director tonight
for his partnership.
So I've got one question before we cut these cakes,
and I'd like it answered out loud,
in front of the same town that heard the toast."

She found the one face she had spent the whole evening
not looking at, across the boxwoods,
under all that finished gold,
and asked him there, live, on the open air.

"Will you sign it?"

One second of dead air on the radio is a stumble.
Three is trouble.
At five, Tick's hand started for the mixer and stopped,
because the lawn had stopped first,
four hundred chairs and not one scrape,
the fiddle gone out of the air,
the whole gold supper holding one breath.

Cal sat where he had sat all evening
and looked back at her across the boxwoods
and did not answer.

She had seen him still before, at a church table in September,
and had learned that still, on that man, was not rest.
The lawn only saw a man not answering.
She kept her eyes where she had put them and did not help him,
because helping him was the one thing on this earth
she was not allowed to do.

Seven seconds.
Nobody moved to rescue it. She didn't either.

Nine.

The question stood out there where she had set it,
live on 1340, in every kitchen in the county,
and the answer did not come.

Ten.

Eleven.
