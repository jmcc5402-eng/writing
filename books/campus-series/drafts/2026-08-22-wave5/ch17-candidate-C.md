The card table quit mid-cake, and Marisol caught both.
One hip against the folding leg, both hands flat under the board,
the sheet cake level and the crisis over
before the frosting ever heard about it.

"Third table tonight," Earlene Tatum said, from the lawn chair
she had set dead center of the grass and had no plans to leave.
"They know you'll catch them. Word gets around."

"Then send me the tables that haven't heard."

The supper had outgrown the lawn by six.
Delores Fountain-Mabry had asked for the whole town at one table,
and the whole town had taken her at her word and then some,
card tables end to end from the porch steps to the boxwoods,
four hundred folding chairs scraping down the rows,
casserole steam and fried chicken going up under the live oaks,
the cake tables holding the east flank
in a cloud of boxwood and buttercream.
The sign-up had asked Ashford for forty dishes.
Ashford had brought sixty-one, no two of them labeled
and at least five of them contested.

"Whoever brought the four bean casseroles," Earlene announced,
"one of you is going home vindicated
and three of you are going home with beans."

"Lot of chairs," Sonny Dillard said, coming past with four more.

Delores wanted the chairs in rows, and got rows.
The fiddler Delores had produced from somewhere in the Mt. Nebo
congregation took the porch steps and tuned at the dusk,
and up the hill the tower struck seven
while the serving line was still going strong.
The evening cooled fast once the sun dropped,
October keeping its schedule,
and by full dark the lawn was in jackets and nobody had gone home.

The string lights were finished.
Every run of them, porch to oaks to the far fence corner,
and when they came on at dark Marisol stood a minute at the switch.
Two Tuesdays ago, half the job had hung over an empty lawn,
gold on the grass with nobody under it.
Tonight the whole town stood under them,
and she had climbed the ladder for most of both.

What she wanted out of the evening
was not a thing she could put on a sign-up sheet,
and she had quit pretending otherwise on the drive over.
Ten seconds. Ten seconds of one man's eyes, from any distance,
in a county where she was no longer allowed to say his name.

The group had the supper before the supper had dessert,
somebody's photo of the long tables going out two states,
mothers who had never stood on Millrow saying it looked like home.
Her own comment rode the middle of the scroll, unpinned,
one voice out of twelve thousand.
Whoever had taken her badge had not taken Thursday.
The notebook rode in her tote under the cake server,
unopened all night, and that was fine.
Tonight the town was writing its own page.

She had known where he was all evening,
the same as she knew where the porch was,
without once being caught learning it.
Cal Sutter kept the far side of the boxwoods,
his crew and their wives at the last table under the oaks,
canvas jacket over the green polo against the cold,
the lights finding the gray at his temples and calling it silver,
and doing him a run of favors
she had no legal way to acknowledge.
He had carried chairs before supper, four to an arm, both arms,
and taken the heavy end of the serving table alone
while three men held the light end and advised him.
At the cake table a grandmother had watched the whole operation
over her reading glasses.

"If I dropped my keys in front of that man," she said,
to the table at large, "I'd drop them twice."

Then, over the heads of the serving line,
across the top of the boxwood hedge, he looked at her,
and she looked back.
Ten seconds, from forty feet, in front of four hundred people,
none of whom caught one thing changing hands.
It settled nothing. Nothing was available to settle.
It helped anyway, out of all proportion to the size of it,
and then Delores's voice came down the rows,
"Cake table! You're losing to the pies,"
and the ten seconds were over,
and Marisol cut cake with both hands steady.

***

The fiddler was two tunes in when Rex Boyd came up the walk.

He came gracious, bareheaded, jacket the expensive kind of plain,
shaking hands up one row and down the next
at the volume he took everywhere, which was one.
Boyd never got louder; rooms leaned to him,
and the lawn did it too, table by table as he passed.

Marisol watched him come and did the private arithmetic.
The paper behind that survey had wanted a signature by Friday,
close of business, and Friday had come and gone
with the line under the two words still blank.
She was one of two people on this lawn who knew it.
The rest of the county believed the thing signed and done,
and Boyd, who knew better than anybody that it wasn't,
had shown up anyway, gracious as Sunday,
because that was what a missed deadline looked like
on a man who could afford to miss nothing.

Tick Moran had his remote rig on the porch rail,
the station banner zip-tied to the spindles,
WDSS 1340 carrying the supper live.
"For those of you at home," Tick told the county, low,
"the whole town is here, and the chicken is holding."

Delores gave Boyd the mic, because a town hands the mic
to the man everybody is already watching,
and he took it easily, one volume.

"I won't make a speech. Delores would take this back."
The laugh came, and he waited it out kindly.
"I'll just say what I see.
I see a town that shows up for its own,
and I love that about this place.
And what I want hasn't changed since summer.
Safety first, progress right behind it.
Ask your own Dr. Cole what an old building costs a town
in a bad winter."
He raised his cup of sweet tea.
"To a safe winter, folks. And to November."

"Don't spend my name on that, Mr. Boyd."
Aisha Cole stood beside the cake table
with a plate she hadn't started.
She didn't say it loud, and it carried anyway.
"Medicine doesn't cosign real estate."

"Oh, come on, Doc." Coach Merritt was three chairs down,
already on his feet.
"The man said safety. You preach safety all week."

"I preach it where it lives.
It does not live in a rendering."

"It lives in facilities. Which is what he's building."

"When it comes out of my mouth, it's medicine.
When it comes out of that mic, it's marketing,
and I don't lend out the difference."

"You have fought me on every single call since June."

"Because you've been wrong since June.
It's been a long season."

The fiddler quit mid-phrase to listen,
which everybody heard, because a tune stopping
is louder than a tune,
and the laugh rolled the length of the lawn
while the coach stood there deciding whether he'd been hit.

"Fiddle," Delores said, and the fiddle started back
where it had left off.

Aisha set her plate down, came around the cake table,
and took up the second server beside Marisol
without being asked, which from Aisha was a whole speech.
"Insufferable," she said, cutting even squares.
"And I am not sorry."

Boyd let all of it go by like a man agreeing about the weather.

"Doctor Cole keeps this county honest," he said, warm, into the mic,
"and I'd expect nothing less.
So let me thank somebody who doesn't say much,
which around here counts as a virtue."
He turned, easy, and found the far table under the oaks,
and the whole lawn's attention went with him.
"Our facilities director.
I want to thank that man for his partnership these last weeks.
Nobody in this county knows better what winter costs,
and nobody's counsel has been worth more to me.
To partnership."

He lifted the tea, one volume, never louder,
and four hundred faces turned to the man under the oaks.

Cal stood where he had been standing,
coffee in one hand, face level as a poured slab.
He did not raise the cup and he did not put it down,
and he said nothing at all.
And the town, which could not see the top of any page,
watched a man fail to contradict the word partnership,
and drew the conclusion Boyd had come to collect.

Marisol kept cutting cake.
Don't sign, don't resign, don't explain.
The terms were hers as much as his.
She had stood in that shop at midnight and helped set them,
and this was what they cost, said out loud on her own lawn.
A gracious man at one volume, spending Cal's silence
in front of every kitchen Cal kept warm all winter,
and the only two people who knew the silence was a held line
were not allowed to stand next to each other.

Across the boxwoods, in the middle of all that turning,
his eyes came to her once, brief, over the hedge,
and went back to nothing.
That made twice.
It would have to last.

***

Delores was halfway down her thank-you list
when the mic started traveling.

"And Providence Primitive for the tables,
which go home tonight, gentlemen, not Sunday.
And the pie women. And Ray, who fed a county at cost
and will deny it if you thank him."
Delores consulted no notes. She had never needed notes.
"And the lights and the sign-ups ran themselves,
the way a school runs itself,
which is to say a woman did it.
Marisol. Stand up and let the town thank you."

The applause was kind, and the mic came down the table
hand to hand and arrived warm.

She stood in the light spill with it
and thanked the table crews and the pie women and Delores,
and the applause came again,
and she was one sentence from sitting back down
inside a town that still mostly loved her.
She looked out over the lawn instead.
Sixty-one casseroles, four hundred chairs,
the finished lights, and above all of it the House,
tall and dark over its own gold grass, door pulled to for the night.

Her hands were steady on the mic. Nothing else was.

After tonight, nobody watching would ever again believe
those two were on the same side.
It would cost her exactly what it bought.
She had known that since the shop, and she did it anyway.

"One more thing," she said,
"since the whole town's at one table and the radio's on."
Down the rail, Tick's hand moved to his board and stopped.
"Mr. Boyd thanked our facilities director for his partnership.
And every pocket on this lawn has been carrying
a picture of a page around for two weeks.
I have looked at that picture as long as anybody here,
and nobody in this county has yet shown me ink."
The lawn had gone very quiet,
the good-supper kind of quiet going over into the other kind.
"So I'll ask what a town gets to ask
the man whose name is typed under those two words."

She turned toward the oaks, mic up, voice level.

"Will you sign it?"

The fiddler let his bow down off the strings.
On the porch rail the station's little board sat open,
sending the lawn out live across the county,
and Tick Moran, a man with no known tolerance for dead air,
let the dead air stand.

She knew the true answer.
She was the only soul on that lawn
who knew what it was costing him not to say it,
and the town heard the only thing the town could hear,
a man not answering.

Four hundred chairs, and not one of them creaked.
Under the oaks the man the question belonged to
turned his head and looked up at the House,
dark above all that gold,
and the whole town looked with him,
and the question stayed out on the open air, live,
one second, and three, and five,
and the answer did not come,
and kept not coming.
