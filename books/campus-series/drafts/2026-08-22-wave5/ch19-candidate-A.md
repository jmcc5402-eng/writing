The storm came through after midnight and did not stay.
Marisol lay awake for the loud half hour of it,
and in the middle of the wind, downhill toward Millrow,
one crack landed that was not thunder.
By first light the group had the photograph.

The pecan limb had come through the porch roof of Hartwell House,
big around as a culvert, still wearing half its leaves,
butt end up through the shingles like the mast of something sunk.
The picture was taken from the sidewalk in gray light,
and under it the group was already being the group,
forty comments deep in prayer hands by six,
every one of them two states away from a tarp.

She could not pin it. The badge was two weeks gone.
She got dressed instead.
Monday mornings were hers, by her own arrangement,
and this one had just told her what it was for.

The tower tried itself at six o'clock, same as any morning,
into a sky the storm had rinsed clean,
and at six twenty-five, right on the town's clock,
the crew trucks came up Millrow with their lights on.
She watched them pass from the Checkerboard's counter,
two cardboard trays steaming in front of her.
She had ordered coffee for twelve.
The number had come to her at the register
from nowhere she could point to,
and she paid it and let it ride.

***

The House had its whole answer forming by seven.

Cal's trucks stood backed onto the wet grass, tailgates down.
Two ladders were up. A tarp ran from the ridge to the rail,
snapping full like a sail when the leftover wind got under it,
and Sonny Dillard, off a night of storm calls,
was limbing the fallen wood with his own chainsaw,
working from the small end in toward trouble.
The lawn smelled of rain and torn pecan, green and sharp,
with two-stroke smoke riding over it.
Ray had come up from Delmar's with two boxes of biscuits
and set them out on a pair of card tables
that had never made it back from the supper.

"Coffee," Marisol said, to the lawn in general,
and work stopped in order of seniority.

Cal came down off the porch for his.
Flannel, canvas, sawdust to the elbow already.
"The Checkerboard's," she said. "Not mine. Your crew is safe."
"They'll be grateful."
He took the cup, and his fingers crossed hers on the cardboard
a half second longer than the handoff required,
and then he was reading the roof again
and she was pouring for Sonny,
and any witness on that sidewalk saw a volunteer with a tray.

"Limb's been dead two years," Sonny said, taking his cup.
"Storm didn't kill anything. Storm just called it."

"How do you know two years?"

"Bark," Sonny said, and did not expand.

"Can the porch be saved?"

"Porch, yes. Roof's a patch till a roofer. Beam wants jacks."

"Jacks first," Cal said, from the porch. "Pretty later."

She had meant to leave the trays and go.
She held a ladder instead, because somebody had to,
and then she hauled brush, because it was walking past her anyway,
and by eight she had quit narrating her own exit
and put her hair up with a pencil off the biscuit table.

"You staying?" Travis said.

"Till the coffee's gone."

"Coffee's gone," DeShawn said, hopeful.

"There's a second tray in the car."

"Then you're staying," Ronnie said, and handed her the rake.

"Where does the brush go?" she asked, first armload.

"Butts to the road, long ends one way," Sonny said.
"Anything else is a bird's nest."
Before she was three rungs up the first time,
Cal came past without a word,
kicked the ladder feet square on the wet ground,
tested the rail once with one hand, and went on.
He had not looked at her. The ladder did not move all morning.

She learned three names before the second tray ran dry,
and every opinion that came riveted to them.

"Marisol," she said, pouring. "Volunteer."

"Ronnie. Bay three." She did not ask which bay.
It sounded load-bearing.

"Travis."

"DeShawn. Two sugars in that one, please ma'am."

"This is a tray, not a bar."

"Ray's got sugar," Travis said. "Ray's got everything."

"Delmar's is closed," Ray said, from the card tables.
"This is me, at church."

Ronnie held that a tarp should be tied, never bungeed,
and was hearing no appeals on it.
Travis loaded brush butt-first
and defended the method like family land.
DeShawn ran the nail gun, had views on biscuit order,
and pronounced the Checkerboard's coffee, publicly, over Delmar's,
with Ray standing right there.

"Say that again nearer the biscuits," Ray said.
"See what happens."

"You load butt-first or you fight it twice," Travis said.

"You fight brush twice anyway," DeShawn said. "That's brush."

"My cousin two counties over lost a pontoon in the '09 storm,"
Ronnie said. "Never did find the motor."

Nobody answered that.

"Biscuits do not hold," Ray called.
"Delmar's does not run a second seating. Eat now."

Out of Travis's truck, WDSS ran the swap shop,
Tick selling somebody's tiller at auction speed,
and the work went on around it.

The survey came up once, out by the brush trailer,
pitched for under the chainsaw, with the boss on the roof.

"My mama still says he signed it," Travis said.

"Your mama wasn't listening Thursday night," DeShawn said.

"She listened. She says a pause is not a no."

"It's not a yes either."

"That's what makes it a pause," Travis said,
and they let the chainsaw have the rest of it.

Marisol stacked brush and said not one word.
She had not said his name in public in two weeks,
and a brush pile was public,
and the cost of that had quit surprising her without getting cheaper.

***

She went to the tailgate at ten for the second round of cups.
The clipboard was lying by the biscuit box, face up.
She was not snooping. She was reaching past it.

Names, in Cal's block capitals, one to a line.
No work orders. No dates. No column for hours.
Twelve of them.
Ronnie sat fourth from the top.
She had known which bay was his since the first tray.
Travis was on it. DeShawn was on it.
Half the coffee she had poured that morning was on it.

*Twelve jobs out of this shop.*
He had said it once, at midnight, under one lamp.
The number she had paid for at the Checkerboard register
had not come from nowhere.
She had been carrying his arithmetic around all morning
and calling it coffee.

She set the fresh cups down on the tailgate,
squared the tray to the edge,
and took Ronnie his while it was hot.

***

The jacks went in under the beam at eleven.
Cal ran them a half turn at a time, corners in order,
watching a string line he'd tacked across the porch face,
and the roofline came up out of its sag so slowly
that the sidewalk audience missed it happening.
He had shed the flannel by nine.
The T-shirt underneath had an opinion about his shoulders,
and two women from First Methodist, stopped on the sidewalk
to donate their assessment of the framing,
had not once looked at the framing.
He came down with the cut ends the chainsaw sent up,
two at a time, one-handed,
and made the weight look like a rumor.

"Level," Travis called, off the string.

"Level enough for church," Ronnie said.

"It's level," Cal said.

"Then y'all sit down and eat," Ray called.
"A porch is not dinner."

By noon the porch stood where a hundred years had set it.
Marisol stood on the walk with a cup going cold in her hand
and watched the beam come true,
and the thing she had been circling since the jacks went in
was finally ready to be said out loud.
She came to the tailgate, where Cal was writing the lumber list,
and said it quietly, for the two of them and no lawn.

"Look what one limb took. One morning and half a crew.
A roofer for the patch. A framer for the beam.
Plaster inside, before winter. Paint.
Wiring, if anybody's honest.
Every trade Boyd's decks keep promising this town,
one porch just asked for by name."

Cal looked at the porch and did not say any numbers.

"The whole House is that porch," she said.
"Top to bottom. What if the restoration is the winter?"

He was quiet long enough that she heard the tarp go once.

"Who else has that?"

"You. The porch."

"Keep it that way till tomorrow night."

"What's tomorrow night?"

"Side of the House, after dark. I won't leave a light on."

"I'll bring the coffee," she said.

The look after that ran a breath past volunteer,
and Sonny's chainsaw fired on the far side of the lawn
and took the moment with it.

***

The decking went back down through the early afternoon.
DeShawn ran the nail gun and Cal drove the edge rows by hand,
two spare nails riding the corner of his mouth,
and she made herself quit noticing his mouth about it.
Somewhere after one o'clock the crew quit translating for her.
Travis handed her the flat bar without looking.
Her hand was already up.
Nobody remarked on it,
which from that crew was the entire ceremony.

"Most volunteers last till the biscuits run out," Ronnie said,
handing her the good gloves, the pair without the hole.
"You're still here."

"The coffee's still here."

"That's what I said," Ronnie said.

"You on tomorrow?" Travis said.

"Tomorrow's got its own plans," she said, and kept raking.

She carried the last cups up onto the new decking
to collect the empties off the rail,
and stopped in the open front door with the tray on her hip,
Cal nailing off the last run at the threshold beside her.
And on the east lattice, above the sawhorses and the wreckage,
the wife's rose had opened — October, holding to form,
the storm having failed to take a single bud —
and Marisol told him so in his own doorway:
"She's still winning."
He drove the next nail a little slower,
and nobody on the crew learned why.

She counted eleven cups back into the trays at two o'clock.
The twelfth was up on the roof somewhere, holding somebody's nails,
and she decided the House could keep it.

***

Her front hall was cool and dim after all that sun.
The mirror caught her before her keys were out of her hand.

Sawdust in her hair.
Not a fleck. A morning's worth,
gold all through the dark of it where the hall light found it.
There had been a version of her, recent,
who would have fixed it at the first red light.
She leaned in and turned her head, one side, then the other,
and gave it the full inspection it deserved.
Then she set her keys in the bowl
and left every bit of it exactly where it was.
