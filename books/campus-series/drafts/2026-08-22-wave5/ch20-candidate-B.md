Tuesday night he came up the back of Millrow with his headlights off,
put the truck in the dark behind the House,
and let himself in at the side door on his own key.
The front door stayed shut and dark to the whole street.
Somewhere down the hill the county slept on
believing what it believed about him,
and for three more weeks that was the job.

He stood a minute in the back hall with the door pulled to.
Cold came off the window glass, old plaster and lemon oil,
and the house held its warmth around him like a habit
it had no plans of breaking.
He ran Monday over again while he stood there.
*She's still winning.*
Her voice, low, in his own doorway,
the crew ten feet off and none of them the wiser,
and two days had not worn one edge off of it.

Her knock came at half past nine, two knuckles on the side door,
and Marisol stepped in out of the wind
with rolled paper under one arm and the thermos in her other hand,
and October came in with her, leaf smell and river cold,
her hair pinned against the weather and losing to it.
He shut the door behind her and threw the bolt.

"Verna's porch light is on late," she said, handing him the thermos.
"I went around by the church lot like a teenager.
Earlene asked me at the market why I've been looking rested.
I told her vitamins.
I have now lied to Earlene Tatum about vitamins, Cal.
That woman has buried two mayors."

"Terms are terms."

"Terms are terms," she said, "and some nights I hate every one."

The parlor stood rigged as they'd left it,
two work lights on yellow legs aimed at the ceiling
so the glow stayed off the windows,
moths ticking at the hot glass,
the wind coming at the shutters in long pulls off the river.
The counter-drawings rode in from the truck bench every night
and rode back out before first light,
and on a sheet of plywood across two sawhorses
the House was coming back into existence in pencil,
sills and spans and the staircase in section.
Real drawings. Real numbers. Nothing borrowed.

"Say the rule again," she said, unscrewing the thermos.
"I like the rule."

"A finding gets answered by a finding of equal standing.
Stamped drawings, stamped numbers, no shortcuts."

"And a ladder."

"And a ladder," Cal said. "He allowed the ladder."

They worked.
He climbed and measured and called it down,
rise and run, header and sill, joist and span,
and she wrote what he called in a column that never wandered,
pencil quick and sure, the one steady sound under the wind.
When the parlor door had to come off its hinges
to get at the frame behind it,
he lifted it off one-handed and stood it against the wall.

"You cannot swing forty pounds of door around one-handed
while I am writing small numbers," she said.
"I've just made an eight that will read as a six
and sink the whole case."

"I'll take the next one off slower."

"That is the opposite of the help you think it is."

The drawings wanted history as much as they wanted inches.
When the sills had last been done.
What the roof had on it and when it had gone on.
What the House had spent on itself
across a hundred years of being minded.
He went to the hall table where the guest book lived,
opened the drawer beneath it,
and brought back the maintenance ledger.

Cloth boards gone soft at the corners,
MAINTENANCE up the spine in old ink,
and inside, a century of the House's keeping in a dozen hands.
*Reglazed the parlor sash.*
*Packed the well pump.*
*New treads, back stairs.*
Line after line, hand after hand,
the dates keeping to the margin the whole way down,
stewards and wives and one man who wrote in carpenter's pencil.

The last written page he could have found with the light off.
Small square writing, steady to its final line,
and then the writing quit,
and the rest of the page stood empty,
and every page after it stood empty.
The work had gone on the whole time.
He had done it with his own hands and logged not one hour of it,
and he had never asked himself why,
because he had always known why.

Marisol looked at the small square writing a moment.
Her hand came off her pencil,
and she let the page be what it was, and waited.

He squared the ledger on the plywood
and turned it around to face her.

"Tonight goes in," he said.

She looked at him, and then at the empty line
below all that quiet,
and took the pencil from behind her ear and wrote,
on the line where the small square hand left off,
*Measured the parlor. Started the answer.*
Her letters ran rounder and quicker than the ones above,
and the date went into the margin where all the dates went,
and the page had two hands on it.

He stood looking at the open book a long time.

"Come in the hall a minute," Cal said.

He carried one of the work lights in
and set it low at the foot of the stairs,
so the glow went up the staircase wall
and a hundred years of couples came up out of the dark,
frame over frame, floor to landing to top.

"The day you followed the plane in here," he said,
"I named that whole wall for you and went right past one,
and you stood there and let me go past it."

"I saw the stroke run long," she said.

"I know you did. I've thought about it since. You not asking."
He looked up at the frame near the top,
two faces too small to make out from the floor,
the same hall behind them, younger.
"She came to Ashford on a fellowship year.
One year. Everything she owned fit in a hatchback,
and the plan was somewhere better by the next fall,
and everybody who met her knew it.
Then she planted a rose on a porch that wasn't hers,
her fellowship year, at a house that wasn't hers either,
in a town she was already supposed to be leaving,
and I stood right there and watched her do it
and knew neither one of us was going anywhere."

"The rose knew first," Marisol said.

"The rose knew first.
We got married on that porch.
Front door wide open, the whole street in chairs on the lawn,
Delmar's smoke drifting up Millrow through the vows.
And afterward we came in this hall and stood on the third step,
because that is where this town takes the picture,
and the picture got taken, and the picture got hung."

He tipped his head at the frame near the top.

"That's us. That's my wedding picture.
And I have walked every tour this House ever gave me
right past it. 'And that's the end of the tour.'
I've said that sentence for years,
standing under my own wedding picture,
and this whole town has let me say it,
every soul on Millrow knowing better,
which is as kind as I have ever seen a town be
about a thing it never once mentioned."

The far work light in the parlor buzzed and went brown.
He crossed and reseated the cord where it fed the leg,
no word about it, and the light came back full,
and he came back and stood where he had been standing
and picked the sentence up where it had stopped.

"There's a word gets aimed at me every so often.
Kind people, every time.
A memorial rose. A memorial wall. A memorial anything.
I have never let that word finish crossing a room.
A memorial is a receipt.
Paid in full, account closed, everybody thanked.
She's not paid up. She's winning.
You stood in my doorway Monday and said so out loud,
and I have spent two days working out
that you're the one person I could stand to tell the rest.
So there it is.
The year, the porch, the picture, her.
I've never had it out whole before, for anybody.
Myself included."

She came across the hall while he was still looking up,
took the handkerchief out of her jacket pocket,
pressed it into his hand, folded,
and said, in the same motion,
"I want it noted the full tour cost me one handkerchief,
and Verna charges a pie."

The laugh came up out of him rusty, half a cough,
surprising the both of them,
a sound the hall had gone a long time without,
and for the length of it the House was easy to stand in.

"I'll square it with Verna," he said.

"You will not. She'll want to know what the story was."

"Then I owe you the pie."

"You owe me nothing," she said. "But I'm taking the pie."

She walked him back to the plywood by the sleeve,
and he brought the light in behind them.

"Now look at your own drawings," she said,
"because I have been sitting on this since the storm.
Boyd's letter promised your twelve a winter.
Equipment, roofs on the row barns, no reductions,
all of it pending, all of it only if the House goes down.
So price the other winter.
Sills, roof, wiring, plaster, gutters, paint,
every line you've drawn this week.
Tell me who does that work."

"Trades," Cal said.

"Your trades. Your twelve.
The restoration is the winter, Cal.
This house needs every trade Boyd's decks were promising,
and it needs them clear till spring."

He took the pencil off the plywood
and ran it down the margin of a cut sheet,
trades against weeks, twelve names against one winter,
and the total came out somewhere no total of his
had come out in a month.
He ran it again, slower, looking to catch it lying.
It came out in the same place.
He set the pencil down.
The number sat on the cut sheet being enough.
He was quiet a good while, and nothing in the quiet hurt,
and the quiet had room in it, which was new.

Up the hill the carillon struck midnight into the wind,
and the wind brought every note of it down Millrow
and in through the shutters, thin and cold and exact.
Neither of them said Boyd's Friday out loud.
It would still be there at daylight.

She reached past him and squared the ledger
to the edge of the plywood,
her hand staying flat on the open page to keep it,
fingers across the line her own writing had just made,
under the line the small square writing had quit on.
He put his hand down over hers there,
over both lines at once,
and neither hand moved,
and neither hand was needed anywhere else in the county.

She turned her face up to him in the work light,
close enough that the light had her mouth in it,
and the wind at the shutters had gone to a far-off sound,
and he bent his head toward hers.

The work light quit.
No flicker, no argument.
One breath the hall was amber,
the next it was dark and her hand was warm under his,
and out on the sawhorse his phone lit the plywood blue-white,
one o'clock gone a minute, one message standing on the screen.
She leaned back an inch to let him see it.
He picked the phone up.
