The supper had a sound before it had a crowd.

Marisol heard it build from the cake table all afternoon,
folding chairs scraping into neighborly angles by the hundred,
card tables clacking open under the live oaks,
the fiddler on the porch steps trying keys against the chair noise,
casserole dishes arriving hot, two at a time, in oven mitts,
steam and fried chicken and cake-table sugar
riding the cooling air the whole length of the lawn.

The string lights were finished.
She had checked that first, coming up Millrow.
The night they went up she had left half of them burning
over an empty lawn, gold on the grass with nobody under it,
and sometime in the days since,
the town had strung the other half
without asking anybody whose knots to copy.
Tonight the run burned end to end,
porch to oaks to the boxwood line,
and the whole town stood underneath it,
which had been the idea the whole time,
just never once the picture.

Earlene Tatum arrived at the cake table with a count.

"Forty-one casseroles," she said,
in the voice she used for river stages.
"Nineteen of them green bean."

"Nineteen."

"The sign-up went down with your badge,
and this town cooked from memory."
Earlene set her own dish at the end of the row.
"Memory runs heavy on the mushroom soup."

"Chicken table's fine," Verna Poteat said, coming past
with two pies balanced up one arm.
"Ham table's fine.
There's a table I'm calling salads to be kind.
Desserts held the line. Desserts always do."

"Tables get zinnias," Birdie Pham said, going by
with a coffee can riding one hip.
"Church gets the tall ones back Sunday."

Tunk Ferrell came through with a plate already built.

"Forty-one casseroles," he said.
"That is a lot of casseroles."

He went on down the tables.

Off the porch rail, Tick Moran was giving the county the menu.
The remote rig sat on the rail with its one red light on,
and Tick leaned on the post beside it
and ran the broadcast at supper volume.

"Live from the lawn at Hartwell House," Tick told the county,
"where I count four hundred chairs and no two of them level.
If you can hear the fiddle, you're close enough to walk.
Caller three, the tables are spoken for. Bring your own chair."

Delores Fountain-Mabry worked the rows off a clipboard
nobody remembered issuing her.

"Cake table holds until the line forms," she told Marisol.
"Then you cut and you keep cutting.
It's an honor. Act like it."

"Yes, ma'am."

Somewhere in there a woman Marisol knew only from a profile
circle stopped at the table and pressed her wrist.
"That tow number got my sister off an off-ramp at midnight.
I never thanked the right person till tonight."

"You just did," Marisol said,
and cut her a corner with icing on two sides,
and the line moved.

Cal Sutter stood past the last row of chairs,
where the boxwoods took over from the folding tables,
half out of the light, a cup in his hand he wasn't drinking from,
canvas jacket on against the cooling dark.
The crowd had left a space around him
about the size of what it believed,
and he stood in that space all evening and let it be left.

Nobody had made him come.
That was most of what the lawn kept not saying about it.

His name went past her table a dozen times before dark,
in low voices, with periods on it,
and she passed cake across the top of every one of them
and said nothing,
which was the job now,
and the job cost a little more every time it came by.

She had walked up the hill wanting one thing
she could not have in public,
ten seconds of his eyes, nothing anybody could point at,
and she got it over the cake knives at first dark,
across the boxwoods and four rows of chairs,
his face not moving, hers not either,
nothing in it a witness could carry anywhere.
It settled nothing.
It helped enormously.

"Line's forming," Delores said, arriving inside the look
with a stack of paper plates. "Cut."

She cut.

***

Full dark came on fast, and jackets came up from the trucks,
and up the hill the tower struck the hour
into a rest between tunes.
Rex Boyd came up the walk at about the same time,
no entourage,
shaking hands at the volume he did everything, which was one.

He was gracious the whole width of the lawn.
He admired casseroles by name.
He complimented the fiddler and got a flourish for it,
and he made the far tables laugh at something
the near tables couldn't hear.

Boyd had set a Friday for that signature once,
close of business, said like a favor.
The Friday had come and gone unsigned — she was one of two
people under these lights who knew it — and graciousness,
table by table, tea glass in hand,
was what a missed deadline looked like on Rex Boyd.

Tick handed him the mic when he reached the porch,
and Boyd held it low and talked underneath the crowd
instead of over it.

"I won't keep a soul from the pie," Boyd said.
"I just want to lift Delores's good tea
to the two things this town does better than anywhere I've built.
Safety, and progress.
And on the first one, don't take a builder's word.
This town is blessed in its doctors. Ask Dr. Cole."

"No," said Dr. Aisha Cole, beside the cake table.

She said it at conversation volume.
It carried like a dropped pan.

"You'll not borrow medicine for real estate, Rex.
I tape ankles and I read scans,
and nothing in my office grades a building.
Toast your decks on your own degree."

Coach Merritt came up out of his chair two tables over,
a ham biscuit still in one hand.

"The Gateway pays for the training room, Doc.
The one you've been after me about since June."

"The Gateway pays for a video with a training room in it."

"Four hundred people heard them promise whirlpool tubs."

"Four hundred people have watched you argue every call
I've made since August," Aisha said.
"I say sit, you hear play.
I say Monday, you hear Saturday.
Tonight I'm saying no,
and you'll hear it Saturday around kickoff."

The fiddler had stopped mid-tune to listen, bow still up.
The lawn had turned its chairs.

"You've fought me all season," Merritt said.

"And I'm undefeated."

The laugh went down the rows and came back around,
and Merritt stood there holding his biscuit like late evidence.

"The county is advised the fiddle has paused,"
Tick said, low, to the rail.

"This is a supper," Delores said, at plate level,
and the argument sat down the same as everything else did
when Delores said things at plate level.

Aisha came back around the cake table
and took up the pie server she had put down.
"Cut me the coach's slice," she said.
"I'm eating it where he can see me."

Boyd never lost the smile and never raised the volume.

"Doc Cole makes my point better than I make it," he said.
"A town that argues at its own supper still eats together.
That's the town the Gateway is for.
And while I have the mic, one more glass.
A quiet thank-you, said out loud.
To our facilities director, for his partnership
through a careful season.
You've all read the same page I have.
A man who lets the work do the talking.
I'll drink to that man all November."

She had read a different page.
She had held it under a bay lamp at midnight,
corner to corner, gray as a watermark,
saying the two words the county had never seen.
Neither one of them was partnership.

The applause came up patchy, tables deciding one at a time.
She had heard a ballroom stand up for Rex Boyd by rows once,
rain arriving on tin.
This was not that sound, and Boyd drank to it anyway.

The lawn looked at the boxwood line,
politely, Ashford-fashion, over shoulders and around cups.
Cal stood where he had stood all evening,
cup in hand, jacket dark past the last string of lights,
and did not raise the cup and did not put it down,
and his face, at forty feet, gave the town
nothing it could use in either direction.

And out of the middle of all that being looked at,
his eyes crossed the boxwoods and found hers.

She held them.
The full ration, nothing returned early,
while four hundred people watched a man not answer a toast
and not one of them saw where he was looking.
It was as close as the terms let the two of them stand,
and it had to cross forty feet of open lawn to happen,
and it was still the warmest thing on that lawn all night.

Delores relieved Boyd of the mic with a schoolteacher's hand.

"Pie's cut," Delores said. "Fiddler, finish your plate.
I said once to put this whole town at one table
and make November look it in the eye.
Well. Here's the table."
She looked down the lawn a moment, checking it against
the version she had ordered,
and then the mic came across the cake table, handle first.
"Lights were her ladder. Say a word, and keep it short.
The chicken's going cold."

The mic was warm from other hands.

She had a thank-you loaded, two lines and a knot joke.
Down the lawn Boyd stood easy in the light he'd toasted by,
having folded one man's silence into a partnership
in front of the whole county,
and on four hundred phones and every kitchen radio
in Doss County, the folding had gone out clean.

She knew what the top of every page said.
That was the one thing she could not spend.
What she had was a live rail feed,
a lawn that had come when the town called it,
and the one question Rex Boyd's whole gracious evening
had been built to keep anybody from asking.

Her pulse went loud in her ears and stayed loud.

"Thank you for the ladders," she said,
"and for forty-one casseroles.
The green beans will see this town through to Christmas."
The laugh came up the rows, easy, and she let it settle,
and did not hand the mic back.
"One more thing, then, since the toast was to partnership."
She turned from the tables to the boxwood line,
supper-calm, loud enough for the rail.
"Will you sign it?"

The question went out over the porch rail
and down the hill into every kitchen in the county.

What came back was the fiddle not starting.
The generator held its one note.
Four hundred people sat still enough to hear the moths
ticking against the string lights,
and the man the question had crossed the whole lawn to find
stood in the space the town had been leaving him all evening
and did not answer.

On the radio they time dead air like surgery.
Somebody would type this stretch up later
and put a number on it. Eleven seconds.
Under the lights it had no number yet.
It was a live mic warm in her hand,
a county leaning toward its radios,
and the answer not coming.
