The storm quit a little after four,
and the quiet it left was worse for sleeping than the noise,
gutters running full, the last rain ticking off the crepe myrtle
one leaf at a time,
and Marisol lay in the dark sorting the sounds of her own house
until she was sure none of them were damage.
Then she quit pretending it was still night and got up.

The group had beaten the sun to it.
Fence pictures, trampoline pictures,
somebody's carport sitting whole in somebody else's yard,
prayers stacking up under every one.
She read it like anybody now, one voice out of twelve thousand,
typed the Doss County tree man's number from memory
under a photograph of a flattened swing set,
and put her boots on.
Monday mornings were hers, spoken for by nobody,
and she had a feeling about where this one was going.

The Checkerboard's griddle was going at twenty of six,
bacon and wet awning canvas all the way to her car door,
and Sonny Dillard held down the end of the counter,
mud to both knees, going through eggs,
his radio parked by the sugar where it lived.

"Lines down by the fairgrounds, nobody hurt," he was telling
the room. "County road's cut clear as of five.
And a pecan limb came through the Hartwell House porch
about three this morning. Whole west end. Clean as a mail slot."

The counter had opinions, and she let them happen around her.
"Two trays," she told the register. "Twelve cups, black,
and all the sugar you can stand to lose."

At ten of six the Courier's upstairs window came on,
storm or no storm.
At six the tower up the hill tried itself,
six slow notes coming down wet over the square,
and she wedged the trays level on the front seat under them.
Verna's office light was burning at the Magnolia Court
when she came around the corner,
and at six twenty-five the crew trucks rolled down out of campus,
headlights still on,
and she fell in behind the last one,
which on a morning like this was as good as a parade permit.

The limb was everything Sonny had advertised,
big around as a culvert where it had torn from the trunk,
crown punched through the west end of the porch roof,
shingles and split lath sprayed down the steps,
and the town's whole answer already forming up underneath it
in the first gray light.
Tarps went up over the hole, snapping like gunshots.
A jack stood under the porch beam, already taking weight.
Sawhorses on the walk, cords in the wet grass,
and Ray had the card tables from the supper set up by the gate,
biscuits steaming through the butcher paper by the yard.

"You'd be the coffee," Ray said, and took a tray off her arm.
"Another ten minutes we'd have had a labor action."

Sonny's truck pulled in behind her, the fire coat traded
for a feed cap, chainsaw in the bed,
and he had it running before the tailgate was all the way down.

Cal was up on the porch roof at the edge of the hole,
shirt dark to the shoulder blades from the wet shingles,
calling the tarp count down to the ladders.
He saw her when the second truck door shut.
Ashford was watching, so what he did about it was nothing,
and what she did about it was lift a tray,
and on a public lawn that counted as the entire exchange.

She worked the coffee down the line of ladders.
The first man off took three sugars without apology.
"Ronnie. Bay three.
That pecan's been aiming at this porch for forty years.
A pecan don't drop limbs, ma'am. It aims them."

"He's got a whole thing about pecans," said the young one
on the next ladder. "I'm Marcus.
Do not get him going on sweet gums."

"Skeet," said the third, and tipped a paper packet into his cup
and worked it with a stir stick.
"Electrolytes. Coffee's a diuretic, ma'am.
Nobody wants to talk about it."

"He'll talk about it," Marcus said. "Give him till nine."

She offered the sugar on down and kept the last cup back,
and carried it up the walk to the foot of the big ladder.
"Coffee," she said. "Director."

"Ma'am." He came down for it, even-voiced, work-fair,
and took the cup, and his fingers closed over hers
on the cardboard a half second before she let go,
nothing any of the twelve could have named from any distance,
and she felt it in the soles of her feet.

He drank half of it looking at the tarp line.
Then he looked at her over the rest,
and the look ran long, past coffee, past the crew,
past anything the lawn had words for,
and the radio in the nearest truck cab broke it up,
Tick Moran at auctioneer speed:
"—if the tree is in the road, that's the county's tree.
If the tree is near the road, that is between you and the tree.
Caller four, go ahead."

The lawn went back to being a lawn.
"You don't have to stay," Cal said, from the third rung going up.

"I know what I don't have to do," she said,
and put her hand on the ladder rail. "Mind your roof."

Nobody assigned her anything, so she assigned herself.
She held ladders. She hauled brush to Sonny's pile
until the pile had a system, and then she was the system.

"Butts one direction," Sonny told her, feeding the chipper.
"Chipper likes it."

"The chipper has preferences?"

"Everything out here's got preferences.
Chipper's just honest about them."

She learned that Marcus ran three weather apps
and trusted none of them past noon.
"All three said it'd miss us," he said, up on the roof.
"Unanimous is how you know it's coming."
She learned that Skeet held opinions on sodium
at church strength.
"You want a packet? For the ladder work."

"No, thank you. I like my coffee unimproved."

"That's how they get you," Skeet said.

"Biscuits are done being warm," Ray called up the lawn.
"After that it's on y'all's conscience."

"Save Skeet two," Marcus said. "For the sodium."

"It's not the same sodium," Skeet said, and went down anyway.

Around ten Ronnie came off the roof for the water jug
and stood a minute at the tailgate.
"Half my fence is out in the county road," he said,
to nobody in particular.
Nobody took it up. The tarp snapped. The saw ran on.
He drank his water and went back up.

The sugar and the spare cups lived on Cal's tailgate,
next to the clipboards,
and when she went back for the sugar around eleven,
the top clipboard was work, columns and job numbers,
initials in the margins.
The one under it was not.
Twelve names in Cal's writing, first and last,
no numbers to them, no dates, no column for done.
She knew the third one.
She'd been holding his ladder all morning.
She counted them once, and then once more.
Two trays. Twelve cups.
She had guessed right without knowing what she was counting.
She put the sugar where the crew could reach it
and said not one word about clipboards to anybody.

"You staying till it's done?" Sonny said, when she came back
past the chipper.

"I'm seeing the porch level."

"Figured," Sonny said, and fed it another armload.

By noon the broken rafters were out and the new ones in,
raw yellow pine against hundred-year gray,
and Cal took the beam's end up off the jack with one hand,
held it steady while Ronnie slid the shim home,
and set it back down without a sound.
He put the level on the porch rail,
and the crew came in to read it like a scoreboard,
and the bubble sat dead center, and nobody cheered,
and everybody stood a little different for a minute.

She stood on the walk with the last of the cold coffee
and looked at the porch, whole again by lunchtime,
and said the thing she had been circling for two weeks,
out loud, in front of God and the crew,
plain as a grocery list.

"A roofer," she said. "A framer.
Somebody who can turn a porch rail true.
That's one porch, one morning.
The whole House is forty of these,
every trade in the county, all winter long,
which is every job Boyd's decks keep promising this town."
She turned and looked at Cal.
"What if the restoration is the winter?"

Cal stood with the level still in his hand.
He looked up at the House a long time,
top of the wall to the porch floor,
and whatever started running behind his face,
he did not do it out loud.

"Porch first," he said, and went back to the doorway,
where the trim had taken splinters off the limb's first bounce,
and set a nail.

By early afternoon they had quit thanking her
and started expecting her,
hollering her name when the small pry bar went missing,
arguing weather apps at her like she had a vote,
and the crew accepted her the way they'd accept a good tool —
the highest compliment on offer.

And on the east lattice, above the sawhorses and the wreckage,
the wife's rose had opened — October, holding to form,
the storm having failed to take a single bud —
and Marisol told him so in his own doorway:
"She's still winning."
He drove the next nail a little slower,
and nobody on the crew learned why.

She left at half past two, the hole papered in,
new shingles stacked on the walk for tomorrow,
the borrowed tables folded, the biscuits long gone.
She drove home with the windows down
through a town wet and bright and loud with chainsaws,
and it wasn't until her own hall mirror caught her,
keys still in hand,
that she saw the sawdust in her hair,
gold through the dark of it, a porch roof's worth,
and she leaned in, and looked,
and caught herself not minding at all.
She set her keys in the bowl.
The sawdust stayed.
