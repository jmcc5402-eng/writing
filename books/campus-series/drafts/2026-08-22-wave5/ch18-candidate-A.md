The shop opened cold on Wednesday,
the concrete still cold from the night,
WD-40 and coffee the first two facts of the morning,
and the radio down low all day
for reasons nobody on the floor was going to say out loud.

The work went on the same as any week,
oil changes and a seized gate hinge
and the ballasts out in the north lot's light poles,
and the whole floor did it
a shade quieter than the work asked for.

The transcript was on the break-room wall by then.

Somebody at the station had typed out the whole supper remote,
two pages, taped up by the time clock
between the safety poster and Boyd's letter,
which still said pending, still had November in it,
and still had twelve paychecks reading it on their breaks.
The break room smelled of toner and burnt coffee
and fresh tape, and would all week.

Tick's sign-on was in there. The fiddle was in there,
noted in brackets. The toast was in there,
every gracious inch of it, volume one the whole way down the page.
Then her question, set out on a line of its own,
same as it had gone out over the county.

PRUETT: Will you sign it?

And underneath, where the dead air had sat on 1340,
the typist had done the only honest thing available
and set one word in brackets, lower case, and left it alone.

[pause]

That word was him.
Typed, taped up, public,
a whole county's worth of not answering, one word long.
After it Boyd's recovery ran on to the bottom of the sheet,
partnership in it twice,
and then the sign-off, and the fiddle coming back up in brackets.

The crew had read it by first break, every man of them,
and not one of them said a word to him about it,
which in that shop amounted to twelve men
saying the same thing at once.

Cal read it standing, coffee in hand, and left it hanging.
Taking it down would have said more than the page did.

"Who types a pause?" Ronnie said, behind him.

"Somebody who was listening," Cal's second said.

She stopped beside Cal after, clipboard against her hip.

"Want it down?"

"Leave it," Cal said.

Nobody took it down all week.

He'd had her in sight twice that night, across the boxwoods,
for as long as the terms allowed and no longer,
chin up under the finished lights,
hair giving up its knot by full dark,
and the question hadn't been anywhere in her face
until it was in the mic and gone out over four hundred chairs.
That was the part the transcript couldn't carry.
Her question in ten-point type
looked smaller than her mouth had made it sound,
and he read it on the break-room wall that week
more times than the coffee strictly required,
which was its own kind of phone call,
and didn't count against the terms, and didn't help.

***

Ronnie brought the rest of the town's verdict in on Thursday.

"Peanut drew one last night and set it down
at the far end of the bar," he said.
"On the house. Told the room it sits there
till the man it's for walks in and claims it.
It sat there through closing.
Nobody so much as turned it."

"Whose?" Cal said.

"Didn't say. Didn't have to.
And Peanut says it counts against the nothing you missed.
Whole town went. You'd have hated it fine."

The Mule didn't hold with microphones.
Verdicts there came across the bar with the caps off,
and a beer poured for a man not in the building
was as far up the scale as verdicts went.
The county had convicted him by suppertime once.
Some part of it, apparently,
was walking that back a glass at a time.

Ronnie wiped his hands and looked toward the break room.

"My brother-in-law tapes ball games he was at," he said.
"Watches them the same night. Whole family's like that."

The line lay where he set it, and the grinder started back up.

Boyd's envelope came up with the Thursday mail,
the north end drawn across the letterhead in gray,
decks and glass and the long lawn running for the gates.
One paragraph, gracious at volume one.
In the spirit of the partnership spoken of at the supper,
the response window for the facilities review had been extended,
and the office looked forward to his signature
by the Friday before the trustees sit in November.

Extended was the memo's word.
The true verb was reset.
The paper Friday had come and gone unsigned,
quiet as any other Friday in the county,
and Boyd had spent no public word on it, and here was why.
A dead deadline was worth nothing to anybody.
Boyd had reset it — the paper Friday having died unsigned —
to the Friday before the trustees come to town,
where a signature would be worth
the most it was ever going to be worth.

Boyd had heard the same dead air the county heard
and priced it different.
The county heard a man not answering.
Boyd heard a man worth more unsigned in November
than signed in October,
and mailed him a longer rope with a ribbon on it.

Cal counted the Fridays between this one and that one.
There were not many,
and he didn't like the arithmetic from either end.
The memo went into the locked drawer
with the thing it was about,
and the key went back in the shirt pocket,
and the shop went on running.

All week he wanted to call her.

It came at the wrong hours, mostly.
Four in the morning, when the whole matter
lay out plain in his head and there was nobody to hand it to.
Quitting time. Any minute anything went right.
He wanted her read on the memo,
and her voice doing to Boyd's one paragraph
what she'd done to a county's worth of parking questions,
and mostly he wanted the sound of her running at something,
and the terms said no, and the terms were right,
and being right had never once warmed up a telephone.

Not her name at Delmar's, not in the group,
not on anybody's porch.
Those were the terms, and they were holding,
and nobody had written one line in them about the phone,
which was the only room left in the county
with the two of them in it.

Thursday evening he got as far as the fence line,
the phone out, her name up in the plain letters
the phone gave everybody, his thumb resting on it,
the light going long and dry off the pasture,
pecan leaves starting to turn along the back road.
Then the handheld went off on the bench behind him
about a door alarm in the North stands,
and by the time the stands were checked and locked
and the hill was quiet, the screen had been dark a long while,
and he let it stay that way.

***

The engineer was at the yard fence Friday morning,
out where the gravel gave up to pasture,
a level rod leaned on the rail
and a benchmark hunt that had run him
clear out the back road from the north end's staking.
Little orange flags stood in the ditch grass
half a mile in both directions now,
the expansion measuring its way toward town
one hundred feet at a time.
The morning was dry blue October,
cold enough that the coffee didn't last.
Cal carried his out to the fence,
because the fence was his, and stood a while, and then asked it.

"Question. Not on anybody's clock."

"Those are the good ones."

"A finding comes down on a building
over a certifying man's title.
Can the man answer it with a finding of his own?"

The engineer set the rod against the rail
and gave the question the same look
he'd been giving the horizon all morning.

"Only with one of equal standing.
Real drawings, real numbers, sealed and dated. No shortcuts."

"That's all it takes?"

"That, and a ladder," the engineer allowed.
"You strike me as a man with a ladder."

Cal laughed, one syllable, and the pasture took it.

So the answer had a shape now.
Rules rigged one direction could be met
by rules run honest the other,
if a man produced sheets that could stand in the same room
as the ones aimed at him.
Sealed, dated, no summary in place of the drawing.
It also had the timeline of a miracle.
Work like that wanted months,
and what he had was Fridays.

He went in and started a list on the legal pad anyway,
and at the top of the list he wrote the new Friday,
the one before the trustees sit,
so the clock would be looking at him
every time he added a line.
Measurements. Stamps. Whose seal. Whose ladder.
What it cost, and what it took in nights.
The House had already answered the honest question once,
on dated glass, in a hand nobody was adding to.
The rest of the answer was going to take a tape,
a ladder, and every Friday he had left.

The parts run brought the new roller motor Friday afternoon
in a crate stenciled TWO MAN LIFT on three sides.
The driver went around back for his hand truck
and came back to find the crate sitting on the bench
and Cal already signing the slip.
The driver looked at the crate, and at Cal, and at the crate,
and drove off with the kind of story
that gets better between towns.

The crew went home at five thirty,
taillights strung down the back road,
dorm windows coming on across the hill
a few at a time, four floors of other people's kids
turning their lamps on against a cool blue evening.

His second stopped at the door with her keys.

"The new Friday," she said. "Better or worse?"

"Longer," Cal said.

"I'll tell them that," she said, and went home.

He stayed.
The list got longer and more honest.
He cut the bays around ten
and let the office lamp be the last thing burning,
and sat with the pad and the code book
and the House key and the drawer key
riding together in the same shirt pocket,
and worked until the list quit growing.

Up the hill the carillon counted midnight down,
and the sound crossed the yard on the cold air
and let itself out the far side.

Ratchet was in the office doorway,
chin on the threshold, on duty for reasons of her own.
When he stood, she stood.

He went out into the yard, where the yard light made its pool,
and she followed and lay down on her name in the stencil,
head up into the light, good as a portrait,
one moth working the air over the whole composition.

He took the picture from the dark outside the light's edge.
Dog, stencil, gravel, October.
No words under it.
There weren't any that kept to the terms.
He sent it to the name in the plain letters
and stood in the open bay door with the cold coming in,
the screen dimming in his hand.

Nine seconds flat, and the phone lit up the dark shop,
her name at the top of the light.
