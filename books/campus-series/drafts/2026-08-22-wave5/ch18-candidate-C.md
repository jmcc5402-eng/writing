The transcript was on the crew corkboard by Wednesday morning.

Three typed pages under one tack,
squared up between the safety poster and the pledge letter,
which was still pinned, and still said pending,
and had hung there long enough
that the crew had quit reading it out loud.
Twelve names' worth of pending, going into its second week.

Somebody at the station had typed the supper up off the tape.
All of it, Tick's introductions, the fiddler,
Boyd's toast with the thank-yous folded in,
and then the question,
PRUETT set at the head of it in capitals, colon and all,
WILL YOU SIGN IT,
and at the place where Cal had stood at the porch rail
with a live microphone held up into his face,
the page spent one word in brackets and moved on.

[pause]

Eleven seconds, the tape said.
The page made it a word about the length of a breath.

He'd had thirty feet and a folding crowd between them
when she asked it,
her chin up into the question,
the finished lights gold on her face,
and eleven seconds had not been long enough
to quit looking at her.
The paper caught none of that.
The paper caught a man not answering,
which was the half the county got to keep.

The shop read the pages in ones and twos all morning,
men coming off their machines with their coffee,
heads tipped, lips moving at the bracket,
putting nothing on the air of the room afterward.
Nobody asked him about it.
The crew had settled where Ratchet had settled,
early, and with their feet,
and reading changed nothing but the quiet.
Under it the shop ran its Wednesday sound,
cold concrete and WD-40 at opening time,
the radio down low out of some idea of respect,
tape and toner off the break-room wall where the pages hung.

Ronnie came in from bay three with the Mule's news,
because Ronnie was the shop's paper of record.

"Peanut read it out last night," Ronnie said.
"The whole bar. Did the eleven seconds and everything,
arm up like a stopwatch.
Then he stood a beer on the bar and said
that one's for the pause. On the house.
From Peanut.
I have watched that man charge a widow for ice."

"My sister heard it in her car," Cal's second said.

Nobody had anything for that.
The impact wrench started back up in bay two.

The beer stood on the house down the hill
for a question the whole county believed was still open.
Four feet from the corkboard,
the bottom drawer of the workbench held the answer,
face down, locked,
the key in his shirt pocket next to the House key,
where it had ridden since the Monday the mail brought it.
The pause had never been a man deciding.
The pause was a man keeping.

Her name rode his break-room wall the rest of the week,
capitals, colon and all, four feet from the coffee,
and the terms said he didn't answer to it in public,
and public now included his own break room.
So he walked past it, forty times a day, and let it ride.

***

The parts truck came up the hill Thursday at eight
with the winter order,
and the driver ran the liftgate down
and started making a plan out loud about the drums.
Cal took the first one off the gate himself,
ninety pounds of gear oil walked in through the bay
against his chest, easy as a feed sack,
set down soft on the rack, no bounce to it,
and went back for the second,
and the driver stood the whole length of both trips
with her clipboard forgotten against her hip.

"Quit showing off for the vendors," his second said,
not looking up from the manifest.

The campus mail made it up at a quarter past ten,
and there was one envelope in it worth shutting the office door.

One page this time. One paragraph.
The letterhead had the north end drawn across the top in gray,
the decks, the glass, the long lawn running for the gates.
It did not mention the Friday that had already come and gone.
It didn't have to.
That Friday had died unsigned, quiet,
somewhere between his desk drawer and Boyd's patience,
and in all the days since, nobody down the hill
had sent so much as a reminder after it,
which told Cal more than the memo was about to.
A man leaves a missed deadline lying in the road
only when the missing of it is worth more than the meeting.

The paragraph reset the deadline instead.
In consideration of the community's continuing conversation,
the office of the facilities director was invited
to return the certification by the last Friday
before the trustees convened.
Gracious as the toast had been. Volume one, never louder.
The first deadline had been a demand with a date on it.
This one was a courtesy with his name already sewn in,
and it priced the pause to the penny.
Eleven seconds of dead air had gone down that hill,
been weighed down there, and come back up as a favor,
deliberation, graciously extended,
every silent week of it sellable in November
as patience Boyd had personally granted the town.

He read it twice.
The second reading found nothing the first one missed,
which was its own information.
On the wall calendar over the desk
he counted the Fridays between under his thumb,
and there were not enough of them.
Then he unlocked the bottom drawer,
laid the memo face down on top of the draft it belonged to,
and turned the key on the two of them,
two Fridays in one drawer now, the dead one and the named one,
and October running out between them.

***

He wanted to call her all week.

Not once, wanting it and getting past it. Constantly.
First thing, with the shop dark and the coffee not made.
Again at ten, with the crew loud in the bays.
Again every time the corkboard caught the corner of his eye,
which was hourly, the tack holding, the bracket not aging a day.
The terms had the county.
Delmar's, the porches, the group, the whole length of Millrow,
every room in it spoken for.
What the terms had left the two of them was the phone,
one room with no street outside it,
and all week the only things he had to carry into it were heavy.
The memo. The drawer. The count of Fridays.
He had stood in this shop once already
and found out at full price
what handing her weight instead of himself came to.
So the phone stayed in his pocket,
and the want ran the week like a low fever,
and the week let it.

Twice her name came to him in other mouths.
Once at the parts counter, a vendor relaying what the county knew,
and once on WDSS, a caller opening with I'll be brief, and lying,
and both times he went on doing what his hands were doing
and let her name go by like traffic.
That was the terms, working.
Nobody watching that shop all week saw one thing worth repeating,
and it cost him exactly what it was worth.

Friday afternoon the carillon struck one o'clock
into a dry blue sky with no more weather in it,
and Cal took his coffee out to the fence line
where the gravel quit and the pasture took over,
pecan leaves starting to turn along the back road,
and found the engineer already leaned on the fence.

Clean boots a week past clean, a surveyor's squint,
a water jug down in the grass by the post,
a man waiting on a truck or on paperwork
in the one spot on campus where waiting felt like work.

"Good fence," the engineer said.

"It gets that a lot."

A while went by at pasture speed.

"Ask you something," Cal said. "Professional."

"Cheaper over a fence than on a letterhead."

"Say an office hands a man a finding.
Sealed, stamped, the whole apparatus.
Can the man answer it? A finding of his own?"

The engineer thought about it honestly,
which took longer than agreeing would have.

"Only with one of equal standing," he said.
"Real drawings. Real numbers.
A stamp that's worth what theirs is worth.
No shortcuts anywhere in the body of it,
because the first shortcut anybody finds
is the whole answer thrown out in the hall.
Any man can holler at a report.
Answering one is licensed work."

"And if he had the drawings and the numbers?"

"Then he'd need time, which is the expensive part.
And a ladder," the engineer allowed.
He looked Cal over a second.
"You strike me as a man with a ladder."

The laugh came up out of Cal
from somewhere underneath the whole week,
one loud honest bark of it,
and the engineer went back to his jug,
satisfied with the hour.
He tipped two fingers off his cap when his ride came,
and Cal never did learn whose man he was.

***

The crew went home at five thirty,
and Cal shut the shop down behind them in the usual order,
wrench, grinder, compressor, radio.
He fed Ratchet at six.
Then he set up at the bench under the lamp
with a legal pad and the code book off the office shelf
and started the list of what equal standing weighed.

Drawings, to scale, of a house he knew by hand
and had never once needed on paper.
Numbers. Load paths.
Footings somebody would have to open ground to look at.
The wall's nineteen years of glass
translated into a language a stamp could countersign.
Weeks of nights, line after line of them,
and the Friday locked in the drawer four feet away
sat at the far end of October and not one day past it.
The list and the deadline did not fit in the same shop.
He wrote the list to the bottom anyway and tore nothing out.

It was near midnight when he quit and cut the lamp.
Ratchet had given up on him an hour back
and taken herself out to the gravel,
and she was down on her stencil in the yard light,
chin on her paws, flat out,
run all the way down by a week
that had not asked her for one single thing.

Cal stood in the bay door with the phone in his hand.

He didn't type anything.
Nothing he'd carried all week was light enough to send,
and there in the gravel lay the one thing in the county
with no weight in it anywhere,
a dog asleep on her own name.
He took the picture and sent it as it was, no words on it,
and set the phone face-up on the bench in the dark shop.

The screen dimmed, and went out.
Nine seconds, and it lit the bench again,
her name plain at the top of the light.
