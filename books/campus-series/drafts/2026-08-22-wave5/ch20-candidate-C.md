Tuesday night the side door took Cal's key,
and he let the two of them into the kitchen dark
and stood his minute, listening the house awake.
The refrigerator hummed. The upstairs shade moved on its draft.
Out front the big door stayed dark and shut,
because after dark that was the rule, and he kept it.
The hall was warmer than the street with no fire lit anywhere,
the house holding its warmth like a habit.

"You do that every time," Marisol said at his shoulder.

"A building reports, if you let it."

"And tonight?"

"All quiet. One woman on the premises after hours."

"Then write me up," she said,
and went past him up the hall with a work light in each hand.

They had the two lights, a sixty-foot tape, a yellow legal pad,
a kitchen stool pulled up to the sawhorse plank,
and the Friday before the trustees,
which was not enough Fridays,
and they had both quit saying so out loud.
The ground had been set in one sentence over a yard fence.
A finding gets answered by a finding of equal standing.
Real drawings, real numbers, no shortcuts.
So the drawings rode behind the seat of his truck, begun,
and got less begun every night,
and the numbers came out of this hall one tape-pull at a time,
while the town slept on believing what it believed,
and the two of them were the only labor in the county
that couldn't be seen doing the work.

He measured. She wrote.
The tape zipped home, the pencil scratched,
moths ticked at the work-light glass,
and the cold off the front door pooled at the far end of the hall
and never made it past the lights.
The dust their tape kept lifting rode the beam,
old plaster and lemon oil,
and her phone lay face-down on the plank,
off duty by her own order.

"Stair rise," he said. "Seven and a quarter, dead even,
thirteen treads running."

"Dead even." She wrote it. "Is that good?"

"It's better than code asks of a new house.
Somebody built these stairs expecting company."

He brought the extension ladder up the back hall one-armed
for the attic hatch and set it with a drop of his shoulder,
and she watched the whole trip over the top of her columns.

"You know what you look like, doing that," she said.

"A man with a ladder."

"That is not what you look like."

He went up. The hatch gave. He came down with figures.

"What's the attic like?" she said.

"Dusty."

She went back to her columns.

Up the hill the carillon dropped eleven down Millrow,
one note at a time through the oaks,
and they worked straight through the counting.
Near midnight she capped her pen.

"I walked past you at the post office today."

"I was there."

"I studied a flyer for a gun raffle so I wouldn't look at you.
Ask me anything about that raffle. I'm an authority now."

"That's the terms holding," he said.

"I know it's the terms holding. I wrote the terms.
I'm only saying the raffle had better be worth it."

***

Wednesday she came with Checkerboard coffee and a question.

"Real numbers means dates," she said, handing his cup across.
"When was the roof last felted? The wiring gone through?
The report can't say the House was minded.
It has to say when, and by whom, in writing."

Cal went down the back hall to the kitchen dresser
and came back with a long green book,
cloth at the corners, a hardware store's name
stamped gold and mostly gone from the cover.

"The House keeps a ledger," he said, and set it by her elbow.
"Every job anybody ever did the place,
back further than anybody now alive."

She opened it with the pen still capped and read every line.
Roof work in copperplate.
A porch jacked and sistered in a hand
that spelled porch with an extra r
and did honest work anyway.
Then the hand changed and stayed changed.
Small. Square. Patient about its letters, page on page.

Her reading slowed.
He watched her know the writing.
She had held photographs of it once, in his shop at midnight,
dates in the corners of eleven little panes of glass,
and had straightened one so it sat square with the rest.
She didn't ask.
Her hair was coming out of its knot
a strand at a time under the work light,
and she left it, and read.

The book fell open of itself at the page it had been opened to most.

"Glazing, north parlor," she read. "Two balusters turned to match.
Gutters, again." She turned the page.
"Roses tied in for the wind."

After that the ruled lines ran on empty.

"Who felted the roof after this?" she said, quiet.

"I did."

"The boiler. The sashes. The third step."

"Me."

"None of it's in here."

"No."

She looked at the blank years, and then at him.
Then she uncapped the pen,
squared the book to herself on the plank,
and laid her hand flat on the page,
fingers spread along the first empty line,
the line below the small square hand's last one.

"Then the book's behind," she said.
"Start with the year after. What got done?"

Monday, in his own doorway, she had told him
the wife was still winning.
He had carried the sentence two days without spending it.
He set the tape down.

"Gutters," he said. "That fall it was gutters.
She'd have entered it just like that. Gutters, again.
She kept the book the same as she talked."

Marisol wrote *Gutters* in her own hand,
on the line where the other hand stopped,
and that small motion did more to him
than the whole night's measuring,
and he stood in the lit hall of Hartwell House
and finally said the whole of it.

"She came on a fellowship year.
Twelve months, a rented room,
big plans pointed at anywhere but here.
The year ended in April and she didn't.
She said the rose needed her.
Nobody ever thought of a better explanation,
so that one stood."

Her mouth did the steady work of letting him finish.

"We married on that porch." He nodded down the hall
at the shut front door and the dark behind it.
"June. Half of Millrow brought casseroles
to a wedding that had a caterer,
and the casseroles went first.
There's a picture of it upstairs.
Two frames past the landing. Same step as everybody.
Third from the bottom."

He crossed to the near work light,
which had taken up a flicker,
and seated the bulb with two fingers,
and the flicker quit, and he said the rest to the light.

"'And that's the end of the tour'
is the only lie I tell in this house.
The tour runs two frames short.
The one past the landing is us. On the step.
Verna charges a pie for every story on that wall except ours.
Ours she's been giving me free for years, by never once asking."

He came back to the plank.

"After. People wanted to name things.
A bench on the square. A fund.
A garden with a plaque in it.
Good people, every one of them,
and I never once let the word memorial out of my mouth.
A memorial is a finished thing. You build one when you're done.
I had gutters. I had a boiler that argues every October,
and a wall to keep watch on,
and a rosebush that has to be tied in before the wind.
I never got done."
He looked at the ledger under her hand.
"And I never wrote a line of it in that book.
Writing under her line would have made the stopping true.
So her line stayed the last line,
and the work went where nobody could read it."

The hall stood quiet around that.
She let the quiet run on long past the place
where kindness usually jumps in to save a man,
and he was glad of the whole length of it,
because it meant she was taking the weight entire
and not just the polite part.

Then she reached into her jacket,
pressed a folded handkerchief into his hand,
and said, in the same motion,
"Well. At least nobody had to kneel on sod."

The laugh came up out of him rusty and surprised,
a sound with dust on it, crooked at the end,
and the hall handed it back off all that old plaster.
He used the handkerchief once and didn't pretend otherwise.

"Where'd you get that one?"

"Verna. The curb market, back in August.
I've been saving it,
and I need you to know I knew it was my worst."

"It was," he said. "Thank you."

She turned back to the ledger and wrote while he talked,
year by year, roof and boiler and sashes and step,
his memory against her pen,
until the blank lines had a history in them
and the book was caught up to the house.

"There," she said. "You were never behind.
You were unrecorded. There's a difference."

Then she flipped the legal pad to a page
she had ruled at home into two columns.

"Now the other thing I brought.
Boyd's whole arithmetic is that the winter shortfall
lands on facilities. Twelve names, if his money stays away.
So I priced the other winter."
Her pen came down the left column.
"Roof. Wiring. Porch framing. Masonry pointing.
Paint enough for a barn. Plumbing that predates mercy.
Every trade on this page is a man in your shop, Cal.
The House needs a winter's worth of exactly your crew.
The restoration is the winter."

He took the pad.
He ran her columns once with the pencil for a pointer,
and then he ran them again, top to bottom,
hunting the mistake,
because a thing that fit that clean usually hid one.
There wasn't one.
Line for line, trade for trade,
the hole in his winter and the work in this house
were the same shape.

He went quiet over it, the pad in both hands.
The numbers sat in him like light showing under a door
he had spent all fall calling a wall.

"Who pays for it," he said at last.
It didn't come out as an objection.
It came out as a route.

"That's a fight I know how to have," she said.
"Tonight I only needed you to see that it fits."

He set the pad down on the plank.
Her hand was still resting on the open ledger,
on the page with two hands' writing on it now,
and he put his own hand down over it,
palm over knuckles, the paper cool under both.
She looked up.
The look went long, and then longer,
past what even a shut house excuses,
and nothing in Ashford was awake to interrupt it,
and he leaned, and she came up off the stool toward him.

The work light hummed down a note and quit,
no flicker to it this time,
and the hall dropped to the little streetlight the oaks allowed.
Down the hill the carillon let one o'clock go,
a single note, and a long quiet after it.
On the plank her phone lit face-down,
a ring of light around it on the wood,
and buzzed once, and buzzed again.

Her hand turned under his and took hold of two of his fingers,
and let go.
She crossed the dark hall and picked up the phone
and lifted it into the light of its own screen.
