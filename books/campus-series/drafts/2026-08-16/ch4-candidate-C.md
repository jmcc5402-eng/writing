# Chapter 4 — Practical Men

POV: Cal Sutter, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate C. Variance card D3.

---

Monday came up quiet, and Cal Sutter considered that a promise.

The motor pool sat on the campus back road, out past the greenhouses,
three bays and a gravel yard he kept raked flat enough to shoot pool on.
Everything in that building had a spot.
Everything was in it.
Twenty years of Mondays had gone into the arrangement,
and Cal defended it like a border.

He'd opened at seven. Coffee at seven ten.
By eight he'd walked the yard once with the cup in his hand,
which looked like leisure and was actually inspection.
Two work orders waited on the desk,
and neither one of them scared him.

Ratchet lay in the early sun on his parking spot,
the one with his name stenciled in official yellow paint.
No work order for that stencil had ever existed.
Nobody had asked to see one.
Nobody was going to.

Inside, the crew had the mower deck up on stands,
and the argument about it was in its second day.
It had developed positions, factions, and a dissenting wing.
Twelve people worked for Cal.
On a good morning it sounded like twenty,
and this was shaping up to be a good morning.

On the bench under the office window lay a crescent wrench.
Wrong size. Not his.
It had been there since the first home Saturday.
Two weeks now.
Returning it was a five-minute errand.
He had meant to run that errand maybe fourteen times.
He had not run it.
There would be a reason,
and he kept a strict policy of not going looking for it.

At ten past nine a car came up the back road,
too slow and too clean.
Ratchet raised his head and did not put it back down.

"Company," Cal said, to nobody, and set his coffee on the sill.

The car parked square to the fence, considerate about it.
Two men got out.
The first was Rex Boyd, the booster behind the stadium expansion,
in jeans and a soft gray pullover,
the expensive kind of casual,
the kind that gets called "just something I threw on"
by men who keep a guy to throw it on for them.
The second man was younger and gleamed.
He had a tablet under one arm
and a shaker bottle of something green in the other hand,
and he looked thrilled about both.

The younger man aimed himself across the yard
and walked straight at the stenciled parking spot.
"Hup — not there," Boyd said, and steered him around it
without breaking stride. "That one's reserved."
He had read the stencil.
He had honored it.
Cal held that against him immediately.

"Cal Sutter," Boyd said, hand already out. "The man himself."

Cal shook it. It was that or leave it hanging,
and leaving it hanging took longer. "Mr. Boyd."

"Rex, please." Boyd tipped his head toward the bays,
where the mower-deck argument had reached its dissenting wing.
"Listen to that. That's a good shop.
You can hear a good shop from the road."
The trouble with Boyd was that he meant it.
Flattery you could wipe off.
This stuck.

The younger man had crouched in front of Ratchet
with a strip of jerky already out of his pocket.
"Grass-fed," he told the dog. "Zero sugar. Zero guilt."
Ratchet looked at the jerky.
Then at the man.
Then he laid his head back down on the yellow paint.
Ratchet had views about visitors
who showed up with the treat already in hand.
It meant they had planned on being liked.

"Two minutes," Boyd said. "Not even a coffee's worth."

Inside the bays, the grinder died mid-scream.
The silence that followed had ears on it.

"Nobody told you to stop grinding," Cal called.
The grinder started back up, sheepish.
It would be listening anyway.
They all would.
Cal had exactly one goal now,
and the goal was Boyd off this gravel
before any numbers got airborne.

"Walk with me," he said,
and hated how much it sounded like agreement.

They went down the fence line, away from the bays.
The younger man followed at a respectful distance,
sipping his green bottle with visible spiritual benefit.
"It's a lifestyle," he explained, to nobody who had asked.

"I'll skip the slideshow," Boyd said.
Behind him the younger man raised the tablet, hopeful.
It was full of renderings.
Every one of them appeared to be set at sunset.
Boyd waved it down without turning around.
"You and I read the same budget book, Cal.
There's a hole in facilities every winter,
and every winter somebody upstairs patches it with prayer.
You hold this campus together through parents' weekend
on chewing gum and overtime, and everybody knows it,
and nobody upstairs has ever once paid for the gum.
The expansion gift closes that hole.
Not once. Every year. Structural.
The athletic department writes a lot of checks in this town.
This is the first one I'd call honest work."

Cal said nothing.
He found a loose staple in the fence post,
worked it free, and put it in his shirt pocket.
The fence had not asked him to do that.
His hands wanted a job if his mouth wasn't taking one.

"And if the gift doesn't come," Boyd said, kindly,
which was the worst available way,
"the hole comes out of people. That's the whole menu.
Twelve names on a reduction list."
He let it sit one beat. Not two. He was good.
"And I'll tell you the part I hate, because I did look.
That list runs through your office.
It'd be your pen."

Cal looked back at the bays.
Three of his crew were checking the tire pressure
on a golf cart, thoroughly, in shifts.
The cart did not need it.
He moved Boyd another ten yards down the fence.

"You didn't drive out here to show me a hole," Cal said.
"Holes I've got. What do you want?"

"See, this is why I came in person," Boyd said.
"You're a hard man to get a meeting with."

"I'm standing in a yard. It's open."

Boyd laughed, one genuine note, and folded it away.
"Hartwell House," he said. "On Millrow."

Cal's hands went quiet on the top rail.

"Here's the thing nobody downtown will say in a straight line,
so I will," Boyd went on.
"The university can't condemn that house.
Doesn't own it. The trust does.
And a trust can't sell the town's porch — because that's
what it is, Cal, everybody's grandmother has a picture
on those steps — without cover.
Serious people need cover to make a hard call."

"Cover," Cal said.

"Your office runs the condition survey
on the Millrow structures this fall. Routine. Overdue, frankly.
One line on one form.
'Unsalvageable,' over the facilities director's signature.
That's not a demolition order. It's not a sale.
It's one professional opinion,
and it lets everybody else stop pretending they're the ones deciding."
Boyd turned and faced him, friendly as a porch light.
"The trustees meet in November.
The finding would want your name on it before then.
That's the whole ask.
A favor between practical men."

Behind them the golf cart got its fourth tire checked.
Cal turned the gate hinge under his thumb, once, testing it.
The hinge was fine.
Everything in his yard was fine.
That had always been the point of the yard.

"And the gift," Cal said.

"The gift travels with the expansion.
The expansion wants Millrow.
It's all one animal.
I didn't build it that way, Cal.
I'm just the one willing to say it out loud.
The money's sitting there with its coat on,
waiting on one word."

Cal looked at the fence a while.
"Leave the House out of it."

"I'd love to," Boyd said, and sounded like he meant that too,
which was the problem with the entire morning.
"The math won't.
And Cal — you've been under that porch on a work order.
Would you hold a safety meeting on it?
Put your whole crew on those boards, all twelve, and have them jump?"

He wouldn't.
That was the tooth in it.
The word Boyd wanted was not even quite a lie,
and both of them knew it,
and only one of them was enjoying the fact.

"The House isn't the university's," Cal said.
"It isn't mine. It sure isn't yours."

"Not one thing you just said is wrong," Boyd said, pleasant.
"And none of it moves the math."

He checked his watch then, or performed checking it,
and stepped back from the fence.
"I'm not asking you today.
I wouldn't insult you by asking on a Monday."
He started for the car, easy, unhurried,
a man leaving on his own schedule at exactly the moment
Cal wanted him gone,
which somehow made it worse.

The younger man popped up and produced a foil packet,
holding it out with both hands, ceremonial.
"Greens sample. It's the algae that does the work."
Cal took it, because refusing would have taken longer.
Ratchet rose and escorted them both across the yard
at a fixed distance of nine feet, formal as a bailiff.

At the car door Boyd paused,
pleasant as a weather report.
"It's a good old house.
I'm sure it was really something once.
But you and I are practical men, Cal.
It's not a landmark.
It's deferred maintenance with a porch."
He left that lying on the gravel like a business card
and drove off at a respectful speed,
the younger man waving at Ratchet through the glass.
Ratchet gave him nothing.

The grinder was back up before the dust was.
Somebody inside hollered that the deck was fixed.
Somebody else hollered that it absolutely was not.
Twelve names, all of them loud,
none of them on any list this morning.
Cal stood at the fence and listened for a while,
longer than the fence needed him.

The maples on the back road had gone rusty at the tips already.
November liked a head start.

Ratchet came and sat against his leg.
"You didn't take the jerky," Cal said. "I saw that.
Good instincts."
Ratchet looked up at him and held the look.
"Don't start. I didn't sign anything."
Ratchet went back to his spot,
which was not a verdict,
but it wasn't an acquittal either.

Cal went in through the office door instead of the bays.
The foil packet went in the trash, unexamined.
The pen came out of his shirt pocket on its own,
an ordinary giveaway pen with the logo half worn off.
He looked at it and put it back.
It had been just a pen at eight o'clock.

The crescent wrench lay on the bench under the window,
where it had lain for two weeks.
Wrong size. Not his.
He picked it up.
It was light, and warm from the sun through the glass,
and he stood there holding it
a good deal longer than a man stands holding a wrench.
Five-minute errand.
He knew right where it went.

Then he set it back down, squared it to the edge of the bench,
and went out to the bays to lose the argument about the mower deck.
