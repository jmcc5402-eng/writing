# Chapter 4 — The Right Number

POV: Cal Sutter, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate: A. Variance card: D7.

---

The jerky was already out of its wrapper and being refused
when Cal came around the end of the parts shelf,
wiping his hands on a rag that made them worse.

The man doing the offering crouched at the mouth of bay two
in white sneakers that had never met a fluid,
holding the strip out at dog height
with the encouraging face people use on toddlers and juries.
Ratchet sat in her parking spot —
somebody had stenciled RATCHET on the concrete years back,
with a border, so it would look official —
and she looked at the jerky, and then at the man holding it,
and then past both of them at Cal,
and did not move one muscle of her face.

Cal had watched that dog accept jerky from mail carriers,
roofing subs, two visiting deans,
and an auditor from the bursar's office
whom the entire campus had agreed in advance to hate.
She had never once declined.
Cal set the rag down and started paying attention.

"Cal Sutter," said the other man, the one standing,
and he said it with his whole chest, delighted,
a man whose luck kept being good. "There he is."

Rex Boyd stood in the middle of Cal's yard,
expensively casual, canvas jacket with no logo anywhere,
which was itself the price tag.
Everybody knew Boyd on sight,
the booster behind the stadium expansion,
his name attached to half the good news in the county.
He looked exactly as pleased to be standing
on cracked motor-pool concrete
as he would have looked anywhere else,
and the unsettling part was that it didn't read as acting.

Behind Cal, the shop went quiet in stages.
First the impact wrench quit, then the radio,
and then, in a coordinated maneuver
nobody would later admit to,
three of his crew developed urgent business
at the pegboard nearest the open bay door.
One of them was holding a screwdriver upside down.
Twelve people worked for Cal,
and he'd have bet the truck that at that moment
all twelve were in earshot and leaning.

"Walk with me," Cal said, and was already walking.

He aimed them across the yard toward the fuel island,
which had sun, no shade, no chairs,
and acoustics that carried nowhere.
Boyd fell in beside him, easy, unhurried,
taking the place in with what appeared to be
genuine professional interest.

"You keep a clean shop," Boyd said.
"People think that's about tidiness.
It's about knowing where everything is
the day something's on fire."

It was, word for word, a thing Cal believed,
and he resented hearing it arrive in someone else's mouth,
wrapped up and handed over as a gift.

"What do you need, Mr. Boyd."

"Rex. And I need two minutes,
which I realize is the most expensive thing on this campus."

The sneakers caught up to them then,
carrying a tablet and a shaker bottle
of something the color of pond water.
He had the build of a man made entirely
of egg whites and optimism,
and he presented the tablet before anyone could stop him,
tilting it into the sun so the renderings came up
as one triumphant sheet of glare.

"The expansion," he said reverently.
"These really sing at golden hour."

"It's ten in the morning," said Cal.

"It will not always be," said the sneakers, undefeated.
He studied Cal for a moment with open admiration.
"Can I just say, sir. That gray at the temples.
Very trust-building. People pay for that."

"He means it as a compliment," Boyd said.
"He means everything as a compliment.
It's why I keep him."
Then he waved the tablet gently down,
and that fast the temperature of the conversation changed —
not colder, just realer,
a man scooting his chair up to the actual table.

"You've seen the winter numbers," Boyd said.

"I wrote the winter numbers."

"Then you know the hole."
And Boyd said a figure, conversationally, no notes,
and it was the right number.
It was the number off Cal's own spreadsheet,
the one that lived in the drawer that locked,
and hearing it out loud in the open air of his own yard
was worse than any threat Boyd could have designed.

"The gift covers it," Boyd went on. "All of it.
Nobody freezes, nobody's furloughed,
the athletic department quits raiding your budget
every time a practice field needs reseeding,
and in January, when other schools are mailing bad news,
your people are just working."
He let that ride for a step or two.
"And if the gift goes elsewhere,
you already know what January looks like instead.
A list comes down from upstairs with twelve lines on it,
and they hand that list to the man who knows the crew best,
and that man ranks it. That's you.
The pen finds your hand either way, Cal.
I've watched men carry that. It doesn't wash off."

The worst part was the kindness.
The second-worst part was that none of it was a lie.
Cal could put a face to every one of the twelve
without trying, which, it turned out,
was the downside of knowing where everything was.

"So take your gift," Cal said.
"You don't need me to cash a check."

"No," Boyd agreed. "I need you for the other thing."
It arrived with perfect manners, like everything else.
"Your office runs the condition survey this fall.
The Millrow structures."

"Millrow's trust property.
We survey it because it borders campus.
That's paperwork, not power."

"The Hartwell House is on Millrow."

Cal kept walking. He was aware of his own boots.

"The House belongs to the trust," Boyd said,
in the tone of a man agreeing with an objection
before Cal could raise it.
"The university can't condemn it. Nobody can.
Nobody would want to. I've eaten pie on that porch.
But here's the thing about trusts —
they can't sell the town's porch on their own.
They'd be run out of church by Sunday.
They need cover, and cover is a professional finding.
One word, really, in a university survey,
over a signature this county trusts.
Unsalvageable.
Then it isn't selling out. It's accepting reality.
The trustees meet in November.
The finding would want your name on it before then.
That's the whole ask. Your honest opinion,
worded usefully."

The sneakers, sensing a lull,
shook the pond-water bottle in Cal's direction.
"Adaptogens? For cortisol."

"No," said Cal, with feeling.

Ratchet, who had followed at range like a chaperone,
crossed the gravel and sat down
on Cal's side of the conversation, facing Boyd.
Boyd looked at her and smiled like she'd voted,
which she had.

"Smart dog."

"She's a good judge," Cal said.
"It's a problem for some people."

He stopped at the fuel island and faced the man.
"Here's my trouble with your two minutes.
Nobody's asked me to lie yet.
That's what's supposed to make it easy."

"Have you been through the House lately?" Boyd said.
"The sills are gone on the north side.
The roof is years past its life.
Whatever number the trust thinks fixes it, double it.
Nobody's asking you for fiction, Cal.
The building will write most of that survey itself."

"Then let the survey say what it says, on its own clock.
Leave the House out of the rest of it.
Out of the gift. Out of your November."

"I'd love to," Boyd said, and sounded like he meant it,
which was the most frightening thing he'd done yet.
"Show me where else the winter goes."

Cal looked back at the shop.
Through the open bay somebody had turned the radio on again,
a little too loud, a little too casually,
and somebody else laughed at nothing,
and the whole building was performing not listening
with everything it had.
Twelve lines. His pen. He didn't have another answer,
and Boyd, being a professional, did not stand there
making him say so.

"Think on it," Boyd said. "That's all today was."
He put out his hand, and Cal shook it
out of a reflex he'd get around to regretting.
Boyd got two steps toward the car and turned,
pleasant to the last.
"It's an old building, Cal. Sills gone, roof done.
It's deferred maintenance with a porch."
He let it lie there on the gravel like a business card,
face up, where anyone could pick it up.
"Don't pay twelve mortgages for a porch. November."

On the way past, the sneakers tried Ratchet one more time,
getting all the way down into a crouch,
jerky extended, face at maximum encouragement.
Ratchet let him commit fully to the position,
held it just long enough to establish the record,
and then looked at the middle distance.
Cal decided she ate free for life.

---

The gearbox was half back together
before Cal noticed he'd been tightening the same bolt
for a while now.

Around him the shop had come back up to volume
a little too enthusiastically,
the radio louder than anybody actually liked it,
which meant they'd heard every word.
Nobody asked him anything.
Somebody set a coffee at his elbow on the way past
without comment, which in that building
was a casserole at the door.

By quitting time the yard had emptied out,
the last truck gone, the bay doors down,
and Cal stood at his bench with the winterizing list
he'd been not reading for ten minutes.

Her wrench sat at the end of the bench,
where it had sat since the first home Saturday.
A crescent wrench in a size he had no use for,
which he knew for a fact,
because he'd checked it twice against jobs
that didn't need it.
It was a cheap wrench, wrong for everything,
the adjustment wheel gritty from over-torquing,
and it had no business on a professional bench,
and he had carried it in from the truck anyway,
back at the start of the season,
for reasons he had filed under later.
Two weeks now he'd been meaning to run it back.

He picked it up.
He turned it over once in his hand.
He put it back down exactly where it had been,
squared it to the edge of the bench,
and declined to examine any part of that.

Ratchet crossed the empty shop
and lay down across his boots,
which she only did on bad days.

"You're a poor judge," Cal told her.

She stayed where she was,
and so, for a while, did he.
