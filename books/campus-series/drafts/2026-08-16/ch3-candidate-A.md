# Chapter 3 — The Second Guest Book

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate: A. Variance card: D5.

---

On an away Saturday, Ashford fit back inside its own clothes.

The fairgrounds were a field again, mowed and modest,
the square kept its own parking,
and the game itself was three hundred miles down the interstate,
seen off that morning like a relative, proudly and with food.
What stayed home was the radio.
Every porch, every pickup, the propped-open door of the pharmacy,
all running the same broadcast,
so that a person crossing the square never missed a play.
You just heard it handed along ahead of you, house to house.

Marisol had a list.

Six items on the back of an envelope,
and underneath the six, unwritten, the real one,
which was a Saturday that asked nothing of her.
Last Saturday she had alphabetized a city.
This one she intended to spend at the speed of a woman
buying tomatoes.

Her son had not made the travel squad,
a fact he'd reported in a text of two words and one thumb,
and which she was not permitted to mind out loud.
Walk-ons traveled closer to Thanksgiving, apparently, if at all.
Her daughter had sent a photo of a Bellamy dining-hall waffle
and nothing else in three days, which meant happiness.
The parents' group chat, meanwhile, had converted itself
into a broadcast booth of its own,
score updates and hotel reviews and one father
posting the same photo of the same stadium from four angles,
all four captioned "we're here."
Her phone went off in her purse
at the frequency of light rain.
She silenced it, mostly, and drove to the market.

The Doss County Curb Market kept one long open shed
on the far side of the square,
tin roof, open ends, shade you could stand in like a creek,
and on an away Saturday it belonged entirely to the county.
Boiled peanuts rolled in their kettle at the near end
and sent a ribbon of steam down the length of the place.
Muscadines went out by the paper sack.
Mums sat in ranks beside the post with the stapled flyers,
FAFSA night and a gospel sing and a tractor, runs good,
and the tomato tables held the middle,
fruit laid out on newspaper in rows like headlines.

Marisol worked her list from the far end in,
and collected her own share of looks doing it,
which she knew, and which she walked accordingly.
A year of remembering she was allowed to be seen
had turned out to suit her.
She was at the zinnia buckets, building a bunch
one stem at a time, orange against red against
a pink with no apology in it,
when the market changed its posture.

She felt it before she could name it.
Nobody stopped talking. Nothing so obvious as that.
The shed simply developed a direction.

Cal Sutter came through the north end
with a bushel basket riding one arm
and Verna Poteat beside him, narrating her own basket,
and he crossed the whole market in no hurry at all.
Through the peanut steam, which broke around him and re-formed.
Past the Dillards' table, where he put out one hand
without looking, pressed the wobble flat out of its bad leg
in passing, and was gone before the table could thank him.
He tipped two fingers off his brow at a baby
riding somebody's hip, and the baby, sensibly, accepted it.
At the tomatoes he set Verna's basket down where she pointed,
chose his own with the one-at-a-time attention
other men saved for hardware,
paid, nodded, and gathered the basket back up,
every motion performed in the sincere belief
that he was invisible.

He was not invisible.
Marisol ran a quiet count from the zinnias.
The peanut kettle had an audience that was not watching peanuts.
Two women at the muscadines had gone silent
in the middle of somebody's surgery story.
By her math, roughly half the market
was carefully not looking at Cal Sutter,
and the other half hadn't noticed him yet, and would.
She stood there holding her zinnias
and understood that she had just joined a hobby.

She wasn't sorry about it, either.
She watched with her chin up and one eyebrow
enjoying itself, the frank appraisal of a woman
who had spent a year remembering she was allowed to look,
and when he swung Verna's basket up onto his shoulder
she awarded the maneuver full marks
and went back to her flowers before he could catch her at it.
Mostly before.

"Been like that for years," said Earlene, at her elbow, unasked.

Marisol did not jump, which she felt deserved credit.
"I was choosing flowers."

"Mm. Whole shed's choosing flowers."
Earlene had a sack of muscadines
and the settled stance of a woman prepared to narrate.
"Man comes through every Saturday like church.
Carries for Verna, squares up whatever's crooked,
buys his tomatoes, believes nobody sees him.
Half of Doss County's got a season ticket."

"He still has my wrench," Marisol said.

"I heard."

"From who?"

"Sugar." Earlene ate a muscadine. "From everybody."

Marisol was assembling a reply worthy of that
when the phones began.

Not one phone. That was what made it strange.
A chirp at the tomato table, then two down by the mums,
then a pocket going off at the peanut kettle,
the sound moving through the shed in a ragged little wave,
phone to phone, until Earlene's lit up in her hand
and Marisol's buzzed in her purse,
and for a moment the radio broadcast was the only voice
in the market and nobody was listening to it.

The picture was a rendering.
Architect-tidy, sun placed just so,
a green and level lawn with nothing standing on it at all,
crossed by two clean sidewalks and dotted
with the small proud trees that exist only in renderings.
Under it, one line.
"Pending trustee approval at the November board meeting."

Marisol looked at the lawn and could not place it,
because there was nothing left on it to place.
Earlene placed it in one second flat.

"That's the Hartwell House," she said.
"Without the Hartwell House."

Marisol knew the House the same as anybody new in town
knew it, which was from the outside.
The big porch on Millrow, white columns gone soft
and friendly with age, the yard where Delmar's hickory smoke
liked to drift over and settle.
She had heard the rest by porch and by group chat.
Couples in Ashford got engaged on that porch.
Always had. You proposed there,
or you spent the rest of your life explaining why not.
She had never once been inside it.

"November," said the woman at the tomato table,
to no one, to everyone.
The word went down the shed in both directions
and came back heavier.
Verna Poteat sat down slowly on the edge of the mum rack
and said nothing at all, and two people moved toward her
at once, and the peanut man turned his kettle down
as if the whole market had become a sickroom.
Then somebody said something about chaining themselves
to the porch rail, and somebody else asked
if it could wait until after supper,
and the shed laughed because the other choice
was standing there quiet, and this was not a quiet county.

At the north end, Cal Sutter stood with his phone out
and the bushel basket set at his feet.
He read the screen for a long moment.
Then he put the phone away, took the basket back up,
and walked Verna's tomatoes out toward the cars,
and if his shoulders carried a different set going out
than they had coming in,
Marisol was possibly the only one positioned to see it.

She saw it.

"Who sent it?" she asked.

Earlene was scrolling, one thumb, unhurried.
"Nobody sent it. Everybody forwarded it."
She put the phone away and delivered the rest
in her flat civic voice, the one she used
for road closures and now, apparently, for this.
"Board meets in November.
That's all anybody knows this morning,
and by supper the whole county'll know that much twice."

The market took the news and went on being a market,
because the peanuts were ready
and the mums were not going to load themselves.
Marisol paid for her zinnias
and let the list carry her around the square,
and between errands, parked in the shade with her flowers
sweating gently in their jar,
she did her good deed for the day and opened the group's queue.

Four hundred member requests, give or take,
which was about what an away weekend brought,
everybody's cousin and seatmate and hotel-lobby friend
wanting into the chat where the good information lived.
She went through them with her thumb,
approve, approve, approve,
pausing only for the obvious junk —
one profile was selling sunglasses; goodbye —
and welcomed the rest in a batch with the pinned rules,
feeling useful, feeling generous,
feeling like a woman with her town's whole Saturday
riding in her lap.
It took ten minutes
and left her caught up with the entire world.

By dusk her last errand was supper,
and supper on a Saturday meant Delmar's,
the screen porch on Millrow with the hickory smoke
settled in low over the yard
and the radio inside gone over to the postgame show.
The line ran out the door and down the steps,
everybody holding the same conversation in different pairs.
November. The board. The House.

The clipboard sat beside the register.
Somebody had zip-tied one pen to the clip
and set a pickle jar of spares next to it,
pens for optimists,
and the sheet on top was ruled by hand,
NAME down one side and a line for everybody's,
nothing else. No heading. It didn't need one.
By the time Marisol reached the front
the town had already named it.
The House's second guest book, people were calling it,
the first being the one inside,
in the front hall she had still never seen.

Ray came around the counter with somebody's order
in a paper bag, took in the line looking at the clipboard,
and signed it himself without being asked
and without putting the bag down,
his name going on big and sideways.
Then he went back to his smoker
with the air of a man who had settled something.

When her turn came, Marisol took the zip-tied pen,
which fought her exactly as far as its tether,
and read the column of names above the empty line,
Poteat and Dillard and a dozen more
she would have to ask Earlene about,
half a town she was still learning by hand.
She added her own.
Marisol, first, and then, after a second, the rest of it,
neat, because it seemed to matter.

She carried her supper out through the screen door
into a dusk gone soft over Millrow,
the smoke settling in for the night,
the House's porch light coming on down the street
the same as it ever had, columns pale under the oaks.

She drove home with barbecue on the seat,
zinnias riding the cupholder,
and the postgame call-in show arguing gently with itself,
and at the stop sign on Millrow she looked once in the mirror
at the porch where the whole town had gotten engaged.
Still there. Lit. Nothing pending about it tonight.
She made her turn and went home
to put her flowers in water.
