# Chapter 3 — Away Saturday

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate B. Variance card: D6.

---

On away Saturdays, Ashford went back to being its own size,
and wore the fit like a favorite shirt.

The fairgrounds sat empty, mowed in stripes, resting.
The square kept banker's hours.
Every porch had a radio going low, tuned to the pregame,
attended by men who would swear they weren't listening
and could recite the injury report.
The whole town had the swept, satisfied calm
of a house the morning after a very good party.

Marisol had one project for the day, and the project was errands.
Seven items, one list, zero ambitions.
After a Saturday spent running a welcome tent
for twelve thousand of her closest strangers,
she wanted a day that asked nothing of her
but correct change and a working turn signal.

The Doss County Curb Market was item three
and the only one she'd underlined.

The market lived in a long open shed with box fans at both ends,
big as dinner tables, stirring everything into one smell.
Boiled peanuts rolled in their kettle and sent up
a salty steam a person could lean on.
Muscadines went by the paper sack.
Mums stood in ranks near the entrance, fat and smug,
ready to become somebody's entire porch personality.
Tomatoes sat in pyramids on last week's newspaper,
graded, as far as she could tell, by pride.

She was at the zinnia buckets at the far end,
deciding between orange and a more committed orange,
when the market changed pressure.

She felt it before she found it.
A shift in the shed's attention, small and total,
half the crowd angling itself politely toward the north door
like plants doing something about the light.

Cal Sutter came through it with Verna Poteat's bushel basket
up on one shoulder, Verna herself alongside,
supervising the basket at a volume the basket didn't need.
He walked it through the boiled-peanut steam
without changing speed for the steam or the crowd or anything,
and the crowd sorted itself around him
the same as water declining to argue with a dock post.

On his way past the Dillards' table,
which had been negotiating with gravity all morning,
he got two fingers under the lip of it, lifted,
toed something under the short leg,
and set it down solid without breaking stride.
The oldest Dillard looked at the table, then at his own hands,
and appeared to reach a private decision about both.

A baby went by at hip height, riding its mother,
staring at Cal with the frank rudeness of the very new.
He tipped two fingers off his cap brim at it, gravely,
one professional to another.

Then he set Verna's basket by the tomato table,
picked out six tomatoes without hurrying a single one,
and counted out his bills with the calm, unwitnessed privacy
of a man who believed, sincerely, that he was invisible.

Nobody had ever told him about the other half of the market.

Marisol pushed her sunglasses up into her hair,
the better to be honest about it, and watched.
No green polo on a Saturday.
A plain work shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow,
and the same forearms, off duty and unaware of it.
Light brown hair the sun was still working on,
a little gray at the temples, none of it looking accidental.
She was forty-three years old,
she knew exactly what she looked like
standing at a flower bucket in this dress,
and she gave the view her full professional attention.
Fair, she figured, was fair.

"Been like that for years," Earlene said, at her elbow, unasked.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were fixing to."

Across the shed, Cal handed Verna a tomato
she hadn't asked for and plainly wanted,
and Verna received it with the graciousness of a queen
accepting tribute she had personally arranged.

"He still has my wrench," Marisol said.

"I know it. Everybody knows it."
Earlene delivered this in her humidity voice,
flat as a road closure.
"Half this county's been watching that man cross rooms
since before your kids could vote.
Pace yourself. It's a distance event."

"I'm not watching. I'm appraising.
There's a difference, and the difference flatters him."

"Mm," said Earlene, which from Earlene was a co-signature.

Cal shouldered the basket and went out the south end
into the white morning, Verna processing beside him,
a parade with an audience of one shed.
Marisol bought her zinnias.
Both oranges.
It seemed wrong to make them compete.

---

The picture reached the market a little past eleven,
traveling phone to phone.

One phone chimed over by the peanut kettle.
Then two more at the mums, then a sound
the shed didn't have a name for,
pockets going off in a ripple from the north door south,
soft and general, as if the building had been dealt in
on something.

Marisol's came to her from three directions inside a minute,
and it was the same picture every time.

A lawn.
A tidy, clipped, architect's lawn,
with little gray trees at intervals
and little gray people strolling on little gray paths,
rendered in the smooth no-weather of a brochure,
on the exact spot on Millrow
where the Hartwell House had stood since before
anyone doing the strolling would have existed.
Under it, one line of caption, polite as a hymn:
*pending trustee approval at the November board meeting.*

"November," somebody said at the tomato table,
and the word went down the shed like a dropped jar.

The oldest Dillard took his glasses off,
cleaned them on his shirt, put them back on,
and it was still there.
A man by the peanut kettle laughed once, at nothing,
the laugh of a man buying himself a second to reread.
Somebody said their mother and daddy
had gotten engaged on that porch,
and three other voices said *mine too* so fast
it sounded rehearsed, and wasn't,
and that was somehow the worst part.

Then the market went quiet, and then it stayed quiet.

Not a hush. Hushes end.
The box fans kept turning.
The kettle kept rolling its peanuts.
Out in the lot a truck radio said kickoff was an hour off,
cheerful, to nobody.
A woman at the tomatoes read the caption out loud again,
slower, as if the words might come apart at a lower speed.
They didn't.
The quiet went on past the point where market quiet
usually knows to quit, on and on,
long enough for Marisol to hear her own phone
buzzing in her bag, and buzzing,
and buzzing.

---

She sat in her car between items four and five
with the air conditioning losing its argument with August,
zinnias sweating in their paper on the passenger seat,
and opened the group.

The parents' board she ran was a tidy machine on a normal day.
Pinned threads, patient answers,
*FAFSA Night (New Date)* and
*Parents' Weekend Hotel Blocks — READ FIRST*
sitting at the top like well-behaved children.
By noon it had a new thread with a title
in full capital letters and eleven exclamation points,
four hundred replies, and casserole-level mobilization
already underway in the comments.
Somebody had questions about the trustees.
Somebody had a cousin who was a lawyer.
Somebody proposed a bake sale with a confidence
that suggested the House could be saved
one pound cake at a time, and honestly,
in this town, Marisol wouldn't have bet against it.

The join queue had gone off like a popcorn pan too.
Four hundred and some new member requests since morning,
shiny profiles wanting in on the day the town
needed all its people.
She thumbed Approve, and Approve,
and Approve All where the app permitted,
welcoming the lot of them in the time it took
the light at the square to change twice.
It was less deliberation than she'd given the zinnias.
She felt tidily, pleasantly useful about it,
the day's one unambiguous good deed,
and moved on to item five.

---

By dusk the hickory smoke over Delmar's had gone
from column to ribbon,
the screen porch was elbow to elbow,
and the radio behind the register was letting
the fourth quarter into the room a few feet at a time.

The clipboard sat beside the register
as if it had been born there.
A legal pad under the clip, a pickle jar of pens,
and one pen zip-tied to the board itself
in a spirit of realism about the human race.
The top sheet was full and the second sheet was filling.
Names in ballpoint, names in pencil,
one name in what appeared to be barbecue sauce.
Nobody had put a line on it for comments,
and several people had located room for some anyway.

By suppertime the town had already named it
the House's second guest book,
and the name was out doing laps.

Marisol was reading down the first page
when Ray came off the smoker,
wiped his hands on his apron,
picked up the zip-tied pen,
and signed without being asked and without a word,
which from Ray amounted to an oration.
He put the pen back and returned to the smoke.

She took the pen up while it was still warm from his grip,
found the next open line,
and signed herself onto it full-length,
big enough to read from a porch,
the zip tie dragging the whole jar half an inch
along the counter as she crossed the t.
Let November come read the sheet.

Down Millrow the House sat back off the street
in the last of the light, porch empty, roof line easy,
looking like the least gray thing in the county.

She picked up her supper at the window when it came,
told Ray his smoke smelled like a winning argument,
got the eyebrow that passed with him for a thank-you,
and left the second guest book
one name longer than she'd found it.
