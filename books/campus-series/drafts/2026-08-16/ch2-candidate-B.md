# Chapter 2 — The Man Who Keeps It Running

POV: Cal Sutter, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate B. Variance card: D3.

---

Cal Sutter wanted a quiet season.

He was not asking for much.
Mowers that started. Dorms that shed water.
WDSS 1340 on the shop radio, low, all day long.
And nobody, anywhere on the whole green face of campus,
needing him to attend anything.

He had said so out loud on Friday, to the crew,
and the crew had heard him out with great respect.
Then his small-engine man wished him luck
in the tone people save for funerals.

Monday morning the town smelled like the day after a party,
which it was.
Delmar's had banked its fires sometime Sunday,
but the hickory hung on over the square anyway,
faint and sweet, smoke with nowhere it had to be.
The fairgrounds were a field again.
The RV city had packed up and rolled out overnight,
leaving neat rows of flattened grass,
a ghost map of somebody's good weekend.

Campus had the stunned look it always got
on the Monday after the first home Saturday.
Trash cans filled to legend.
One shoe on the library steps.
A folding chair in a tree, and nobody asking how.

Cal drove the back road to the motor pool at six forty
with the window down and his coffee in the cupholder,
and felt about as close to happy
as he allowed himself to get before seven.

The motor pool was his.
Not on paper. On paper it belonged to the university,
same as everything else on his key ring.
But paper had never once fixed a pump,
and everybody knew whose yard it was.

The key ring was a fact of nature by now.
It weighed most of a pound and rode his left hip,
and he could find any key on it in the dark,
by feel, without slowing down.
The whole campus ran through it.
People said that about him like it was a compliment,
and Cal supposed it was,
and some days it just felt like weight.

Ratchet was already on shift.

She had her own parking spot, second from the end,
her name stenciled on the asphalt in the same white paint
as the visitor spaces. Nobody remembered voting on that.
It had simply become true, and now it was law.
She met Cal's truck at the gate, walked it half a circle,
nose up, tail on official business,
and issued him his grade, which was a B.
Cal had made his peace with the B.
The A was reserved. In all his years in that yard
he had never seen anybody get the A.

The crew rolled in by seven,
coolers and radios and one leftover sunburn apiece.
The pressure washer started on the first pull,
and everyone agreed not to mention it out loud,
in case it heard.

By seven thirty the office phone was going steady.
The woman who ran his tickets called the count
through the window like a race announcer.

"Forty-one work orders from the dorms," she said. "Forty-two.
A shower head, two window screens, and a swing set."

"We don't have a swing set," Cal said.

"They made one."

He put that ticket on top.
A thing built wrong was more dangerous than a thing broken,
and besides, he wanted to see it.

The wide-area mower had come back from the weekend
refusing to start, and two of his men had taken turns
being sure about the fuel line.
Cal stood by it and listened to it crank once.

"Seat switch," he said, and kept walking.

By the time he'd poured his second coffee
the mower was running and both men were quiet.
Revisions were being made.

The golf carts had come back from Saturday abused.
One had a bent rim and a smell nobody would claim.
Cal put it on the lift himself,
spun the wheel, read the wobble,
and had the rim swapped before the parts guy
finished saying they were probably out of stock.
They were not out of stock.
Cal knew what was on his own shelves.

The phone kept up all morning.
The bursar's office had an air handler making a sound
they described, in writing, as haunted.
A loading dock gate was down again across campus.
A delivery app kept sending drivers to the wrong side
of a fence with no gate in it,
and every driver called the number posted on the fence,
and the number posted on the fence was, in the end, Cal.
He gave directions.
He did not sigh where anyone could hear it.

At some point in the morning a foil-covered dish
appeared on the seat of his truck. Third one this month.
The town had decided, a while back,
that a man on his own could not be trusted to eat,
and the town would not hear appeals,
and the town was not entirely wrong.
He set it in the shop fridge with the others
and wrote THANK YOU on the whiteboard,
which the whole crew knew was addressed to the county at large.

On the end of his bench sat a crescent wrench,
wrong size for anything he owned.
It had come home with him Saturday.
He had not decided anything about it yet.
It sat there being the wrong size,
and once, passing, he squared it up with the edge of the bench.
That was all.

---

Around ten he sat down at the shop computer
to order gutter screws before the fall storms,
and the calendar reminder was waiting for him,
patient as a process server.

CONDITION SURVEY — MILLROW STRUCTURES.
Thursday, two o'clock. From the development office.
No name attached. Development-office invites never had names.
They came down the hill like weather.

He had declined it once already, last week,
on the principle that he had never accepted it.
It had come back anyway, undented.
Declining a meeting twice meant somebody
walked down the hill to come find you,
and then you were having the meeting in your own yard,
standing up, with witnesses.
So this time he opened the thing.

Cal's position on meetings was, roughly,
Ratchet's position on bath day.
Both of them could technically be caught.
Neither was going to come when called,
and there would be a certain amount of low growling.

The invite had an attachment, and the attachment was a list.
Addresses on Millrow Street, down the hill at the edge of campus,
the row of old buildings nobody budgeted for
and everybody photographed when the leaves turned.
He read down the list, one line at a time.

The last line said Hartwell House.

He sat still.

Out in the yard the pressure washer ran on,
and somebody laughed at something,
and WDSS played whatever it was playing,
all of it one thin wall away
and briefly none of his.
He read the list again, top to bottom, at the same speed,
like a man rechecking a torque he already knew.
Then he clicked accept, and sat a moment longer
with his hand still on the mouse.

Then he ordered the gutter screws,
because that was what he had sat down to do.

Nobody in the shop heard about the meeting.
There was nothing to hear. It was a survey.
Surveys happened.
He told himself that twice,
which was once more than usually necessary.

---

The crew knocked off at four.
Cal locked the shop at five, walked the gate chain twice,
and did not go home.

Ratchet was in the truck before the door was all the way open.
She did that some evenings. She seemed to know which ones.
He had quit asking how.

Millrow Street ran along the downhill edge of campus,
low and shaded, oaks meeting overhead,
the kind of street the university owned all around
but not quite through.
The light came down through the leaves in pieces
and lay on the pavement like change.

Hartwell House sat halfway down, back off the street.
White frame, deep porch, tall windows,
older than most things in town that weren't a church.
A trust owned it. Not the university.
It was nobody's work order, and it never had been.

Cal's key ring opened every building on campus.
The key to Hartwell House was not on it.
That key rode by itself in his shirt pocket,
on a plain steel ring with nothing else,
and it did not get explained to anyone,
because no one had ever thought to ask.

He parked in the gravel drive.
Ratchet took the porch, lay down at the top of the steps
like hired security, and was asleep inside a minute.
Cal got the ladder off the rack
and went around back while there was light.

The gutters had caught two weeks of oak trash
and one tennis ball, origin unknown.
He worked the eave a section at a time,
throwing down handfuls, checking the hangers as he went,
and when he ran the hose the water walked the whole run
and came down the downspout clean.
September storms were coming.
The gutters didn't know that.
Now they didn't need to.

The boiler lived in the cellar and had seniority.
It was older than the trust that owned the house,
and it had opinions.
Cal bled the air out of the lines,
drained a bucket of rust-colored water off the low point,
and knelt on the cool concrete to check the pilot,
cheek nearly to the floor.
The flame stood up blue and steady.

"All right," he told it. "Don't get proud."

He topped off the pressure and listened to the pipes tick,
and then stood a while with the flashlight off,
just listening.
A house will tell you things in the dark
that it keeps to itself in the daytime.
This one was quiet, floor to roof.
Quiet was the whole job.
He turned the light back on and went up.

The last thing was the kitchen faucet,
a slow drip he had been meaning to get to.
The kitchen was at the back of the house,
which meant the front hall,
which meant the stairs.

He crossed the hall with his eyes level and forward.
The staircase went up along the left-hand wall,
and there were photographs on that wall, going up with the steps,
frames catching the little bit of streetlight from the porch glass.
He did not look at them.
He was good at not looking at them.
He had had practice.

The third step showed its wear even in the half dark,
the tread worn pale in the middle,
a shallow scoop you could read with a thumb.
He went on by. He fixed the faucet.
It took ten minutes and two washers,
and afterward he wiped the water off the porcelain,
and then he wiped it again.

He let himself out the back and came around front,
and stood a minute in the yard with the toolbox in his hand,
looking the place over, sills to ridgeline,
while the last of the light went orange on the upstairs glass.
Then he locked the front door and checked it,
which was not the same as doubting it.

The house went dark behind him,
which was how it was supposed to look.
Nobody drove down Millrow much after supper.
Nobody counted the trucks that did.

Ratchet stood, stretched fore and aft,
and was in the truck before he was.

He came up Millrow with the windows down.
The evening was cooling off in installments,
the cicadas trading shifts in the oaks.
WDSS had the swap-and-shop rebroadcast on,
and a man three towns over had a stock trailer
he didn't need anymore,
and a woman on the very same road, it turned out,
had been wanting one all summer.
Cal turned it up a little. He liked this part.
He drove home listening to the two of them find each other,
and Ratchet rode with her head out the window,
grading the dark,
finding it acceptable.
