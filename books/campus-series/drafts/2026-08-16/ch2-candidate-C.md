# Chapter 2 — Deferred Maintenance

POV: Cal Sutter, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate: C
Variance card: D4

---

The first home Saturday had taken all week to arrive
and all weekend to leave,
and everything it left behind was Cal Sutter's problem.
That suited him.
A quiet season, in his experience, was not a season
where nothing went wrong.
It was a season where everything that went wrong
went wrong to somebody who knew what to do about it,
and the list of things Cal Sutter did not know what to do about
was short, and he kept it that way.

He had the motor pool gates open by six fifteen.
The coffee maker, older than half his crew and twice as dependable,
was doing its work by six twenty,
and WDSS 1340 came up off the shop radio into the rafters,
the Monday man reading out the weekend lost-and-found
in a voice built for exactly that.
One cooler, blue, heavier than it ought to be.
One folding chair, camouflage, which was maybe why.
One set of upper dentures, recovered from the fairgrounds,
described on air, with dignity, as gently used.
Cal turned it up a notch and unlocked the parts cage.
So far, a perfect Monday.

Ratchet was at her post before the first truck rolled in.
She had a parking spot of her own at the end of the row,
RATCHET stenciled on the asphalt
in the same official yellow as the director's,
and she received each arriving vehicle from the middle of it,
seated, ears up, patient as a toll booth.
She graded everybody who crossed the yard.
There were two grades.
Either Ratchet stood up for you or she did not,
and in the history of the motor pool
no appeal had ever been granted.

His crew chief came through the office door sideways,
on account of the clipboard.

"Sixty-one work orders out of the dorms," he said. "Sixty-one.
It's syllabus week. What are they doing to my buildings?"

"Living in them," Cal said.

"One of them says a ceiling is humming."

"That's the fourth-floor fan coil. It hums when the filter
loads up. Somebody's drying laundry on the return grille again."
Cal signed the top sheet without sitting down. "Forty of those
will be air conditioning, thirty-nine of the forty will be
the thermostat, and the thermostat will be fine. Put the new kid
on the mowing rotation with the veterans, and start the rest
at the top of the hill and work down."

The wall phone rang at seven and kept at it all morning,
which was the town taking attendance.
The chapel called about the organ blower.
"It's not the blower, it's the belt," Cal said. "It squeals
till the room warms up. Run it five minutes
before choir practice and it'll behave itself."
The press box called about the door.
"Door's not stuck, it's proud," Cal said. "Lift on the handle,
then push. Don't shoulder it. You'll bruise yourself
and embarrass the door."

Midmorning, the gooseneck came back loaded with three zero-turns
and had to go into bay two,
which was the one job in the yard everybody had an opinion about.
Bay two sat between a steel pillar and the parts shelving,
with a hand's width of grace on either side,
and backing forty feet of trailer into that slot
was the shop's unofficial licensing exam.
Veterans took three cuts at it on a good day.
On a bad day they told God things about that pillar
that God presumably wrote down.

The new kid did it in one.

He'd been on the crew three weeks,
distinguished so far mainly by enthusiasm
and by filing the flat screwdrivers in the Phillips drawer,
and Ratchet had not stood up for him once.
But he swung the truck wide, dropped it into reverse,
and put that trailer through the gap in one pass,
off the mirrors, one palm riding the wheel,
talking himself through none of it,
and stepped down out of the cab
as if nothing in particular had occurred.

The yard had gone quiet somewhere in the middle of it.
Somebody set a torque wrench down very gently.

"Cattle trailer," the kid said, to the silence. "My granddad's.
You back one into a sale barn at fourteen
with him watching, you can back anything into anywhere."

Ratchet got up from her spot, crossed the yard,
and sat down on the kid's boot.
Regraded, on the spot, in front of everybody.

"Well," Cal said. He looked at the trailer, then at the kid.
"Don't teach the others. I need something to hold over them."

At the end of Cal's own bench, meanwhile,
sat a six-inch crescent wrench that was not his,
confiscated Saturday from a tent
that had been losing a fight with it.
Under it was a note in his own handwriting that said RETURN.
It had said RETURN since Saturday night.
He filed the matter under later in the week,
on the grounds that a man should not rush a Monday,
and got on with the day.

---

His office was a desk in the corner of the shop
with a window on the yard,
and a calendar he consulted under protest.

Cal's position on meetings was, give or take,
Ratchet's position on bath day.
He accepted that meetings existed.
He accepted that once in a while, through no fault of his own,
he would be caught and made to sit through one.
His policy was to be elsewhere, doing something true,
and the policy had never once failed him.

Which is why the thing sitting on his Thursday stopped him
with the coffee halfway up.

CONDITION SURVEY — MILLROW STRUCTURES.
Thursday, two o'clock. Sent from the development office,
no human being's name attached,
and he had never accepted it.
It had accepted itself,
and there it sat on his calendar like it paid rent.

He reached for decline on principle
and opened the attachment out of professional spite.
A scope list. Buildings at the tired end of Millrow Street,
sheds and storehouses nobody had loved in years,
and he ran down the list with his jaw already set for boredom.

Fourth line from the bottom: Hartwell House, Millrow Street.

His stomach dropped.

He set the coffee down and read the list again,
top to bottom, slower,
as if the first pass might have been a filing error,
and his stomach dropped at the same line the second time.

For a minute the shop radio played to a man
who wasn't hearing a word of it.

Then Cal Sutter did something that would have made
his crew chief check him for fever.
He accepted the meeting.
First try, no growling, inside of a minute,
the fastest yes the development office had ever
gotten out of facilities,
and if anybody who knew him had been watching,
they'd have known on the spot that something was wrong.

The wall phone rang again.
"Lift, then push," Cal said, before the voice got started.
"Like you're fond of it."
He hung up and stood at the window a while,
looking at a yard where everything worked.

---

He clocked the crew out at four thirty
and stayed on past it till the yard emptied,
and then a while longer, till the back road went quiet too.
Ratchet walked him to his truck and did not offer to come along,
which was their arrangement.

The truck was his own — no seal on the door,
no two-way crackle, a bench seat and a toolbox
that answered to nobody's inventory.
On his belt rode the ring the whole campus ran through,
gates and boiler rooms, the press box, the chapel
with its squealing belt, keys with a purpose apiece.
The key he drove down Millrow Street to use was not on it.
It lived by itself on a plain steel ring in the ashtray,
and no list anywhere said what it opened.

Millrow was the downhill edge of everything,
where the sidewalk gave out and the streetlights
stood farther apart, like they'd had a falling-out.
Hartwell House sat back off the road behind two oaks,
trust-owned, which is a way of saying
owned by nobody who would ever drive past.
Cal parked around the side, off the street,
where a truck drew no questions,
the same as always.

The boiler first.
Down the outside steps, pull-chain light,
and there she was, cast iron, older than the trust,
an argument he had been winning by one point for years.
He lit her, watched the pressure come up honest,
let her run long enough to prove herself.
It was August, and nobody on earth needed heat,
but a boiler you only visit in October
is a boiler that quits on you in November,
and this one was not allowed to quit.
He shut her down satisfied.

The gutters next.
The northeast downspout had been adopted, again,
by a squirrel with real estate ambitions,
and he cleared a whole acorn operation out of the elbow
and ran the hose till the water came through clean.
He reset a popped gutter spike while he was up there
and took the long look around a roof gets
from a man on a ladder who knows what he's looking at.

Then the house, because he always finished inside.

He let himself in the back and went through the rooms
without turning on lights, because he didn't need them.
Kitchen faucet, half a turn past the drip it liked to develop.
The hall clock had run down over the weekend, and he wound it.
Window sashes, front parlor, both latches.
The house made the small ticking sounds of a house
being listened to, and he listened.

The staircase he did last, and quickest.
Photographs climbed the stairwell wall, frame over frame,
all the long climb up,
and he kept his eyes on the treads going up
and on the treads coming down, the same as every visit.
He checked the handrail bolt at the turn. Solid.
The third step was worn pale in the middle,
dished soft from years of feet,
and he set his boot at the edge of it, off the wear,
both directions, without ever quite deciding to,
and he did not look at the wall,
and that was the whole visit.

None of it was on a work order.
No folder in the facilities office had ever heard of this address,
and the receipts — washers, a gutter spike,
a thermocouple in the spring — were paid in cash
and lived in the glove box, submitted to nobody.
The university did not know he came here.
The town did not know he came here.
As far as Ashford was concerned,
Cal Sutter went home at four thirty like everybody else,
and he did the quiet handful of things it took
to keep Ashford concerned with something else.

He locked the back door and checked the latch twice,
which the latch did not require.
Out front the porch light came on behind him,
on its timer, right on schedule,
because he had set the timer and he kept it running.
From the sidewalk the house looked warm and kept,
and he stood there long enough to be sure of it.
Then he got in the truck and drove up the hill toward supper,
the whole campus locked behind him for the night
and one house on Millrow Street
locked a little better than that.
