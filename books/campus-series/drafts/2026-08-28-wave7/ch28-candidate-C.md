# Chapter 28 — I Yield My Time

POV: Cal, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate C. Variance card: D1.

---

Cal heard the meeting before he saw it.

From the lot the gym had the sound it kept for game nights,
a hum coming through brick and double doors,
and he sat in the truck with the engine off
and the folder on his knee until he was sure what he was hearing.
The Curb Market vans stood nosed along the fence,
sides still lettered for Saturday.
The quarter hour came down off the clock tower,
four notes across the cold,
and under the last note the doors opened for somebody
and let the noise out plain.
The town had come indoors.

Inside, the floor was tarped and the tarp was full.
Folding chairs ran back past the free-throw line
and more were still coming open,
that flat aluminum clap, one after another,
a running count of how many neighbors kept arriving.
Coats hung over chair backs. Breath showed on the overflow steps.
The radiators along the north wall ticked like a pit crew,
fast and businesslike, all hands on the cold.
On a cafeteria table by the door sat casseroles still in foil,
a regiment of thermoses, two pound cakes,
and a coffee urn with a bad gasket that sighed every few minutes.
Somebody had scheduled the biggest civic fight in county memory
and the town had catered it like a church supper.

"It's a public hearing, Earlene," Verna said, taking a plate anyway.

"So's supper," Earlene said, and kept serving.

The Liars' Table had turned out in Sunday clothes,
a whole row of pressed collars and stiff necks.
Ray had a tie on. "These are the good chairs," he said.

Tick had a card table under the scoreboard,
one microphone taped to a stand and a cable run out the door,
carrying the session live on WDSS
for everyone whose knees or babies had kept them home.
He gave Cal a nod and pointed at the mic, meaning it was hot.

Cal took a place standing along the wall with his crew,
all twelve of them, Ronnie from bay three at his shoulder,
Travis and DeShawn still in work canvas,
his second in her one wool coat,
holding the drawing tubes flat against her chest
so the paper wouldn't crease.
He had asked them not to sit,
because he wanted the board to see the winter he was offering
standing up.
He listened to the chairs keep clapping open behind him
and understood something he had no plans to say out loud,
which was that all this had not come for the towers,
and it had not come for Boyd.

The chair rapped the table with a knuckle and the PA popped.
"This is a public session of the board of trustees.
One item tonight. The Boyd gift, as offered.
Full footprint or none.
The condemnation survey is before the board as due diligence.
Public comment to follow. Three minutes a head."

Boyd rose from the front row and did not need the podium.

"I'll be brief," he said,
and he was the quietest thing in the building,
so the whole room went still to hear him,
which he knew, and had dressed for.
"The gift stands as offered. All of it or none of it.
Half a gift is half a promise, and I don't make those.
As for the survey, your own diligence brought it to you.
I take no pleasure in what it found.
The responsible choice, regrettably, runs through Millrow."
He sat down to the sound of his own chair, and nothing else.

Ronnie tipped his head an inch.
"Respectfully done," he said, low. "Like a vet putting you down."

Cal kept his eyes on the bound survey at the chair's elbow,
the page inside it with a signature block he had never touched,
and his thumb rode the edge of his own folder,
because a season of being talked about had come down to one table
and one stack of honest paper set against one dishonest one.

The chair read names off index cards
and the comment line formed under the far basket.
Verna took her three minutes to hold up the clipboard
and say there were near seven thousand names on it now,
and that she had witnessed most of the signatures personally
and would witness the rest on request.
Peanut came up with an index card of his own,
looked out at the coats and the thermoses,
and put the card back in his jacket.
"I had something funny," he said. "It isn't the day for it.
Fix the House." He sat down.
The joke had died somewhere back in that line,
and the room had let it, and Cal heard the difference,
because a gym full of this town gone quiet on purpose
was a sound you could stand a ladder on.

The chair called the next card. "Pruett."

She came up the aisle in the sound of turning chairs.
She had a pen over one ear that she was not going to need.
She brought the mic down to her height with one small squeak
through the PA, both hands sure on the stand,
and stood at it as the owner of any room she had ever entered.

"State your name for the record."

"Marisol Pruett."

"You have three minutes."

"I know exactly what three minutes are," she said,
and the crowd made its small sound of knowing her right back.
"I yield my time to the director of facilities."

Cal's ears went hot.

He knew what her three minutes were worth
better than any person in the building,
because he had spent a season watching her spend them,
three minutes to seat a county fair,
three minutes to park a stadium,
three minutes to fill a jar in every store window on the square.
She had never in her life given a minute away.
Last night they had each tried to hand the other a door out,
and neither one had taken it, and she had left alone.
Here was her answer, delivered in public, on the record.
She stayed in the room, and she handed him the floor,
and the floor was the most expensive thing she owned.

"Is the director present," the chair said, not quite a question.

Cal came off the wall.
His boots on the tarp were the only sound in the place.
Rows of chairs creaked as they turned to follow him up the aisle,
a slow wave of aluminum he walked through,
and his second met him halfway with the tubes and the folder
and took the wall back without a word.
At the podium he raised the mic, the squeak going the other way,
and heard his first words come back off the far wall
a half second late, so he slowed down and let his voice catch up.
He would rather have rewired the building in the dark.
He had kept quiet all season
because everything he said got printed sideways,
and the quiet had done its own damage.
He talked anyway.

"My name is Cal Sutter. I'm the director of facilities.
I did not sign the survey on that table.
I want that on the record first,
since the county's had a season to wonder about it.
What I have signed, I'm about to show you."

He laid the drawings out on the exhibit table
beside the clipboard, and the mic picked up the paper,
a dry sliding sound the whole gym got to hear.

"This is a restorability report on Hartwell House.
Measured drawings. Real numbers. They're the boring kind.
The boring kind check out.

"The survey calls the north wall failed.
On the north wall there are glass tell-tale slides,
set across the cracks and dated in grease pencil.
You glue a slide over a crack, and you write the date on it,
and if the wall ever moves, the glass breaks.
It's the simplest instrument there is.
It can't be argued with and it can't be rushed."

He turned a page and let the PA carry that too.

"The log for those slides runs back years.
The first half of it is in my handwriting."
He kept his eyes on the page.
"The rest is in a better hand.
She kept the watch on that wall longer than I did.
Every slide on it is whole.
Dated glass doesn't care what a page says."

Nobody in the room moved.
Under his voice the radiators went on ticking alone.

"The wiring is the true problem,
and the survey is right about it,
and it is the one thing in there that's right.
It's priced in this report, and it's priced first.
Rewire before plaster, before paint,
before anything that shows,
because you fix what can kill you
before you fix what embarrasses you.

"The last pages are my crew. Twelve of us.
Our winter is attached in writing,
scaffolding up through March.
Ronnie priced the lifts himself,
and he priced them like a man who'd have to stand on them.
Behind every number in here is an engineer's stamp.
He's independent. He's nobody's man.
That's why I asked him.

"The survey says the House's upkeep was never on the books.
That part is true.
I did it on Saturdays, off the clock,
and I never wrote it down, and that's mine to answer for."
He said the next part to the drawings, not to the board.
"You were never behind. You were unrecorded.
There's a difference."

He took out the pen and uncapped it,
and the click of the cap went out over the PA
and out the cable to every kitchen listening on WDSS.

"There's a signature block on the last page,
for whoever will stand behind these numbers.
I never signed theirs. I'm signing mine."

He signed it flat against the podium,
and the mic carried the scratch of the nib to the back rows,
his name going down where the whole county could hear it land.
Then he squared the pages and gave the room one more sentence,
because one was all it needed and the town would do the rest.

"I was married on that porch,
and the House has kept that day for me ever since."

He capped the pen.

"I yield the rest of my time."

For a moment the gym was all radiators.
Then the whole room let its breath go at once, everybody together,
one long sound rolling up into the rafters,
and somewhere inside it a thermos cap went quietly back on.
Cal walked back through it to the wall.
Ronnie's hand landed on his shoulder and stayed.
Marisol sat facing front in the middle of the room
she had given away, her chin not quite level,
and he made himself look at the board instead.

The half hour came down off the clock tower
while the trustees leaned into each other,
hands over microphones, a minute of murmur and paper.
Then the chair straightened.

"The chair will entertain a motion."

"Move that the board accept the gift as amended,"
a trustee said. "All phases save Millrow.
The Millrow phase is tabled pending independent review."

"Second."

"All in favor."

The ayes went down the table in a row.

"Opposed." The radiators ticked. "Motion carries.
The board accepts the gift shy of Millrow."

Before the chair had found the next page,
a gray-haired man stood up in the third row
with the pledge folder under his arm.
He did not go to the podium. The gym carried him fine.

"I speak for the trust," he said.
"Hartwell House has been the trust's all along,
and it stays the trust's.
Restoration proceeds on the report you just heard.
The money is in this folder, pledged jar by jar,
and the town is welcome to count it any Tuesday it likes."
He looked over at the wall where the crew stood.
"And the crew is contracted through March. Signed this morning.
We only ever lacked the numbers,
and now the numbers have a name on them."

Ronnie made a sound low in his chest
like a jack letting a truck down easy.
Down the wall the crew was shaking hands with itself.

Boyd was already on his feet, buttoning his jacket.
"Ladies. Gentlemen. You've been diligent and I thank you for it,"
he said, warm as a banker at a christening.
"The towers proceed. They never needed Millrow to be beautiful."
He shook the nearest trustee's hand
and went up the aisle at his own pace,
and near the doors he inclined his head
to the young man carrying his coat,
and the gym's acoustics did Cal one last favor,
because volume one carries in a room built for whistles.
"Call the survey people. Move the demolition walk up.
It's a new date, not a moved one.
I don't move a date on a man twice. It's rude."
He went out, and the cold came in for a second behind him,
and the doors banged shut.

The trust's man stayed on his feet,
the folder held up where the whole gym could see it,
and the room came up around him, row by row,
chairs scraping back off the tarp, coats swinging on,
the radiators ticking away under the rising noise
until the place sounded like the first home Saturday
let indoors out of the cold.
Cal stood against the wall with his crew,
their winter signed and a wall of whole glass behind them,
and across all the folding chairs he found Marisol,
on her feet with everyone else,
in the middle of the room she had handed him
and the town had filled.
