# Chapter 26 — Wind in the Upper Deck

POV: Cal, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate B. Variance card: D1.

---

Rex Boyd asked for Wednesday at the stadium,
which meant the stadium would be empty,
which meant empty was the point.
Cal went, because you learn what a man means to do
by seeing where he wants to stand while he tells you.

They rode the freight elevator up from the north tunnel,
Boyd making pleasant small weather about the concourse tile,
and came out onto the upper deck and into the sound.

Cal had been in the bowl a hundred times empty, working,
and it had never once been quiet.
The wind was in the girders overhead,
a long tone off the steel, climbing and settling,
climbing and settling,
and under it the whole structure ticked,
concrete and aluminum trading the morning's heat,
thousands of small clicks with no pattern to them,
as if the building were counting something
and keeping the total to itself.
Somewhere down in the lower bowl a chain knocked
against a gate, twice, and stopped.

Boyd went to the rail. The topcoat was too good for the concrete
and he wore it anyway, and he stood listening a while,
gracious even about weather.

"You hear that," Boyd said. Not loud.
He didn't need loud; there was nothing up here to talk over.
"Ninety thousand seats of nobody, and the wind's the whole crowd.
My architects tell me the expansion changes that sound.
I told them to keep it."

Cal put his hands on the rail and waited.
The steel was cold through his palms
and the tone ran along it faintly, right into the bone.

"The trustees are here the first Tuesday of November,"
Boyd said. "I would like to hand them a settled town.
I think you would too, whatever you think of me,
and I have quit guessing what you think of me."

Cal didn't answer that. He kept his hands where they were.

"I'll come to it," Boyd said. "You came without being chased,
and I return that in kind. The new plaza, off this concourse.
There's a wall at the mouth of it, first thing a person sees
coming in on a Saturday, last thing going out. Bronze on granite."
He turned from the rail. "Her name, Cal. I'd put it
where ninety thousand people read it every Saturday.
Cast, not engraved. There as long as this building's here.
And the winter fund doubles. All of it for ink by Friday."

The tone off the girders climbed and settled.

For the length of one breath Cal could hear it,
Saturdays from now, strangers slowing at a wall,
her name said out loud by people who never got to say it to her.
His chest did a thing he hadn't asked it to do.
The worst of it was that he wanted it,
and he made himself admit that, standing right there,
because a man ought to know the exact weight
of the thing he is about to put down.

He resettled his cap and set his feet
as if the concourse had a pitch to it.

"No," Cal said.

He didn't dress it up, and he didn't stay for the reply.

He was ten rows shy of the tunnel when Boyd's voice
came down after him, no louder than it had been at the rail.
The bowl carried it; that is what bowls are built to do.

"Triple," Boyd said. "The fund triples, and Friday holds.
I don't move a date on a man twice, Cal. It's rude."

In the tunnel the wind quit all at once,
and the ticking followed him down the ramp, fainter,
and then it was just his boots.
The carillon was ringing noon when he came out
into the empty lot.

---

Thursday morning the shop was every one of its usual sounds,
the impact wrench going in bay three,
the compressor cycling behind the wall,
DeShawn's radio holding its ground against the grinder,
and then, inside ten seconds, it wasn't.
Cal looked up from the bench, because a shop that goes quiet
is a shop where something's wrong.

Ratchet stood at the open bay door with her tail going,
which she did for about four living people.
Past her, in the doorway light, stood the engineer
from the yard fence, same canvas jacket, same surveyor's squint,
running it down the bays like a man checking their fall.

"Heard a rumor about drawings," he said.

Cal wiped his hands clean before he went to the truck,
because the drawings had been made clean
and were going to stay that way,
and pulled the tube from behind the seat.
They rolled the sheets out on the steel bench
and weighted the corners with sockets,
and the man went through them one at a time,
paper snapping in the draft off the door.
The crew kept working and kept it low.
Even Ronnie's impact came in short, polite bursts.
The engineer asked two questions about the shoring
and one about the load path, and Cal answered all three,
and for a while the only sound between them was pages.

"These sill numbers," the man said once. "Yours?"

"Measured twice," Cal said. "Two of us on the tape."

"Who held the dumb end?"

"Ronnie," Cal said, and from bay three Ronnie said,
"I heard that," without turning around,
and the man went back to the sheets.

"You shot these elevations at night," he said.

"Off ladders," Cal said. "Both gables."

"It shows. A ladder doesn't let a man round up."
He squared the sheets with the flat of his hand.

"Whose man are you?" Cal said. "I never did learn."

The squint came around to him.
"Nobody's," the engineer said. "Never have been.
Makes me a poor hire and a fair witness."
He tapped the top sheet with two knuckles.
"You were told a finding gets answered by work
of equal standing. This stands.
Send me the last two sheets when they're inked,
and they go out over my stamp."

He gave Ratchet's ear one scratch on his way to the door,
lifted two fingers without turning around, and was gone.

Cal stood a minute with his hand flat on the drawings,
pressing down a curled corner that didn't need pressing.
Then DeShawn's radio came back up, and the grinder after it,
and the shop went back to being loud.

---

Thursday night Peanut Kyzer flipped the sign at the Blind Mule
and locked the front door from the inside with everybody in it.

Cal hadn't stood in the room since the night
it ran out of glasses. Closed, it was a different room.
No jukebox, no talk at the rail, half the chairs
still legs-up on the tables, and under all of it
the bottle cooler humming to itself,
so steady you quit hearing it until somebody opened the lid.

The pool table had turned into a border.
Coveralls down one side, still smelling of the shop.
Clipboard people down the other, still dressed from work,
and Marisol among them like she was born to borders.
Cal's second had brought the Checkerboard pie, two of them,
still in their tins, and Peanut dealt forks
straight off the wash rack, and for a minute
the two sides of that table had nothing to argue,
on account of their mouths being full.
Peanut poured coffee for the clipboards,
drew two pitchers for the coveralls,
and set a ginger ale at Cal's elbow without being asked.

"Board's yours," Peanut said. "Chalk's under the register.
Anybody writes on my specials board,
it better be worth reading."

The chalk had its own sound in that quiet,
tap and drag, tap and drag,
and the room went still enough to hear the drag part.
Cal put Boyd's winter list on the left,
line by line, the decking, the demo, the rough carpentry,
twelve names' worth of work.
On the right he put the House.
Scaffolding, shoring, the roof, the gable rebuild,
the same trades, the same paychecks, contract through March.
He wrote MARCH at the bottom and underlined it once,
and the scrape of the underline carried to the back wall.

Then he set the chalk on the rail and stepped away,
dusting his fingers down his jeans,
because a crew talked into a thing isn't a crew,
it's an audience.

"Same paychecks says who?" Ronnie had a fork in one hand
and hadn't put it down to argue. "Chalk's cheap, Cal.
My bank doesn't cash chalk."

"There's a stamp going behind those numbers," DeShawn said.
"As of this morning. I watched it happen."

"A stamp's not a contract," Ronnie said.

"The contract's real," Marisol said. "Drawn through March.
It's in the truck. You can read every page of it tonight."

"And when Boyd's decks start without us?" Travis said.
"He crews up from out of county and we get to watch."

"Then we watch from scaffolding with our names
on the checks," DeShawn said.

"You ever hang decking in January?" Travis said.
"I left a glove frozen to a joist one year.
Got it back in April, with a pry bar."

"House work's half inside by the new year," DeShawn said.
"Look at the board. Roof's dried in by then or we're all
in worse trouble than a glove."

Travis nodded slowly, and then said, to nobody in particular,
"My kitchen's one of the twelve."

The cooler hummed. Forks moved. The board stayed lit
under Peanut's one good pendant lamp.

"For the record," Peanut said, arms crossed at his own bar,
"that is the most anybody has ever ordered off that board,
and I once watched a wedding party try to drink it blank."
The coverall side laughed, and most of the clipboards,
and Ronnie let himself grin around the fork.

Then Ronnie carried his beer over and stood at the board
and read the right-hand column top to bottom,
lips moving on the numbers.
Nobody hurried him.

"March is far," Ronnie said.

"So is winter," Cal said, "from November,
with nothing on the books."

Ronnie read it through one more time.
"All right," he said. "It adds."

Nobody called for hands. Peanut cut the second pie,
and the two sides of the pool table
reached over it from both directions,
and Marisol pressed Cal's ginger ale back into his hand
on her way past, cold from sitting, and he drank it anyway.

---

Friday morning Ronnie was in before the compressor.
The pledge letter had hung on the break-room corkboard
for weeks, Boyd's letterhead yellowing at the pins,
the word "pending" penciled across one corner
where somebody had been keeping score.
When Cal came through with his coffee,
Ronnie was standing in front of it.
He pulled the two pushpins, and the pins went
tick, tick, into the little cup on the sill,
and he held the letter out.

"Need the wall space," Ronnie said.

Cal took it and put it in the locked drawer,
because the drawer was for things that were settled,
and this was.

The shop ran loud all day, ordinary Friday noise,
impact and grinder and DeShawn's radio,
Ratchet asleep in the warm spot by bay three.
At noon Cal cleared the bench and inked the last two sheets,
the pen scratch small under all that racket,
and tubed them up for the man with the stamp.
Boyd's paper stayed in its folder
on the corner of the desk, where it had sat all week.
The carillon came in faint through the open bays at five,
and the crew racked tools and rolled the doors down
one at a time, each door louder in an emptier shop,
and the compressor bled off with a long hiss and quit.

Friday ended. Nothing got signed.
