# Chapter 26 — Wind in the Upper Deck

POV: Cal, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate A. Variance card: D3.

---

Boyd's man was waiting at the tunnel gate Wednesday morning.
He didn't say where they were going. He didn't need to.
Cal followed him up the ramp
because he meant to hear the whole offer once, out loud,
so nobody could ever say he hadn't.
The concourse smelled like cold concrete and cut grass.
Their boots were the only noise on it.
Cal counted the painted section numbers as they passed
because counting kept his hands easy.

The bowl was empty.
Ninety thousand seats and not one person in them.
Wind came through the girders of the upper deck
and made a low sound up there that never settled.
Two sprinklers ran in a far corner of the field.
That was the crowd.

Rex Boyd stood at the fifty with his hands in his coat pockets.
He didn't wave Cal down. He waited,
and let the empty place be as big as it was.
Cal came down the steps and crossed the track.
The grass was mowed sharp for nobody at all.
He kept his own hands out of his pockets
because Boyd's were in his.

"Cal." Volume one, the same voice he'd use in a hallway.
In that bowl a hallway voice carried like a paging system.
"Thank you for coming. I know the week you're having."

"You built the week," Cal said.

"Some of it built itself. I won't argue the paper with you.
That's done, and you won it, and I can be gracious
about a thing that's done."

He turned and looked down the field toward the south end.
"So let me end the week well, for both of us.
The plaza goes in down there, in the new plans.
Every soul who comes to a game walks through it.
There's a wall at the gate,
and there's a place on that wall for bronze."

The wind moved the flags on the rim. Boyd's coat didn't stir.
Cal noticed that.
The man had scouted his spot out of the wind.

"Her name," Boyd said.
"I'd put it where ninety thousand people read it
every Saturday. Raised letters. Prominent.
My own crews would set it before one deck goes up.
And the winter fund doubles.
Same paper you already have. Same line at the bottom.
Friday."

For one breath Cal saw it.
Bronze holds light. It would hold it down there all season.
He never told anyone, then or later,
that for that one breath he wanted it,
wanted it worse than the shop, worse than being right.

Then the breath went out, and he saw the rest of it.
Boyd had found the one thing Cal wanted most
and put a price on it.

Cal looked at the south end a moment. Then at Boyd.

"No," he said.

He didn't build anything onto it.
He turned and started for the tunnel.

Boyd let him get nearly there.

"Triple," Boyd said, at his back, no louder than before,
and the bowl handed it down the field anyway.
"The fund at triple. Friday stands.
I don't move a date on a man twice; it's rude."

Cal walked into the tunnel.
The wind quit all at once in there,
and his ears kept the sound of it a while on their own.

---

Thursday morning the unnamed man was back at the yard fence,
forearms on the top rail,
giving the shop the surveyor's squint.
Cal saw him from bay three and went out the side door,
and on the way he pulled the roll of drawings
from behind the truck seat,
because the counter-report was real in every way but one,
and this was the man who had told him what the one was.

He spread the sheets on the tailgate
and set a socket on each corner.
The man came off the fence and looked.

He read for a long time. He didn't talk while he read.
Cal offered him coffee once.
He shook his head and turned a page.
He had a flat carpenter's pencil above one ear,
and he brought it down for the load pages,
checked one figure twice, tapped it, and went on.
Ratchet came out, smelled his boots,
and sat down against them, and he let her.

"Who drew these?" he said.

"We did," Cal said. "Nights. In the House."

"Who ran the numbers?"

"Same we. Same nights."

"Who measured the sill plates?"

"Me," Cal said. "Twice. Once because it mattered
and once because I didn't believe it."

The man turned the last sheet back and squared the stack.

"Real drawings," he said. "Real numbers."

"You told me at this fence a finding gets answered
by work of equal standing," Cal said.
"Work a stamp will stand behind.
We made the work. We don't have the stamp."

"You have it. Had it since the load pages held."

Cal put his hand flat on the stack,
mostly to feel it hold still.

"Whose man are you?" he said. "I never did learn."

"Mine," the man said. "Been mine my whole working life.
A stamp's like a ladder.
You don't climb another man's,
and nobody tells you where to set your own."
He gave Ratchet one scratch,
put the pencil back above his ear,
and stepped away from the tailgate.
"Finish the set. I'll stamp it where it got drawn."

He went up the yard,
two fingers off his cap at the gate, and was gone.

Cal rolled the drawings and slid them behind the seat.
Then he stood a minute with his hand on the truck door
before he went in, and told nobody anything yet.

---

Thursday night the Blind Mule had a sign on the door.
CLOSED FOR INVENTORY, in Peanut Kyzer's marker.
Under it, smaller: PRIVATE EVENT.
Under that, smallest: NO.

Cal had not been inside since the night the place
ran out of glasses. He came in last,
because the crew needed the room to be theirs
before it was his.

Peanut had the front lights off and the pool table lit.
Coveralls stood down one rail, most of the twelve,
Ronnie and Travis and DeShawn in from the shop,
Cal's second at the end with her jacket still zipped.
Clipboard people held the other rail, the House side,
Marisol among them with her arms folded.
She found Cal's eye once and gave him a small nod,
and he took it the same as coffee.
On a stand by the corner pocket sat a Checkerboard pie.
Nobody claimed bringing it. Everybody reached for it.
It was half gone before a word got said.

Peanut poured coffee down both rails,
it being a work night on both sides,
and left the pot where the whole argument could reach it.

Cal went to the bar board
and wiped the specials off with his sleeve.

"That chalk's for prices," Peanut said.

"These are prices," Cal said,
and both rails laughed, coveralls first.

He chalked Boyd's offer on the left, all of it, fair,
the fund at triple, a winter of deck work,
because if the math was going to win
it had to beat the whole number, not a kind version of it.
On the right he chalked the House.
Scaffolding. Shoring. The roof frame.
The same trades. The same paychecks, week for week,
contract through March.
Then he put the chalk down and stepped back.

Ronnie got to the board first.
"Left side's real today, Cal.
Right side's real if a vote goes right."

"The trustees come the first Tuesday of November,"
Marisol said. "With a stamped report in front of them."

"Stamped by who?" DeShawn said.

"His own man," Cal said. "Monday, at the House."

"My cousin framed decks for Boyd one winter," Travis said.
"Pays on the first, every first."

The pie went around again.

"I got a kitchen," Ronnie said. He wasn't loud about it.
"I got a kid with teeth coming in crooked.
Pending don't pay for teeth."

Cal's second unzipped her jacket then
and took out the pledge letter.
She had pulled it off the break-room corkboard
on her way out, tacks and all,
and she laid it on the rail
where both sides could read the word on it.

"It's said pending since the leaves were green,"
Travis said.

"That's the left column," Cal said.
He put his knuckle under Boyd's numbers
and the chalk dust showed white on it.
"Every figure over there has said pending for weeks.
A payday a man can move is a leash.
The right column is a contract. March is in writing."

"Scaffold work in January is cold," DeShawn said.

"Deck work in January is cold with Boyd watching,"
said Peanut, who was not on the payroll of anybody present.

"And if the vote goes wrong anyway?" DeShawn said.

"Then it's a hard winter," Cal said,
"and I'm the man who dealt it.
I'm not going to chalk you a column that says otherwise."

The room stayed quiet after that in a working way,
the crew doing arithmetic with their arms crossed.

Ronnie looked at the board a long time.
The room let him.
"Same trades," he said, mostly to himself.
"Same weeks. More weeks, even."
He picked the letter up off the rail, folded it twice,
and put it in his chest pocket.
Nobody called for a vote, and nobody needed to.

Marisol cut the last piece of pie
and set it in front of Cal.

"Eat," Marisol said.

He ate it, standing at the rail with chalk on his hand,
and she stayed at his shoulder
while the room came back up to noise around them.

---

Friday the bays were full by seven.
Ronnie had a gearbox open in three.
Travis and DeShawn had a dually on the lift,
arguing over a bearing that turned out to be a brake.
The phone rang about parts, and about a water pump.
Nobody in the shop said the word Friday out loud.
Travis watched the clock until lunch.
After lunch he quit watching it,
and the rest of the crew quit around the same time.

In the break room the corkboard had a clean pale rectangle
where the letter had hung, and two tack holes.
Cal stood in the doorway with his coffee and looked at it,
then went back to work,
because an ordinary Friday was the only answer
he meant to give.

Nobody came from Boyd's office. No cart, no call.
Marisol called at noon about Monday,
and they talked about Monday,
because Monday was the day with work in it.
The counter-drawings stayed behind the truck seat.
The drawer in the office stayed locked.

At five the carillon came across the roofs, same as any day.
The crew washed up. Ronnie left first.
Cal's second cut the compressor,
and the shop went down in stages, fans, lights, radio.
Ratchet made her last lap of the bays
and got up into the truck when Cal opened the door.

Boyd's paper had a line at the bottom for a signature.
Friday came and went and the line stayed empty.
Nothing got signed.
