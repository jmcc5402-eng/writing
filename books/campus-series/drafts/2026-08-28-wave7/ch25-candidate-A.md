# Chapter 25 — Receipts

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate A. Variance card: D5.

---

The motor pool was the only lit window on campus at one in the morning,
a single yellow square at the end of the service lane,
and Marisol drove toward it with her coat buttoned to the chin
over her nightshirt and the truck's vents still blowing cold.

Cal had left the side door off the latch.
Inside, the shop smelled of solvent and cold concrete,
one work lamp burning over the long bench,
the space heater giving everything it had
to about four square feet of floor.

The originals were already laid out.
Squared, in rows, both envelopes set above them like headings.
Every sheet carried the gray watermark corner to corner,
DRAFT — UNSIGNED, and every sheet carried its date,
and under the lamp the gray read nearly silver.

Ratchet came across the floor, toenails ticking the concrete,
put his chin on her boot, and left it there,
one warm weight in the whole building.

She had driven over with a single question.
She had rehearsed it at both stoplights and once in the lane,
and standing at the bench she could not get it past her teeth.

Cal watched her not ask it.

"Publish it," he said. "All of it. Me too."

"Cal." Her breath showed at the edge of the lamplight.

"You've seen what this county does with a maybe," he said.
"I'm done being one. I wrote those pages and I never signed them.
Put both facts up where everybody has to walk past them."

"They'll read it however they want to read it," she said.

"They will," Cal said.
"But they'll be reading the real one when they do."

He squared the top sheet with two taps against the bench
and held it out to her.

"Yours," he said.

She looked at his hands instead of his face,
because his hands were steady
and his face she could not have taken straight on just then.
A man who dated every page and locked every drawer
had handed her the one thing in the county that could ruin him,
and called it hers while he did it.
She knew exactly what it cost because she knew exactly what he was,
and it was the bravest thing she had ever watched a careful man do.

Here was what she would have denied in daylight,
at any table in town, under oath if it came to that.
For two and a half years she had told everyone who asked
that she needed nothing handed to her, and it was true,
and underneath the true part she had wanted exactly this,
to be handed something that cost the man handing it.
She had stopped expecting it years before she stopped wanting it.

"Say it once more," she said, getting the phone level.
"For the record."

"Publish it," Cal said, plain as a parts order. "All of it."

Her eyes went hot on the first photograph
and she blinked it down and kept shooting.
Every page, both sides of both envelopes,
dates plain, watermark plain, gray on gray,
her fingertips past feeling the glass by the end
and the frame held level anyway.

"Last chance," she said, thumb over the phone.

"Marisol," Cal said, and that was all he said.

When she finally stepped back,
Ratchet sighed like somebody losing a pillow,
and Cal put the originals away in their drawer
and walked her out through the breath hanging in the lane.

---

She came in under the Checkerboard's bell at six,
out of a dark that still had two hours left in it,
into front windows fogged gray from the griddle.
Earlene Tatum already had the back booth and two plates of eggs,
and pushed one across as Marisol sat.

"Liars' Table met early," Earlene said. "Full quorum.
You want the verdict?"

"I want the eggs first," Marisol said.

"Have both. Verdict's short."
Earlene waited until Marisol had a proper forkful down.
"You can win crooked. You just can't keep it."

"Unanimous?" Marisol said.

"Peanut abstained till he heard the part about keeping it,"
Earlene said. "Then it was unanimous."
Then she arranged her silverware, knife squared to the table edge,
and added, "We had astroturf before the internet, honey.
We called it the Chamber of Commerce."

Marisol finished the eggs and most of the toast,
hungrier than she had been in two days,
and put the verdict with the rest of her evidence.
It held better than half of what she'd read all night,
and it had come with breakfast.

---

By six thirty the booth had stopped being breakfast.
Aisha Cole took the bench opposite with a phone in each hand
and began dealing screenshots across the table.

"Fakes left, neighbors right," Aisha said.
"Fake, fake, real but always like that, fake, fake."

Birdie Pham came by on a circuit with the pot,
filling cups without being asked and without slowing down.

The bell went again and Kat came in with the cold on her,
hugged Marisol hard enough to move the table,
hung her bag on the booth hook,
a zipped folder standing in the top of it,
and took the fourth seat.
Kat, her realtor once and her friend
a long time before Ashford ever heard of either of them,
started in like no time had passed at all.

"So. Third date. He orders the margarita that comes in the fishbowl.
Then a second one. By the third
he is standing up on the patio
telling four strangers and a waiter that I—"

"Thirty-one," Aisha said, turning a phone around.
"Thirty-one accounts. All furious since yesterday.
All spelling it ya'll, apostrophe in the wrong seat.
Identical. Every single one."

"Thirty-one people," Kat said. "In this town.
All awake at one in the morning with the same bad apostrophe."

"They're not people," Aisha said. "That's the point. People nap."

"Am I ever finishing that story?" Kat said.

"You're on the docket," Aisha said.

"Pot four," Birdie said, pouring.

The tall stack Kat had ordered landed at her elbow
and went cold there over the next hour;
every time she loaded a fork, a phone went off first.

"Somebody's got Tunk Ferrell on their list," Aisha said.

"Tunk ushers," Marisol said. "Every Sunday, twenty years.
Pull the service times.
He can't be posting at nine on a Sunday, he's holding the plate."

"Pulling," Aisha said. And, a while later, "Cleared.
Our loudest neighbors post straight through church.
Tunk's never once."

"File name," Aisha said next, and slid the phone across.
"Look at the file name on the leak."

Marisol read it twice. "Kudzu Media."

"Gala program," Birdie said, pot in hand.
"Back page. Small print."

"I know," Marisol said. "I kept one."

At seven Aisha said, not looking up, "Four minutes.
They answer inside of four minutes. Every time.
I checked one a.m. It held at one a.m."

"That's a schedule," Marisol said, "not a neighborhood."

Whoever had built thirty-one cardboard neighbors
had planned for a mob and a panic,
and had not planned on one booth of women
who liked each other before eight in the morning.

---

Marisol Pruett was a self-employed bookkeeper.
She read strangers' invoices and timestamps for a living,
and when she finally looked at the whole leak straight on,
that was all it was. Invoices and timestamps.

The originals went up first, all of them,
shot clean at the shop with Cal's consent on the record:
gray watermark corner to corner, DRAFT — UNSIGNED,
every sheet dated.

Beside them, the leak came apart in the open.
It had been photographed off a screen,
a glare bar riding every frame.
On the wide shots the watermark washed down to a copier smudge.
The last frame was cropped so tight
that the gray fell outside it at both ends,
and Verna Poteat's line was all over town before the second pot:
you don't trim a thing that fits.
A crop that close got measured first, and measuring is intent.
Ronnie down in bay three had read margins like that
on a warranty fight once,
and his read was already circulating with the rest.

The leaked file had kept its own name,
and the name had Kudzu Media LLC inside it.
By noon the county had gone back through its junk drawers
and found the gala program, back page, eight-point type:
digital engagement, Kudzu Media LLC.
It landed not as a twist but as an arrival,
a bus the whole town had watched coming
finally reaching the stop.
Nobody proved where Kudzu's money went from there,
and nobody said a name over it.
The county could count its own dots.

The thirty-one furious neighbors went up
next to the Courier's bound volumes,
where the town had been spelling y'all correctly
since two mayors ago.
Thirty-one accounts, one identical typo, every time.
And Tunk Ferrell walked out of the whole mess in the same breath,
twenty years of Sunday-usher timestamps putting him in a pew
while the loudest accounts in the county
posted straight through every service in town.

None of it took a genius.
It took April, and Marisol was good in April.
The coffee went cold at her elbow twice before she noticed either cup.

In her camera roll, between the shop pages and the gala program,
sat an old video, hers, weeks old now,
Cal fixing a hinge in the middle of an argument.
She watched four seconds of it with the sound off,
then moved it where nothing from this morning could touch it.
It was hers, not evidence.

---

By four her moderator badge was back on Grapevine,
restored with no more ceremony than the coup had spent taking it.
The pledge jar gave back what the lie had raised,
deflating by supper to about the number she had figured that morning,
and she was glad to watch that money go.
Two names she would have called allies on Saturday
left the group loudly on the way out,
long posts, doors hit hard in print.
She let them go without a reply.

Cal's truck came up her drive at six
with its headlights already necessary,
the counter-drawings still riding behind the seat.
He stood in the kitchen doorway, shoulders filling most of it,
the cold still on his jacket,
and she took her whole time crossing the room to him.

"They spelled your name right this time," she said.

"First time all month," Cal said,
and hung the jacket where the stove's warmth could find it.

"Hungry?" she said.

"Since Saturday," Cal said.

Supper was on the stove and neither of them hurried it.
Two loud exits, one arrival,
and the day had ended up where she'd aimed it.

Ashford, given the week, retold the whole thing as a romance.
By Friday the version going around the checkout line
had the shop pages as love letters,
the crop as plain jealousy,
and the dance photo back where the town wanted it,
first real smile and all.
Marisol heard it through to the end
and paid for her groceries.
