# Chapter 25 — Receipts

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate C. Variance card: D2.

---

At one in the morning the motor pool was a single work light
and the smell of rubber, and Cal had already laid it all out.

Both envelopes sat open at the far end of the workbench.
The pages ran the length of it in two rows, squared to the edge,
every sheet dated, every sheet carrying the gray watermark
corner to corner. DRAFT — UNSIGNED. Sixteen times over.
He hadn't missed one.

Marisol stopped just inside the door.
Ratchet came across the concrete, toenails ticking,
put his chin on her boot, and left it there,
which settled the question of whether she was staying.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"You either."

They'd both had the same Monday.
The leak had hit the county at breakfast, cover pages
shot off somebody's screen, cropped so Cal's typed
signature block read as signed, and the dance photo
from Saturday ran beside it, recut as proof of a fix.
Her first real smile at him, captioned like evidence.

"The jar had its best day ever yesterday," she said.
"On the lie."

"Money doesn't know," Cal said.

"I know."

She came to the bench.
Across the shop the bottom drawer of his desk stood open,
key still hanging in the lock, empty for the first time
in a month.
Everything it had guarded all fall was lying under one bulb
where anybody could put a hand on it.
She set one finger on the nearest sheet and read the date
and the gray, and then read them both again.

She had driven over with a single question.
She'd rehearsed it at every stop sign between her kitchen
and campus, and it had gained weight at each one,
because there was no way to ask it that didn't ask him
to hand the county his own name.
Publish these pages and the signature died as a lie.
Publish these pages and the county also learned
whose locked drawer they had come out of,
and the county would chew on that for a year.
She stood there with the question in her mouth
and could not get it out.

Cal picked up the stack.
He squared it on the bench, tapped the edge twice,
and held it out to her with both hands.

"Publish it," he said. "All of it. Me too."

She took the pages.
They weighed more than sixteen sheets of paper
have any business weighing.
He had picked up the one thing in the county
that could ruin him, put it in her hands,
and called it hers.
Marisol was forty-three and had been handed plenty of things
by plenty of people, most of them problems.
Nobody had ever handed her the whole of himself before.
She admitted the rest of it right there, silently,
in the place she kept what never made the group:
she had quit expecting anybody would.

"Cal."

"It's yours," he said. "Been yours a while."

Her eyes went hot, once.
She blinked it down and got her phone out,
because crying on the evidence helps nobody.

"Say the words," she said. "So it's clean."

"You have my permission. Every page, both envelopes,
my name on the story. Take the pictures."

She shot them one at a time, flat under the work light,
dates plain, gray plain, the envelope corners in frame
where they mattered.
Cal held each sheet down at the edges while she worked,
brown to the wrist even in October,
one knuckle skinned from some hinge or other,
the steadiest hands she had ever watched do a brave thing.
Ratchet's chin stayed on her boot through all sixteen.
Nobody spoke for sixteen pages,
and it was the most anybody had said to her in years.

When it was done she put the pages in her bag,
flat between two stiff folders
so the corners wouldn't crush.

"Six o'clock," she said. "The Checkerboard.
Aisha's already building folders."

"I'll be at the shop," Cal said. "Where they can find me."

"Standing in the open."

"Beats a drawer."

The counter-drawings stayed behind the truck seat.
Not tonight. Tonight belonged to the paper.

---

By six the Checkerboard smelled like bacon and coffee
and mobilization.

The Liars' Table had already ruled.
Earlene Tatum brought the verdict to the booth with the eggs,
set the plate down in front of Marisol,
and delivered it standing up.

"You can win crooked," Earlene said.
"You just can't keep it."

"Is that the table's ruling or yours?" Marisol said.

"Same thing on a Tuesday. Eat your eggs.
Verdicts sit better on protein."

Then she slid in alongside, took up her own silverware,
and arranged it while she talked, knife squared
to the placemat edge, fork brought into line,
spoon given a quarter turn until it sat true.

"We had astroturf before the internet, honey," she said,
to the spoon. "We called it the Chamber of Commerce."

Aisha Cole was running two phones above one untouched
plate of toast, doctor-fast, thumbing screenshots
into folders before they cooled.
Birdie Pham came around with the pot on the half hour,
filling cups nobody had asked her to fill.

"Eat the toast, Dr. Cole," Birdie said, passing.

"In a minute," said Aisha, who had said it twice already.

And the fourth chair, empty since wine night was invented,
was full.
Kat, her realtor, her friend from clear back before Ashford,
had driven in before dawn with her reading glasses on
and a story already going.

"So the margarita boyfriend orders a third one at lunch,
at lunch on a Tuesday, and he looks me dead in the eye
and he says—"

"Batch," Aisha said, and turned a phone around.

Kat set the story down in midair and picked up
the screenshot instead of her fork.
Every wine night owed one interrupted story.
This one paid on time.
Her omelet cooled at her elbow all morning
and nobody, least of all Kat, did a thing about it.
Her bag sat on the bench against her hip, zipped,
and stayed that way.

Marisol spread the morning out across the table
and went to work, because this was, in fact, her work.
She was a self-employed bookkeeper.
She read strangers' invoices and timestamps for a living,
eight hours a day at her own kitchen table,
and a fake grassroots campaign is only somebody else's
books, kept badly.

The originals went up first.
Sixteen photographs off Cal's bench, gray corner to corner,
every date legible, his permission stated in the caption
in his own words.
Then she set the leak beside them and let it testify
against itself.
Photographed off a screen, a glare bar straight through
the middle.
On the wide frames the watermark washed down
to a copier smudge.
And the last frame cropped in tight, so tight the gray
fell outside the picture at both ends.
Half the county was already quoting Verna Poteat on that
one, and Verna sat three booths down like a civilian,
accepting tribute over biscuits:
you don't trim a thing that fits.

"You don't crop that close by accident," Aisha said.
"You measure first."

"You measure," Marisol said. "Measuring is intent."

"I've staged a lot of listings," Kat said,
tapping the tight-cropped frame.
"When a photo's cropped that hard,
there's a water stain just out of frame. Every time."

By seven, Ronnie down in bay three had his margin read
of the crop circulating, warranty math done
on a phone photo.

The next part was already sitting in the leaked file's
own name.
The images had ridden into the county inside a file
stamped Kudzu Media LLC, and by nine the county had
caught up to the back page of its own gala program,
where the same name had sat all fall in eight-point type.
Digital engagement, Kudzu Media LLC.
Kat still had her program in the junk drawer
like everybody else.
Aisha had a photo of the back page posted
inside two minutes.

"So who's paying Kudzu?" Kat asked.

"Not my line to draw," Marisol said.
"I post what's on paper.
The county can count its own dots."

Then the accounts.
Aisha had thirty-one of them foldered by eight o'clock,
the loudest of the concerned neighbors,
and every last one spelled y'all the same wrong way.
Ya'll, apostrophe inland, thirty-one for thirty-one,
while quoting the Courier's bound volumes back at a town
that had said all of it first, two mayors ago.

"Thirty-one accounts," Aisha said.
"Thirty-one identical ya'lls."

"Strangers don't share a typo," Marisol said.
"A keyboard does."

"Who does this all day?" Kat said.

"Somebody on a payroll," Marisol said. "Volunteers sleep."

The same folder cleared Tunk Ferrell in one breath.
The loudest of the neighbors had posted straight through
every Sunday service in town, a month running.

"Tunk's ushered twenty years of Sundays," Earlene said,
squaring her knife again.
"Never missed one that I know of.
Whoever that is, it ain't him."

"Somebody should tell Tunk he's famous," Kat said.

"He knows," Earlene said. "He's already insufferable."

Last came the clock, which was Marisol's favorite part,
because clocks don't editorialize.
She pulled the reply times on the sock accounts
and posted those too.

"Inside of four minutes," Aisha said. "Every reply
I timed. Even the three-in-the-morning ones."

"That's a schedule," Marisol said. "Not a neighborhood."

Scrolling for the last exhibit, her thumb passed
the old video from September, Cal fixing a hinge
with his shoulder set against the door,
arguing with her the whole time and fixing it anyway.
Her thumb hovered.
She scrolled past.
That one was hers.

The post went out at nine forty.

Birdie topped off the cups.
"Drink up. Pot's dry after this one."

Kat raised her cup anyway.
"The end of the margarita story, by the way,
is that I paid for the margaritas."

"Course you did, honey," Earlene said.

Marisol sat back and watched the county read.
The pledge jar had put up its best day in history
yesterday, riding the lie, and she had hated
every dollar of it.
This felt like the opposite of that.
Best morning of work she'd done in two years,
she judged, and not one dime of it billable.

---

Her moderator badge came back at ten of five,
no note, no apology, the little shield sitting
beside her name in Grapevine as if it had never
been pried off.
She looked at it for a while and left the group
a two-word pinned post. Evening, loves.

By supper the surge had deflated exactly as she'd
priced it, the lie-money draining back out of the week,
and she was glad to watch every bit of it go.
Two allies quit loudly, with paragraphs.
Marisol left a heart under each announcement
and went back to her plate.

"Drawer's locked again," Cal texted at six.
"Nothing in it."

"My table, Thursday," she wrote back. "Bring the dog."
Then she set the phone face down beside her plate
and finished her supper while it buzzed.

By Friday, Ashford had the whole week retold
as a romance.
The dance photo went around a third time, uncropped now,
her first real smile at him right there in it
for anybody to check.
She heard three versions of herself by Friday night,
every one of them softer than the original.
One had Cal writing the post himself.
One had her weeping at the motor pool.
Nobody in any version stayed up past one
photographing watermarks with cold hands.
She minded none of it.
The romance part was true.
