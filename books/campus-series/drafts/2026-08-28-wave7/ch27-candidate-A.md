# Chapter 27 — What It Costs

POV: Marisol, third person limited, past tense.
Candidate A. Variance card: D7.

---

Marisol ran her pencil down the pledge column a third time
and got the same total, which on any other Monday
would have been the good news.

The hall table had gone under hours ago.
The counter-report drawings, corner-weighted with a stapler
and two of Cal's sockets. The maintenance ledger,
open to twelve winters of men on that roof for nothing.
The pledge folder, fat and dog-eared and hers,
every ten-dollar promise in it made by somebody
who was angry at a lie.
Cal knelt on the hall floor over the scaffolding plan,
pencil behind his ear, marking loads by the work light,
and every few minutes he said a number
and she said it back to him.
That had been the talking since dark, and it had been enough.
*This is us, happy,* she thought.
Two people counting the same building
from opposite ends of a room.

He had poured her a mug an hour back without being asked,
set it at her elbow on his way past, black,
the good mug with the chip turned in toward the handle,
and gone back down to the floor.
A sandwich sat in the kitchen in its wax paper
where it had sat since seven.
Neither of them had made a move on it.

"Forty-one on the west run," he said.

"Forty-one. You gave me forty-one already."

"Wanted to hear it in your voice."

She read him the next three without being asked,
and he penciled them onto the plan,
and the work light threw one yellow circle
wide enough, just, for the both of them.

The tower tolled nine out over the dark campus,
and under the last stroke Cal's phone buzzed
against the floorboards.

He read it twice, no faster the second time.
Then he got up off his knees and held the phone out
across the drawings.

"Boyd," he said. "By way of the university's counsel,
so it's official twice."

She read it out loud, because a roomful of trustees
was going to hear it in the morning,
and she wanted to know what it sounded like
coming out of a person.

"'Mr. Boyd wishes the board to understand
that the gift is whole or it is not a gift.
A partial footprint fails the donor's intent,
and in that event the shortfall stands.'"

The hall was exactly as warm as it had been a minute ago.
She pulled her cardigan closed anyway,
set the phone face down on the ledger,
and kept her hand on it.

"Full footprint or nothing. Millrow and this House,
or the hole in their budget stays open,
and they get told tomorrow that half a gift is no gift."

"It was always the play," Cal said.

"It was always the play when it was tomorrow's problem.
It's nine o'clock. It's tonight's now."

She looked at the table with everything on it.
Twelve winters, three hundred small promises,
a stamp from an engineer with no reason to lie.
All of it true, all of it good,
and in the morning a man with a checkbook
was going to hold it up by the wrong corner
and let the county read it upside down.

---

"Say the worst version," he said. "Out loud.
We've been stepping around it all night
like a hole in the floor."

He had come to stand across the table from her,
which lately was a distance neither of them used.
The work light picked out the gray at his temples.
She noticed, and kept the table's width.

"The worst version is all true," she said.
"The leak was faked. We proved it was faked,
and the proof reads like the girlfriend's campaign.
The drive's best day was bought with outrage at a lie.
Every picture of the two of us on every phone
in this county is the caption under both.
Boyd doesn't have to tell one more lie tomorrow.
He just has to point."
She squared the pledge folder to the table edge,
a bookkeeper's habit, buying nothing and knowing it.
"Everything true about us is the weapon, Cal.
There's no arithmetic that fixes it."

"Then I want one thing said plain," he said,
"so it's been said. There is no piece of this
where I wonder about you. Not the drive,
not the report, not one minute since August."

"I know," she said. "Not one second, either direction.
That's not the fight we're in.
Nobody has to be wrong for the story to work.
We just have to be visible."

He took the pencil down from behind his ear
and laid it on the drawings, and she watched his hands
do it, still brown this far past summer,
and understood that he had already decided something
and was about to hand it to her.

"Then here's mine," he said. "I stand down. Tonight.
My name comes off the cover letter.
The report goes in tomorrow under the engineer's stamp,
which is the only name it ever needed,
and I am not in that room.
Nobody calls your fight bought
if the man they say bought it never shows."

"Cal."

"Price it first. You'd want it priced.
The crew turned Boyd's money down on my say-so.
Twelve winters in that ledger, most of them mine,
and the morning it comes to a vote,
their foreman finds somewhere else to stand.
That's the cost. I know its size. I'd pay it."

She had never once seen him take a step back
from anything that had his name on it.
He fixed what he stood near.
And here he was, the strongest man in the county,
volunteering to disappear from the one fight
that had been his before it was anybody's,
offering it level, priced out, like a number
he had checked twice before saying.
It was the finest thing anybody had ever offered her.
She made herself hold still under it.
She would have liked, just once,
for the brave thing and the wanted thing
to be the same thing. Not tonight.
So she matched him.

"Or I disappear," she said. "Same trade, other side.
My name comes off the pledge page tonight.
The group posts totals with no face on them.
I'm not at Millrow, I'm not on this porch,
I'm not within a mile of that room tomorrow.
If I'm not in the picture, nobody writes the caption."

"And the price," he said. "You'd want it priced."

She picked up the mug, both hands around it,
and did not drink. It had gone lukewarm an hour ago.
She held it for the shape of it.

"The price is the one part you don't get to argue
with me about," she said,
"because I have paid this exact bill before.
Two and a half years ago I had a house
that went loud with quiet.
Both kids launched the same week,
and you could stand in that kitchen at noon
and hear the refrigerator think.
I drove a year sealed up silent. Radio off, windows up.
I couldn't have told you why at the time. I can now.
And my first week alone I built a toolbox,
piece by piece, out of principle and out of spite,
so I would never again have to ask anybody
for anything with a handle on it."

"It was the wrong wrench," Cal said.

She laughed. It was small, and it was real,
and it hurt like cold water on a bad tooth,
and it was the only thing all night
that had made the hall feel like theirs.

"It was my wrong wrench," she said,
"and I'd fight you for it again tomorrow."
Then she leveled her chin at him.
"That first Saturday, under that tent,
before I knew your name, I swore something.
My Saturdays belong to me. Not to lawyers,
not to boxes, not to a house two states back
that is still half mine. There's a date circled
in June on my kitchen calendar,
and telling you it exists is the farthest
that sentence has ever gotten out of my mouth.
I rebuilt a whole life around Saturdays in this town.
So when I say I know what it costs
to lose a life you built, I'm not being brave.
I'm being accurate. I paid it once, to the pound.
If my face in that room tomorrow is the thing
that loses you your Saturdays, your shop, this House,
then I have done to you the one thing
I know all the way from the inside.
I will not be the quiet your life goes loud with, Cal.
I have lived in that sound."

"I know the sound too," he said, and that was all,
and she let it be all. He had paid once
for the right to say it that short.

His hand left the drawings and crossed half the table
toward hers and stopped at the seam in the planks
and went back.
Neither of them said anything about it.
The space where the touch didn't happen stayed there
between the ledger and the drawings,
and she looked at it and did not reach across it either.
That much they owed the morning.
Two careful people, both hands home.

"Nothing gets decided tonight," she said.
"Both offers stay on the table until morning.
Nobody gets to be noble before breakfast.
That's the deal."

"That's the deal." He looked at the drawings
a long moment. "Say the last of it.
The part we're both sitting on."

"Whichever one of us disappears," she said,
"Boyd still gets tomorrow.
He built it so the truth serves him either way.
That's tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight we just carry it."

Somewhere below the hill a truck downshifted
on the county road, ordinary as anything,
carrying somebody home late who had no idea.

She put her coat on at the porch door.
Neither of them crossed the hall to close the distance.
Tonight the distance was the honest thing,
and they were both too careful to lie with their hands.

"Lock up behind me," she said.

"I always do. Take the mug. There's coffee in it."

"It's yours," she said. "I'll come back for it."

---

The first work light went out while her boots
were still crossing the porch.

She stopped at the rail with the mug in her hand.
Down the hill, Delmar's pit was already smoking
for tomorrow's crowd, hickory on the night air,
the closest either of them had come to supper.
At the corner, the Checkerboard's sign buzzed
its stubborn OPEN at an empty street.
The town was keeping its lamps lit around them,
its whole ordinary Monday going on in the dark,
and she stood in her coat and let herself be glad of it
for exactly as long as she could afford.

She set the mug down on the rail.
Her thumb rested a moment on the curve of the handle,
where his hand had been.
Then she took it back
and went down the steps into the yard.

Behind her the second work light went out,
then the lamp in the hall,
one window at a time going dark
until the porch light was the last one burning,
and then it wasn't.

On the rail, the mug he had poured her sat full,
untouched, going cold in the night air.
