1. l.570–596, 630–640 "She said yes, Dan." The drop lands in his body —
   cold under his ribs, knees to water, the paper sticking to his palm —
   but it's all fear and blaming himself; the last line is "He had not
   asked her." This is the second time he hears about her job from his
   boss with the door shut (26: "I'd rather have had it from you"). She
   told the chair — and through him his boss — before she told him, two
   days after he stood up for her. He should be hurt AT her, for a few
   lines, then the want under the hurt. Now longing plus bad news: a 3–4.
   Conflict between them is what gets this to 7. [MORE]
2. l.630–633 "believing he had paid on Monday to be square with her" —
   "square" won't read, and it isn't the reason she gave him. Thursday:
   "I'm not going to be the thing this cost you. I'd rather be gone than
   be that." On Monday the trustees struck his extension. So she is
   leaving BECAUSE he paid. That's her conflict, wanting him against being
   his price, and it isn't on the page. Dan is the one person who heard
   her say it — he should remember it here, in her words, so the reader
   goes "oh no, of course she did." [WHO]
3. l.537–542 "He was not going to call her yet." Why not? Class closed,
   terms gone, she's cleared, he sits in his office from one to three.
   Readers will yell at him to pick up the phone. If the point is he
   waited too long, say it and let it hurt; if he has a reason, say it
   plain. And when did she send it — before the finding or after? After is
   her choosing to go. [POINT]
