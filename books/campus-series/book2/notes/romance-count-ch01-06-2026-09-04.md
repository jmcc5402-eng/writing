## The pattern

The romance does not thin by POV — Dan's chapters and Aisha's fail at the same rate — and it does not thin in the plot-heavy middles, which are where the beats actually cluster (the tent, the whiteboard, the porch). It thins at the *openings*. Five of six chapters open with the POV lead alone at work — the first-aid table, the walkthrough, the emergency plan, the whiteboard, the walk up Millrow — and in three of them the other lead does not enter the POV's head until the plot delivers them in person. The drafts treat the absent lead as someone who appears when they walk on, not as someone the POV carries; so every chapter's first third is a professional at a task, and the reader who bought this for the feeling has to wait 130 lines each time to get it. The one absence beat that works — "heard whose sentence it was as he said it," ch 4 L358 — shows the fix is kind 4 in every case: her line in his bag, his card under her board, his coolers off her north edge. Under the binge card, the momentum snap is specific: a reader who finishes ch 1 on "Professional interest... Nobody asked" and turns straight to ch 2 gets 130 lines of laminated script cards before the man thinks of her. Ch 3 is the worse case for pleasure: it is the book's best plot chapter and its emptiest romance chapter, and the ends of it — the bolts in her fist, the phone that stays quiet — are exactly where a reader wants one thought of him and does not get it. The thinness at the ch 1 and ch 6 first thirds is the same disease at sub-clinical level; they pass and should not be touched, but the top-up brief's drafter should know the floor is being cleared by a pie question and a letter refusal, not by anyone's eyes.
