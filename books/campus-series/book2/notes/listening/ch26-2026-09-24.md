# The Survey Stakes

## Before you read: where we are

Dan, Thursday the twenty-eighth of January. Three blind drafters.

**Targets.**

**The plot.**

It opens a step back, Monday, in the review's interview room. His head is on her the whole time: her face through the car glass, the parka on its hook. The reviewer asks if he'd have played Trey. He answers as the coach he used to be, and the half he means stays in his head.

Thursday morning the athletic director gives him two things. The partner comes in July either way, so why spend a season defending her line? And her new school out west has called him about her. He learns it from him, not her.

Whit tapes an ankle and says his girl is a sophomore here. At Odell's, a customer says the doctor was right. The survey stakes cross the ground by the annex. That evening on the Coach's Table, Tick offers him the air where he said "her protocol." He lets the sound bite stand.

At night he drives to Verna's and sees the loaded car. Each offers the other the way out. She says keep the season, she was leaving anyway. He says he'll take the cost. Neither accepts. In the truck he weighs it: the lawyer says don't, and speaking at the hearing ends the extension. The turn signal, away from home, is his answer.

**Dan.**

The struggle on the page, not in five lines: a dream job, his staff's jobs, and her. He knows what his silence costs her.

**Aisha.**

Seen only through him. Angry, packed, and still wanting him.

**The romance.**

The heat notch lands inside her door, shut on the gravel. They kiss, and it does not turn into staying.

**The town, the clock.**

Four days to the hearing, six to the findings. Odell's anchor counts something true that nobody has counted yet.

## The chapter

On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board

The plastic water bottle in Dan Merritt's hand cracked whenever his grip changed on it, and it changed every time the talk came near Aisha Cole. The independent review had called him in on Monday the twenty-fifth, the day before the radio had him, to say whether she had been right to keep Trey off the field for the playoff. The university's lawyer sat beside him to see that nothing else got answered.

The reviewer asked fair questions. His head kept going to her car window on Wednesday, at noon in the rain. Her eyes had come up to his mouth through four inches of glass and waited for him to say one true thing. He had told her film was at four. Then the glass had gone up.

The bottle cracked in his fist, and his palm was wet on the plastic.

This morning his own team parka on the hook in his hall had swung against his hand, and it went through his chest like cold water. He wanted her in that hall so badly it made him stupid.

He answered the next three questions with half his head. The other half was on her hand over his in the cab the night of the ice.

"Coach." The reviewer's voice brought him back. "Knowing what you know now, would you have played your quarterback on the nineteenth?"

It was the question every coach got asked about every hurt kid in America.

"Every coach in the country plays him," Dan said. "I'd have played him."

The rest of it was right behind that, whole. A coach always wanted to play the boy, him first of all, and that was the reason the call had never been a coach's to make. It was the doctor's. It was hers. He took a breath to say it, and that last word put him back at her window in the rain. The reviewer read the line back once, word for word, and turned a page, and the breath went out of him with nothing in it.

In the hall the cold found the sweat on his spine.

"Glad that's over," the lawyer said. "The review sits Monday the first. That's the hearing. You're not on its list, and you don't go near it. You don't call her. Anything you add makes it worse for her."

"I know it."

He did know it. Something else was wrong, too. It was the feeling he got on a sideline when a play went wrong in front of him and he could not see yet which of his own men had missed the block.

On Thursday the twenty-eighth, three days after the interview, the athletic director called Dan up to his desk at eight in the morning, and by then Dan knew what had gone wrong on Monday. His line had not stayed in the room. He had heard his own answer on 1340 on Tuesday at half past seven, in the truck in the Fieldhouse lot, read out flat and stopped where he had stopped. His stomach went cold, and his hands locked on the wheel. He had his thumb over her name before the news was over, and the lawyer's *don't call her* kept it there.

"The partner comes in July, Dan," the athletic director said. "Whatever the review finds, it comes in July. I'm not spending a season defending her post when it's the partner's by July, and I'd like it if you didn't either."

It was a real argument. The athletic director had a review on his desk and eleven families behind it, a class that would close on Wednesday the third, and trustees who would vote in February on whatever he chose to hand them. He wanted a quiet February. The words were the athletic director's. The reason under them was not, and Dan knew whose it was.

"One more thing," the athletic director said. "A department out west called me Tuesday about Dr. Cole. A reference call. They've offered her a directorship, and they want her answer by Saturday the sixth. They didn't say if she's answered. It's a better job than this one. I'd take it."

Something went out from under Dan, like a stair he had miscounted in the dark. His heart was going as if he had run the stadium steps. She had not told him. He was hearing it from his boss with the door shut.

"She'll decide it herself," Dan said. "She always has."

His own job was the only one he had ever wanted. His staff had come to this town on his contract, and if his extension died in February, every one of them was out looking for work by spring without having done a thing to earn it. Against all of that was Aisha, with a job out west and nine days to answer it, and a whole county sure she had been wrong because it had heard only half his answer. It pulled both ways in his chest and would not stop. He would keep quiet through the hearing's week for his staff's sake, and he hated it.

At ten the kicker who had gone down on the ice was in Dan's office door, bouncing on the bad ankle to show it, asking if he could kick today. Dan walked him over himself. He told himself it was because the kid would never have gone on his own, and that was true, and it was not the reason that had him out of his chair so fast. It was her building.

In the rehab room the trainer had the kicker on the near table with the tape going. The cover man was next door in the whirlpool room.

"Is it going to hold, Whit?" the kicker said.

The trainer was Carl Whitlow, and Dan had called him Whit all season without asking him one thing about himself.

"Doctor said ten days on this," Whit said. "Today's ten."

He kept his eyes on the tape while he said it, and his ears colored up. It would be the man next door who signed the kid off today, not her. Whit had kept her count anyway.

"He'll baby it on the plant," Whit said to Dan. "Have somebody watch his hips, not the ball."

Dan had not seen it, and it was right.

"Can I still make my eleven o'clock?" the kicker said.

"You'll make it," Whit said. "My girl's a sophomore here, and she's never been late to a class. I'd hear about it if I made you late."

"I didn't know you had a girl here," Dan said.

"Sophomore." For a second Whit's whole face was a father's. "She's why I'm still in this building."

Whit wanted his job, and he wanted his girl through this school, and on a trainer's pay the second one rode on the first. Dan looked at him and saw a man for the first time.

On his way out he passed Aisha's office. The clinic's boxes stood along the wall with their numbers out, all but one at the end, turned to the wall. The number on the side he could not see was in his own marker. He looked at that box a second too long and went out the warm-end door onto her step.

Wooden stakes with orange tops had been driven into the frozen dirt between the annex and the fence. They ran from the near corner, the whirlpool room, out toward the fence. The flags last week had marked the ground, and the stakes marked where the building went. If the trustees passed it in February, the building would stand on top of her clinic, and the partner the money brought with it would be doing her job by July, whatever the review found on the third. He stood on her step until the cold came up through his shoes.

After four hours in his office with film he did not see, Dan drove down to Fountain's on Fountain Row at three for a haircut before the signing pictures on the third, a small thing, and the one thing that week he knew how to get right. Odell was a deacon at Mt. Nebo on Sundays and a barber the rest of the week, and what he wanted out of any afternoon was a straight line on the back of a neck and a man who held still for it.

A man in the waiting chair wanted the coach to pick a side.

"That doctor was right about 7," the man said. "I'll say it if nobody else in here will."

Dan said nothing. Under Odell's cape his hands had shut on each other, and he could feel his heart in the heel of each palm. His neck burned under Odell's fingers, and Odell felt it there, and the fingers did not stop.

"Forty days since that boy sat, Coach," Odell said. "He's on a bike, and he knows his own name. I counted it this morning over my coffee. Somebody ought to say that number on the radio."

Dan kept his eyes on the mirror.

He sat in his office after Odell's till half past six. Then he left the truck on the square and walked to Delmar's, the barbecue place at the foot of Millrow, and by seven he was in the guest chair on the screen porch with the heaters going. Ray, who ran the pit, had the smoker lit. Tick Moran hosted the Coach's Table from this porch, an hour on 1340, and he had called this one for signing week. The lawyer had cleared Dan to sit it for football and nothing else.

At twenty to eight Tick kept his hand on the fader and looked at Dan across the card table.

"Coach, the county heard a line of yours on this station Tuesday morning," Tick said. "Air's yours, if you want to put anything beside it."

Tick wanted a fair show more than a loud one, and Dan took the offer for what it was, the one kindness a radio man had. Behind the serving window, Ray stopped what he was doing.

On the fourteenth, with the ice on the hill, Dan had said her protocol on this same air, and the whole county had heard the doctor in it without her name. Tonight it would hear the gap where the rest of his answer belonged. He believed the lawyer. He also knew it was the easy thing to believe tonight. He had Odell's forty days in his head, a true number and not even his, and his breath came short over the sound of the heaters.

"I'm here to talk about the class, Tick," Dan said.

Tick took his hand off the fader. "Then we'll talk about the class," Tick said, and took line two.

He wanted to say her name into that microphone until his hands hurt with it, and he sat there with both hands flat on the card table and did not say a word of it. The county heard him talk about the class until eight, and it heard what he left out.

"Brisket's done, Coach, if you're eating," Ray said through the window when the bulb went off.

At twenty past eight he drove out of the square toward the Magnolia Court, Verna's motel, instead of up the hill. He put the truck in Verna's gravel beside the RAV4 and cut the engine. A truck in that gravel at night had been a post once already. He left it there. Verna Poteat looked out at it once from her office window, and her look said her tenant did not need it.

The RAV4 sat in front of nine. The light from Verna's office fell on the boxes on the back seat and the navy parka folded flat on the top one. Nobody loaded a car like that for a hearing. Aisha was going. Nobody had told him so, but he believed it all the way down.

Nine's curtain was open on a dark room, and the lamp was off. The door opened before he got to it, because Aisha had heard the truck. She stood in it in a sweater with her arms folded and her curls down. Her face in Verna's light was set hard. Her eyes were not.

"Doc," he said.

"Not in the gravel," Aisha said, and stood back from the door.

He went past her into nine, and she shut the door on the gravel and the light and the county. If anybody caught a coach and the team physician together before signing day on the third, both of them were gone that day, and he knew it with Verna's whole lot on the other side of an inch of door. He had come in anyway.

She pulled the curtain shut and did not touch the lamp. Lamp on and curtain shut had meant come, once, for him and nobody else. Tonight the curtain was for the county, and she left the lamp off so it would mean nothing. Verna's light came thin through the curtain, enough to see her face by.

His hand went toward the lamp out of habit. He took it back and did not know what to do with it, so he put it in his coat. The corner of her mouth moved and did not get as far as a smile.

"At the interview," he said. "I had more to say, and I didn't get—"

"Don't." Her chin came up. "Don't bring the rest of it to me, Dan. I didn't write the first half down. The review did. If there's a second half, the review's the one that needs it. Not me."

She was not letting him spend it on her. He saw that, and it went into his chest worse than the radio had.

"The athletic director had a call about you Tuesday," he said. "From out west."

Her mouth pressed flat, and she looked at her folded arms before she looked at him again. Her throat moved once. "So you heard it from him."

"I'd rather have had it from you," Dan said.

"I know." She looked at the curtain, and back at him. "I didn't call you, and you didn't call me. Here's what you do. Say nothing Monday. You've had the practice. Sign your extension in February. I'm on the interstate the morning of the sixth, and nobody in this county has to pay for anything."

Her voice held steady, and her hands did not. They were fists in the sleeves of the sweater.

Ten years ago the same deal had been settled for him, before anybody said it to his face. He got a season, and a woman on the academic side got a different life.

"No," Dan said. "Whatever it costs, I'll pay it. Not you."

"It costs you the job you've wanted your whole life," she said. "It costs every man on your staff. You'd be paying all that for a woman with her car already packed."

"Then that's who I'm paying it for."

"No." It came out low, and her eyes did not leave his.

Then her eyes went to his haircut.

"You got a haircut," she said.

"For the signing pictures."

"You're throwing your job away for me, and you got a haircut first."

She laughed, one short sound behind her wrist. Then he was laughing too, at the worst time in the world, and for a second they were only glad of each other. It passed.

Each of them stood there holding a way out for the other, and neither one would reach for it.

She had gone back to the door from the curtain. Her back was to it, and he was a foot from her in the dark. He could feel the cold coming off the door and the warm coming off her. Her eyes were wet, and she was angry, and she wanted him. He could see all three. Then her hand came out of the sleeve and took hold of the front of his coat.

She did not pull. She held on, and her eyes stayed on his. Her knuckles sat hard on his breastbone, right over his heart, and he felt each one of them through the coat.

"Aisha," he said. It was a question.

"I know exactly what I'm doing," she said. "Come here."

He came the last step on his own. He put one hand flat on the door beside her head, and the wood was cold under his palm. Her breath came up warm against his chin. For a second neither of them moved, and he could hear the furnace working in the wall. Then he bent his head and kissed her.

Her mouth was warm, and it was angry, and it went through him to the knees. Her back was against the door, and her fist was still in his coat between them, and he did not let himself come closer than that fist. His heart went so hard under the coat that he knew she could feel it against her knuckles. Heat went up his throat into his face. His other hand found her waist over the sweater and held on, and then the hand on the door came down and found her other side. His breath went ragged against her mouth, and he could not get it back. He wanted her more than he had ever wanted anything, and he was the reason her car was loaded in the gravel outside, and both of those were in his two hands at once.

She was the one who stopped. She turned her mouth off his and put her forehead against his chest, and for a while she only breathed there.

"Go home, Dan," she said.

Her fist had not let go of his coat. She was telling him to go and holding on to him at the same time, and he could feel her knuckles shaking against him. He kept his hands still at her waist and left it to her. His heart would not come down, and his breath went into her curls.

Then her fingers opened. Her palm came flat against his chest and pushed, not hard, and he stepped back at once. Her eyes were wet.

"I'm not going to be the thing this cost you," she said. "I'd rather be gone than be that."

"I know you would," he said.

He wanted to ask her not to go, and the words were right behind his teeth. She had just told him she would rather be gone than be his price, and the county's last word on her was still his. A man who had done that had no business asking her for anything. His throat had shut on anything else he might have said.

She opened the door behind her without turning around, and the cold came in off the gravel. He went out past her. She did not shut it until he was in the truck.

Dan backed out of Verna's gravel and stopped at the edge of the road with the engine running.

The lawyer had said don't. If he walked into the hearing on Monday the first and finished his answer, the athletic director would never carry his extension to the trustees, not for a coach who went into the review against the university's own lawyer. In one hand he held the job, and his staff's work with it, down to his receivers coach Ty Beaumont's truck payment. In the other was a woman with her car packed. He could decide she weighed more. He could not make it free, and it hurt low in his chest where the job had always sat.

More than the price, he was afraid she would be on the interstate on the morning of the sixth believing the last thing he ever said about her was the thing a stranger read on the radio on Tuesday morning.

The cold of her door was still in his palm, and his coat still had the shape of her fist in it. She had told him to go home.

He put the turn signal on, away from home. It ticked in the dark cab, and he did not turn it off.
