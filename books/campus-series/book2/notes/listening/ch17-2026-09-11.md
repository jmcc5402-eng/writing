# Chapter 17 — The Gray Season

## Before you read: where we are

Aisha, December 20 to 26. One drafter.

**The plot.**

The town emptied the morning after the game. Christmas is loud for the married and long for everybody else, and Aisha stayed with the clinic's line forwarded to her cell. The Gaults drive to Birmingham to prove her wrong, and she sends that office her whole chart the same afternoon, unasked. On the last night of the week the motel is empty and the two of them have a whole night for the first time. The second heat scene: it runs up to the first piece of clothing on the floor, slowly, and cuts there.

**Aisha.**

She gets the thing she stopped expecting, in the dark, on terms, and the firewall holds without either of them touching it. Early in the week, before any of that, she gives thirty-four her stool at the Checkerboard till January, breakfasts paid, and nobody thanks her.

**Dan.**

Seen from her side. "Review" is the county's word for his contract now. He walks down the hill to a lit room because she left it lit, knowing that if anyone sees, it is the same story as ten years ago.

**The romance.**

The terms are five days old. The signal has no poles, so it is her light this time. The night gets the room it needs: the walk to each other, the looks, the hands, the pauses, the things two serious people say wrong. Nothing about the call. Ends up.

**Boyd, the town, the clock.**

Boyd's crews chalk the Millrow curbs in the empty town. Cal and Marisol at the Checkerboard counter, warm. Verna sees the light and says nothing, ever. Six weeks to the first Wednesday in February.

**Your calls.**

Christmas Day alone, one paragraph. Or the two of them together on the day.

## The chapter

On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board

PomPoms&Prayers: Album's gone quiet and I'm taking that as good news. Boys home, phones down, nobody in a parking lot. Merry Christmas, everybody, from two states over.

The RAV4 took a parking space on the square with nothing on either side of it, nose in, on the first try, and Aisha Cole sat with the engine off and looked down the row at the rest of the empty ones. The out-of-state plates had left on Sunday, the morning after the game, the RV city off the fairgrounds by noon and the hotel block empty by dark, and the students had gone the week before with the semester, so the town was down to the size of a parking space, and she was in it. It was the twenty-first. The clinic in the annex was closed for the break and its line forwarded to her cell, and she was the only doctor that line reached. The class closed on the first Wednesday in February, on the other side of the new year, and that was when the terms ended; she was counting it in weeks now and not saying the number. Between now and then a doctor in Birmingham was going to read her chart and say whether she had been right about Trey Gault, and a table in February was going to say a word about Dan Merritt's contract, and the county already had the word. Review. He had said it himself in her corridor the week of the boxes, what the first round would cost him with Trey sitting, and it had cost him that.

She went in and ate her eggs at the second stool with the parka on, and the Liars' Table at the window was Tunk Ferrell and Earlene Tatum and the paper, and nobody had a joke for her and nobody looked past her. It was a Monday in a town that had gone home.

She drove up Millrow after with the sun not doing much. Two of Boyd's people were on the curb below Delmar's with a measuring wheel and a chalk line, snapping blue marks on the concrete in the cold, and one of them stepped back for the RAV4 without looking up. At the top the lot between the annex and the Fieldhouse was empty, the poles over the practice field off for the break, and his building was dark across the lot at nine in the morning, every window of it, and she looked at it longer than she needed to and went in.

The first call on the holiday line had come Sunday night at ten, a father in another time zone asking whether a boy could fly home in a walking boot. He could. She had typed it. That was the clinic over the break: her cell, her name, her building, and nobody in it.

Thirty-four came down the corridor at ten past ten with two fingers of his left hand taped together, by himself, and wrong. He held the hand up to her at the door of her office and did not say a word, and she did not ask what he had jammed it on or why he was still in a town everybody else had left. She sat him on the treatment table in the whirlpool room, cut his tape off, felt the joint, moved it, watched his face, and buddy-taped the finger to the next one so it would hold. "Two weeks. Leave it taped. Don't test it on anything." He nodded. She walked him down the corridor to the warm-end door and pointed down the hill toward the square.

"The Checkerboard. Second stool from the register, next to Coach's. Starting tomorrow that stool's yours till the semester starts." He looked at her. "Breakfast's paid. You don't have to say anything to anybody. Sit on it and eat."

He nodded again, once, and went down the hill with his hand held against his coat.

At noon the same space on the square was still empty, and she took it again. The one cook had the griddle and the pass-through and the register, and she stood at the register with her card out.

"The second stool," she said. "There's going to be a kid on it in the mornings from tomorrow till the semester starts. Whatever he eats, I'm paying for now."

"That's the stool you sit on."

"Till January it's his."

The cook looked at the stool, and at her card, and took the card.

"How many mornings."

"Count them and charge me. He won't say much."

"He got a name?"

"He's got a number. Thirty-four. He won't give you either one."

"Doesn't have to. It's eggs."

He ran the card and gave it back and went to the griddle, and she went out to the square with her one seat gone and did not look at the stool on her way past it.

The Checkerboard on Tuesday morning had one cook on the griddle and Dan Merritt on the stool nearest the register, and Aisha came in out of the cold with her hands in the parka's pockets and stopped inside the door to see where she was going to sit.

The second stool was empty. There was a plate in front of it on the counter, wiped clean with toast, with the fork laid across it and a glass of milk half gone, and the kid was not there and had been. Dan had his coffee and the Coach's Slice in front of him and his phone face down beside the cup, and he looked up when the door went, his shoulders coming around on the stool before his face did, and his eyes stayed on her a second past what a counter allowed.

"Doc," he said.

"Coach."

She went the length of the counter past him and past the plate, close enough behind his stool that she could have touched his shoulder going by, and he sat still for it, and she went by, and took the last stool at the far end under the pass-through, under the sign with his order on it, with the whole counter between them and the town in the middle. The town in the middle was Cal Sutter and Marisol Pruett on two stools with their coats over the backs, Marisol with her phone face down between their plates, and Cal with both forearms on the counter and his cup in both hands, the two of them easy, the two of them settled, in a diner on a Tuesday with nothing in the world to prove to it. Earlene came up the counter with the pot, filled Aisha's cup without a word, looked at the plate on the second stool and at Aisha, and said nothing about either.

"Cook's out of biscuits till the truck comes Monday," Earlene said, to the room, and went back down to the Table.

Marisol turned her phone over and read it and turned it back.

"The board's running a meal train for the Gaults," she said. "Somebody pinned a sign-up at the top Sunday night. Forty slots through New Year's, and thirty-one of them are taken by people who've never been in that kitchen and are going to have it delivered from two states off."

"Which ones are open," Cal said.

"The twenty-fourth's got somebody in Atlanta who's never met a Gault. The twenty-fifth is open. Nobody wants to cook Christmas twice."

"Put us down for it."

"It's Christmas, Cal."

"Christmas is loud for the married and long for everybody else. We can spare a night. Eat."

The last word was for her eggs, going cold under the phone, and Marisol picked up her fork. Then her eyes went down the counter to the far end, to Aisha, and back up it to the register, to Dan, and she did not say a thing with her face, and she had known since a booth in December which of the two of them Aisha had named in a bar. Cal drank his coffee.

Aisha ate. Dan Merritt was at the other end of a counter with his phone face down, and she was at this end with hers in her pocket, and between them a married couple was arguing about a sign-up link, and that was the closest the two of them were allowed to that before February. She looked down the counter once. He was not looking at her. He was looking at his pie, and his mouth was working at the corner, at something Cal had said or at nothing, and she knew that corner now, and looked at her plate. Nobody in the Checkerboard could have said a word about it. Marisol could have, and would not.

Cal paid at the register beside Dan, and the two of them came down the counter on their way out, and Marisol stopped at the far end with her coat half on.

"Friday, if you want a table with people at it, you've got my number."

"I've got the clinic line."

"Bring it."

Cal held the door, and they went out into the square together, and the bell went once for the two of them.

When she left she went back up the counter past the plate and past his stool, and he did not turn, and the cold on the square was the same cold as yesterday.

The release came to the annex at two on Tuesday afternoon, on paper, in Missy Gault's hand, at the warm-end door. One page from a physician's office in Birmingham, a records release with Trey's signature at the bottom of it, his own, because he was an adult and it was his chart. Missy did not come in.

"They want it before Wednesday," she said.

"They'll have it by three."

Missy looked at her, and nodded, and went back to her car, and did not say thank you.

She sent the whole chart at ten to three, both scans, the tape times, every step of the ladder in her typing, Missy's own pages, the paper with her whole name on it, to the Birmingham office with one line on top and no question in the line about what any of it was for.

*Complete record for Trey Gault attached: imaging, both studies; exertion times; the family's daily log. Call if anything is missing. A. Cole, MD.*

She knew what it was for. The Gaults were driving to Birmingham on Wednesday to have another doctor read her chart and say she had been wrong, and she had just sent that doctor everything she had, because a chart was made to be read, and hers said what it said in any city, and a doctor who held a page back from another doctor had a reason to, and she did not have one. If Birmingham said the other thing, a table in February would have one more page for the word it already had about Dan. She locked the annex at four. Across the lot the Fieldhouse had its film-room window lit, and he was in it, a big man alone in a building with the heat running for nobody, and she stood by the RAV4 with her keys out and looked at that window longer than the cold made sense, and then went down the hill.

Christmas Day was Friday, and Aisha ran it first. She was on the square at five with the streetlights on and nobody under them, and the Checkerboard's window lit and the rest of the square dark, and she ran the loop of it twice and had it to herself. She read the board at six in room nine with her boots on, and the board was parents in other states with their sons home, and it had nothing in it since Christmas Eve. The holiday line rang once, at ten, a father wanting to know whether his son could lift over the break with the shoulder, and she told him what the shoulder could do and typed it, and that was the work of the day. Verna's office light was on across the lot all day under the word VACANCY, lit again now that the town had gone, and Aisha did not go over, and Verna did not come. She had never been married. She had stopped expecting it years before she stopped wanting it, and a Christmas in a motel room with two suitcases in it was the shape that came with that, and she sat on the end of the bed in her running clothes and let herself have all of it for about a minute, and it was sad, and she did not call it anything else. Then she did what she did with every patient she had ever charted, and wrote down the rest of it. Dan Merritt was in a house she had never seen the inside of, on a Christmas with no film in it, and he wanted her, and had said so with his hands in her office, and she wanted him, and had said so to two women in a bar, and neither of those had been true on any Christmas she could count. That was the good thing. She named it, out loud, to the room: she had somebody to want, and he wanted her too. Then she got up and put her shoes back on and ran the square again in the daylight, the pink word on, and waved at Verna's window going by, and the day was glad after that, all the way to dark, and she let it be.

Room nine had the lamp on and the curtain open at nine o'clock on Saturday night, and Aisha stood at the window in her socks with a can of seltzer in her hand, waiting to see whether Dan Merritt would walk down a hill to a light.

The motel was empty. The VACANCY word was lit over the office, every letter of it, and it came across the gravel and through her window and lay on the lid of the far suitcase, and under it Verna's office light was on, like every night. The light was hers this time, and it meant one thing, that if room nine's window was lit he could walk down to it.

He came at twenty past nine, on foot from the Fieldhouse, where the truck was, twenty-five minutes across the square in the cold and up Verna's gravel with his hands in his jacket, so there was no F-150 in the lot for anybody to count. Verna counted everything, and whatever she counted tonight was going to stay in that office. Aisha had the door open before he knocked.

He came in and she shut the door, and he took the sideline jacket off inside it and looked for where it went, and she took it out of his hands and hung it on the closet rod next to the parka and the wool city coat, the one with a letter still buttoned into its inside pocket, and nobody touched that coat, and the door was shut, and it was the two of them and the lamp.

He had not shaved. There had been no cameras on Saturday to shave for, and it was a week of it now, dark, the most she had ever seen on him, and it made him look like a man on a day off, and she had never seen him be that. With the jacket on the rod he had nowhere for his hands. He put them in his back pockets and took them out again and let them hang, big and empty at his sides, and she liked him for it more than was reasonable. She had one chair. She did not know which of them it was for, and she stood with her hand on the suitcase rack and then took it off the rack, because a rack was not a thing a person stood with a hand on.

"Merry Christmas," he said. "I'm a day late with it."

"You're the first one to say it to me in a room."

"Nice room," he said. There was nothing in it to say that about, two suitcases and a lamp and a bed, and they both heard it land. He shut his eyes and opened them. "That came out wrong. I had a better one on the walk down."

"Say the better one."

"I've lost it. It was about the light."

"Then say that one."

He looked at the lamp, and at the window with the curtain open, and back at her, and did not hurry it.

"I came out of the Fieldhouse and looked down the hill, and the motel was dark end to end but that one, and I knew whose it was, and I've never been that glad to see a window in my life. That's the better one. I found it."

She smiled at him, with her mouth shut and then not, and it was a small thing to be that pleased by. She was that pleased. "Sit anywhere. There's the chair and there's the bed and I'm not going to say which."

He sat on the end of the bed, on Verna's blanket, with his knees up because the bed was low, and she took the chair, and there was the width of the room between them, which in room nine was not much and was all there was.

"What did you do with it," she said. "Christmas."

"I was at the house. It was quiet. I read the board twice and it was nothing but people's kids being home. I stood in the kitchen at seven with the truck keys in my hand and I put them down, because you hadn't said, and I wasn't going to guess at a light that wasn't on."

"I ran at five and had the square to myself. The line rang once. I took it. That was the day." She turned the can in her hand. "And I sat here and thought about a man in a house I'd never seen the inside of, and that was the best of the day, and I said so out loud. To the room."

He looked at the blanket he was sitting on, and at her, and his face was glad in the open, with nothing on it to manage, and she had never had that from him across a sheet.

"What did the room say."

"It's a motel room, Dan. It's heard worse."

"What else has it heard."

"A furnace that runs behind when the wind comes north. Verna wrote me a note about it in November on the back of a receipt, and I've still got the receipt."

He laughed at that, the real one, the one that carried, and the furnace ran behind under the north wind like the note said, and neither of them was cold.

"What's the Fieldhouse like with nobody in it," she said.

"Loud. Every door's open and the heat runs and there's nobody to close a door on. I've been the only man in the building since Monday, and I've talked to the ice machine twice."

She laughed, the one she had, all of it at the same time, too loud for a motel room at nine at night, and put the can up to her mouth after it.

"And you're telling me it talked back."

"Second time. Same thing your room said."

She looked at him over the can, a man who had walked twenty-five minutes in the cold to make a joke about an ice machine on the end of her bed.

"I'll say the thing I walked down here with," he said. "I want you, Aisha. I've wanted you since a bad room on a third floor where you laughed at me about the floor, and I've said every other thing to you first, about sheets and boards and February, and I'm done saying the other things."

She set the can down on the windowsill.

"I want you," she said. "I said it in a bar to two women in December so it would go nowhere. In here it goes somewhere. That's the difference between a bar and this room, and it's the only one."

"Missy came to the—" She stopped on it. It had come up out of the week on its own, and it was not a thing for this room, and she shut her mouth on it and he watched her do it.

"That one stays up the hill with the truck," he said.

"It does."

She looked at the width of the room, like a lot she was about to cross, and then she stood up. He stood up too, because he was a man who stood when a woman did, and then he did nothing else, and she saw him decide that, saw it go through him and settle: he was going to let her cross it. She crossed it, and stopped where she could see all of it, the collar of the quarter-zip with no coat over it, his hands at his sides where he had put them, and his eyes. His eyes were dark and they held on her longer than was comfortable, longer than a man held a look across a sheet at five fifteen, and he did not soften it, and she did not want him to. He did not have a coat on. She did not have a coat on. Every time she had been this close to him there had been nylon between them, or wool, or a sheet of paper and a trainer in earshot, and there was nothing between them now but a room that was warm for once, and she stood there and let that be the thing for a moment, the plain fact of no coat, and felt her pulse where her hand was going to go. Then she lifted her hand. It did not go to his face. She had done that in her office and knew what that felt like. It went past the jaw to the back of his neck, and she put her palm flat there and left it.

The neck of a man who had worn pads once. It was wider than her hand and it was warm all the way through, no collar between her palm and the heat of it, and the heat came straight up into her hand with nothing in the way, none of what had always been between them, and she had not known until then that that was the thing she had wanted, one hand on the back of his neck in a warm room. His head came down to her. He did that slowly too, and she watched him come all the way, and then his mouth was on hers, and it was not the office, with a lot to cross after it and a building to go back to, it was a man with the night in the room and nowhere he had to walk back to, and he kissed her like he had that much time. Her other hand went up his back over the quarter-zip and she could feel him through one layer of cotton, the width of him, the heat of him, his shoulders moving under her hand when his arms came around her, and she had had those shoulders across a field and a rail and a counter and had never in her life had them under her hands, and had them now. He walked her back a step, and another, until the suitcase rack was at her hip and the lamp was behind him, and she got both hands under the hem of the quarter-zip to the shirt under it and the heat under that, and he made a sound she had never heard him make, low, and said her name into her hair.

She thought, with her hands on him, in plain words, because that was how she thought. The report from Birmingham would come back after the new year and it would say what her chart said or it would not, and either way the county had her for the game, and the county could have her. He had a word waiting for him on a table in February and she was part of the price of the word. Ten years ago a woman on the academic side of some other building had done exactly this, a room with a door and a coach on the inside, and a screenshot had cost him a season and cost her a different life, and the rule he lived by since had her face on it. Aisha had that woman's job now, near enough, and had shut the door anyway, and would shut it again. She was afraid of all of it. She was not afraid of him. She would not have told him that she had turned the lamp on at seven and stood at that window in her socks for two hours and would have stood there till midnight, and she was not going to tell him now, because his hands were at her waist and going under her sweater to the skin of her back, warm, and not still at all, and there was no room left in her for the sentence.

She got the collar of the quarter-zip in both hands and pulled, and he understood her, and reached back for the neck of it and took it off over his head one-handed, a thing he had done with a hundred jerseys in a hundred locker rooms, and let go of it, and it went down on the floor of room nine by her boots under the rack, and lay there.

She looked at it lying there, on her floor, in her squared-away room, and she was going to let it lie there all night.
