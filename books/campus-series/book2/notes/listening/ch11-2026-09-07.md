## The chapter

On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board

Tim Brasher: Class signed this week. Eight days. Flying in for the nineteenth? The hotel block is full.

Kendra Voyles: Forty minutes out and calling it close enough.

DeeAnn Prewitt: Coach got his class. Now give him his quarterback.

Friday, December eleventh, and the nineteenth was eight days off. The class had signed this week. The building was pleased with itself, and the county had one thing left to want. It wanted Trey Gault on the field on the nineteenth, and Trey played on the nineteenth only if Aisha Cole signed a form saying his brain had healed, and it had not healed yet, so there was no form. The check ran at five fifteen on the turf, printed. This was the fourth one since Monday, and Monday was the day she had stopped asking Dan Merritt for anything. Four days of that now. Wine night was tonight, at the Mule, with Kat pouring, and she was going because Marisol had asked and because she had a thing to say to somebody who was not a chart.

The lights had been up white since five. She came onto the turf from the annex side with the sheet in one hand and the tablet in the other, in the parka, the cuffs pinned back, her hands out in the cold where Verna had put them, and Merritt came off the near hash to meet her with the trainer cutting tape a few yards off, same as every night this week. He took the sheet. He had Friday's jaw on him, dark, days of it.

He smelled like the field. The cold first, the kind that got into a jacket and stayed, and under the cold the crumb rubber of the turf, and under that a man who had run with kids for an hour and was warm all the way through, and it came the width of the sheet to where she stood. She had never had that from him. His voice, yes, and the size of him in a small room, and the heat off him through two coats at a rail, but not this, a yard of December air with him on the other side of it, and nothing in her training for what to do with that but stand there. She stood there.

"Thirty-four," she said. "Sleeve stays on through the weekend. Still no cutting. He cuts for me before he cuts for you."

"He'll ask."

"He's been asking all week. Tell him no again."

"And Trey."

"Held. Same as yesterday, same as Monday. The next step is when this one holds, and I'll say when that is."

He read it. Held overnight, in her typing, four days of it in a row, and the sheet said what she said, and he read it anyway. Then he did not hand it back. He stood there with it. He looked past her at the annex across the lot, low, dark at the cold end, and looked back at her, and she watched a man wait to be asked a question. He was not going to get one. She had handed the asking back on Monday in front of a man cutting tape, and she was not going to pick it up on a Friday because of a look on his face. Nobody asked anybody anything.

"That the sheet?" he said.

"That's the sheet."

"Doc."

"Coach." She put the tablet under her arm. "Sleeve stays on."

She went to thirty-four at the near hash and put her own hands on the sleeve, and the pins at her wrists went with her, and by five forty she was across the lot with the parka zipped and the smell of the field still with her, and could not have said, by the RAV4, how much of it was the field.

* * *

The Blind Mule had one booth, at the back past the pool table, under the dark TV, and wine night had it for the winter, because a porch was a September room and the Checkerboard ran one cook in the winter. Aisha came in at seven with the parka zipped to the chin. The coach's stool at the rail was empty. It was Friday, eight days out, and he was wherever a coach was at seven on a Friday eight days out. She had looked at the stool before she looked for the booth, and caught herself at it, and went to the back and sat down with her back to the rail.

Marisol had the wall side already, with her phone face down. Kat had driven in for the weekend with two bottles on the passenger seat and her phone full of listings, and she was pouring the first bottle into three mason jars when Peanut Kyzer came down the bar with the towel over his shoulder and stood at the end of the booth and looked at the bottle.

"House rule," he said. "Outside bottle, I keep the cork and I charge by the jar."

"Since when?" Kat said.

"Since you walked in with it." He took the cork off the table, put it in his shirt pocket, and went back up the bar.

"He's charging me for his own jars," Kat said.

"He's charging you for the room," Marisol said. "I'd have charged you for the cork."

Kat reached across and got hold of the parka where Aisha had folded it on the bench with the name up. "A. Cole," she read off it. "That is a man's coat."

"It's the team's coat. The order came in wrong."

"It came in a size."

"It came in the size they had."

"Honey, that coat has a coach in it somewhere."

"It has me in it," Aisha said, and took it back, and put it on the bench on her other side, out of reach.

"What's your board doing tonight," Kat said, because Marisol had the phone face down and kept looking at the back of it.

"Counting to eight," Marisol said. "Out loud, in three time zones, and asking after the Gault boy every third post."

"And the rest of it."

"The rest of it's still up." Marisol turned her jar by the rim. "Every post about you since the porch is still up. I've read them all. I could take them down in four minutes. I've timed it. And the minute I do, the next thing on that board is that the mod deletes what she doesn't like, and the mod's son is the walk-on that same board would like off the roster. It's got two names it likes this month. One of them's yours and the other one's mine. So they stay up. I leave the knife where it is so nobody can say I hid it. I can tell that board where to park. I can't tell it what to think about my own son."

"I have lanced things," Aisha said, "that were less angry than Rhonda Sipes."

"Rhonda drives in from out of state for every home game. She's earned an opinion. She hasn't earned yours."

"Speaking of February," Kat said, who had not been, and turned her phone around. "Condo on the square, second floor. Owner's in Dallas and lights it seven weekends a year. He'd take a doctor. He'd take a February."

"Everything you send me has a February in it."

"Everything in this town has a February in it. That's when everybody finds out if they're staying."

"That's not a listing, that's a prognosis."

"Condos," Marisol said. "Everybody wants one until they meet the HOA."

Kat had a story. She had started it in the lot, by the sound of it, and it came with the second pour. "So I'm showing the farmhouse out the county road, the one with the roof, to a couple from Atlanta, and the wife wants to see the cellar, and I have never been in that cellar, and we get the door open and there is a man down there with a headlamp on, sitting on a bucket, and he looks up at the three of us and says, 'Y'all the—"

The TV over the booth came on. Nobody had touched it. It was a man selling a knife set, at the volume a bar keeps a TV at on a Saturday, and it ran long enough for the man to cut a shoe in half, and then it went dark again, the same as it had come. Nobody at the rail turned around. Peanut did not look up from the pour line.

"Where was I," Kat said.

"A man on a bucket," Aisha said.

"Was there?" Kat looked at her jar. "I've lost him."

She had lost the man on the bucket and she had not lost anything else. She put both elbows on the table.

"So," she said. "The coach."

"No."

"You did this on a porch in September with a shaker in your hand. I've had the whole thing from Marisol, twice."

"I said he fought every call I made. That was a clinical description."

"It was a description," Marisol said.

Aisha picked up her jar and set it down again without drinking from it, and it made a sound on the table she had not meant it to.

"Fine," she said. "I want Dan."

She had never said his first name out loud in this county. It came out plain, one syllable, at the volume you would order a drink at, and the booth held still for it.

"I want him, and I can't do one thing about it, and I'm saying it in a bar to the two of you instead of to him, because a bar is where you can say a thing like that and have it go nowhere."

Nobody said anything. Kat, who had a line for everything, did not have one. Marisol had both hands around her jar and was looking at Aisha and not at the phone. Somebody broke a rack at the pool table, and the balls ran, and stopped. It went on. She let it. She had said it out loud. She had not died of it. She sat in it a while longer, and the two of them let her, and it was the kindest thing either of them did all night.

"And the other thing," she said, "while I've got the floor. I may not have a job here in February. The annex is on a survey. Everything in it boxed by February. The memo says my program's relocation will be addressed in a subsequent communication. That is a sentence somebody got paid to write. I've looked at the drawing on the film-room wall for my rooms, and they're not in it. That's all I know, and that's all anybody has told me. So there it is. I want a man I work across a sheet from, and by spring I may not work across anything from anybody."

Kat took her elbows off the table.

"For the record," she said, "I'd put the two of you in the farmhouse. Bad roof and all. He can reach the gutters."

Aisha laughed, the one she had, all at once and too loud for the back of a bar, and did not say a word against the farmhouse.

"Roof's still bad," she said.

"Roofs get fixed."

Marisol had not said a word since the name. She was looking at Aisha over her jar, and Aisha watched her hear it and keep it, and knew without a word passing that it was not going on that board or anywhere else, tonight or ever. A woman who could leave a knife where it lay for her own son's sake could hold one thing for a friend.

"Drink your wine," Marisol said.

"I've had my wine. I'm driving."

Peanut set a seltzer at her elbow before she had turned around to ask for one, the can sweating, and went back up the bar, and she drank that for the rest of the night.

Nobody was going to tell him. Kat would take it back up the road on Sunday. Marisol would take it home and put it nowhere. Aisha would take it to the turf on Monday and hand him a sheet across it. A booth was where you said a thing so it could go nowhere.

* * *

Kat walked her to the door and said the rest of it into her ear, low, by the brick, "Gutters, Aisha," and Aisha went out into the cold with it.

The Zinnia Wagon was at the curb with its tailgate down, and Birdie Pham was loading wreaths and bundled greenery back into the bed by the light of her phone, what the square's storefronts had not taken.

"Good night in there?" Birdie said.

"Kat's here."

"Then it was."

Aisha took a wreath off the curb and handed it up. "I said a thing in there," she said, "that I'd have said at twenty-five and known better than to say since."

"Everybody's twenty-five once," Birdie said, and took the wreath. "The internet just wasn't there for mine, thank God."

It was not the truth. It was kinder than the truth, from a woman young enough that the internet had certainly been there, and Aisha took it as handed.

She sat in the RAV4 with the engine running and the heat coming up, and Merritt was in the car with her the whole time, the Friday jaw, the field on him, the sheet he had stood there holding like a man who would rather have been handed something else. He had waited tonight for a question, and she had watched him wait and given him the sleeve instead. She had said his name in a bar. It was still his name in the car, and it was warm in here now, and she put it in drive.

* * *

The Blind Mule at midnight, last call, and Mackenzie Doyle was on a stool at the rail with her second beer gone flat in the can and the receivers coach on the stool beside her, where he had been since ten. Ty Beaumont, receivers, on Coach Merritt's staff and nowhere on hers, which had been the first thing she checked and the second thing she liked. He was built like the position he coached, long and quick, and he had kept his hands on his own side of the rail all night, which she had noticed, and he had meant her to. She had taken her hair down at eleven and had not decided yet whether that was for him. She had come to Ashford State this fall to run admissions. A semester in, she could say carillon without thinking about it, and she knew which side of the square the university drank on. It was this side, at the rail, on a Friday. There was nobody at home for either of them. She had asked him that on Monday in a Fieldhouse doorway with his coat in her hands, plainly, and he had said nobody, and she had said same. Two beers in two hours apiece. Sober enough that it was a choice, and that was the only kind she made.

Peanut Kyzer rang the bell on the rail once. "That's it," he said. "Settle up. Lot's icy. Mind the step."

Down the rail a phone lit in the hand of a woman from the registrar's office whom Mackenzie knew to nod to, and Mackenzie nodded, and thought nothing about it, and got her coat.

Ty paid for both cans and did not make a thing of it, and she let him, and did not make a thing of that either.

Two people leaving a bar in one truck was a sentence in a town this size, and she knew it. She had looked down the rail before she stood up, and nobody was looking. The lot was gravel with the cold come down hard on it, and his truck sat at the far edge, a crew cab, the long dark shape of it with the four doors, and they walked to it together, not fast.

"This is a bad idea," she said, at the passenger door.

"It's a good one. It's a bad month for it."

"Shit, it's cold."

He opened the door and the dome light came on and put the two of them in a box of yellow for the whole lot to see. She got in fast. He came around and got in and reached up and killed the light with the flat of his hand.

"Mack," he said, in the dark. Nobody else called her that. She had told him so once, and he had kept it.

The staff jacket came off him over the console and went into the back seat, and her coat was next, his hands at the zipper and hers over his, because she wanted it off more than he did, and then it was off, and his mouth was at the place under her ear he had been looking at since ten, and she reached up past his shoulder for the handle over the door, for something to hold on to, with the windows already going white around them.
