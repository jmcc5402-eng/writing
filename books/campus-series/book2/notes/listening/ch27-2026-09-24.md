## Before you read: where we are

**The plot.**

The trustees' boardroom, forty seats, every one full. Earlene in the hallway says today they find out if doing her job is allowed.

The reviewer's first question is why her ladder ran slower than other programs'. She answers from the chart: the August baseline, the dated orders, the forty-one boxes locked away since December. A trustee asks what they would be saying today if she had cleared him and he had gone down.

Marisol lays eleven signatures beside the consultant's handout. One coined phrase, in both. Who wrote it stays unproven, and she says so.

Then Dan, unscheduled, in the doorway in the suit he hates. The university's lawyer half stands. Dan says it in front of everyone: "I said I'd have played him. That's exactly why the rule says I don't decide." Then her name. She watches it cost him.

By evening the pledge is "re-sequenced," and his extension is off the February agenda.

**Aisha.**

Turned, but not toward him yet. She fights for her own record first. His words land in her chest, and she still has a car packed.

## The chapter

On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board

DossCoDadx3: Our youngest called home to ask if you can microwave an egg. She is a junior. Her mother took the phone away from me. I am not allowed to know how it ended.

The coat rack outside the trustees' boardroom was going over under the weight of half the county's coats, and Aisha Cole caught it by the top hook and stood it back up against the wall. Nobody was taking a coat back out on this Monday, the first of February. They had come to watch the independent review sit, and on Wednesday it would say in writing whether she had made the right call when she held Trey Gault from a playoff game. If it said she was wrong, that went on her record, and her record went with her to every job she would ever apply for. The offer out west was made to a doctor with a clean one. She doubted it would outlast a finding against her.

Verna Poteat, who ran the Magnolia Court, the motel where Aisha rented room nine, had driven her in at eight in her own car. The loaded RAV4 stayed in Verna's gravel, where nobody at the review would see it packed to go, with the team's parka on the top box. The wool coat was back in nine. She had on the one good jacket she had come to town with, too thin for the hallway.

Through the double doors all forty seats were taken, and people stood along the back wall. Half of them had liked something against her on the parents' board, and half had come to see she got a fair hearing, and she could not have told which half by their coats.

Missy Gault sat on the aisle in the third row with her purse and her notebook in her lap. Missy wanted her son safe, and she wanted him on the field, and Aisha had watched her try to hold both in the same two hands since the night Trey went down in November. Beside her sat Denny Gault, Trey's father, with his coat buttoned to the collar, indoors. He had held court about his boy all fall, and this morning he had not said a word to anybody. At a table on the right sat the athletic director, and beside him the university's lawyer, a man in a gray suit with no folder. The lawyer looked like a man who wanted nothing said by anybody, ever.

Earlene Tatum had saved the front row with her scarf and come back out to the hallway bench to wait on the doctor.

"All you ever did in this county was the job they hired you for," Earlene said. "This morning we see if the county lets a person get away with that."

"I'd like to know that myself," Aisha said.

Earlene looked past her at the front doors. "Your coach isn't here."

"He isn't on the review's list."

"Lists," Earlene said, as if she had found one on the bottom of her shoe.

Aisha did not look at the doors. *Your coach*, in Earlene's dry voice, put her heart up hard. She wanted Dan to come through those front doors so badly her palms had gone damp, and she wanted him to stay away just as badly. On Thursday night she had told him to say nothing Monday, and what it would cost him if he did. Then her back had been against her own door and her fist in the front of his coat. His mouth had been on hers, warm and angry, until she was the one who stopped. Her mouth remembered it now, in a hallway full of the county, and heat went up her neck under the thin jacket. She had sent him home. He had gone. Until signing day on Wednesday, if anybody caught a coach and his team physician together, the university would fire them both the same day. Her breath came short. She let it.

At nine the reviewer sat down at the front table and read the rules off one typed sheet. Aisha had a small table at the left with her own printed copy of the record tabbed at her elbow, not the paper chart in her office drawer, and no lawyer, because she had not thought she needed one to tell the truth about Trey's brain.

"This review sits in an open room," the reviewer said. "It will hear anyone it chooses to, at my discretion, and nobody else. Everything said goes in the transcript. The NCAA's own rules call a team physician's authority over return to play unchallengeable and autonomous. That rule is Independent Medical Care. Did Dr. Cole use it well? That's the question. I'm told the coffee in the front hall is better than it looks."

"Dr. Cole," the reviewer said. "You may sit to answer, if you like."

She stood. Her knees wanted to lock. She unlocked them. She put one palm flat on the stack of paper so it would keep still.

"The checklist lets a step go in a day," the reviewer said. "It lets a player ride out a mild headache on the bike. At the student's first headache you stopped him and started him over. Most programs would have had him back for that game. Why did yours run slower?"

It was the fair question. Dan had pushed her on it across her sheet all fall. He was the only coach who had ever argued it to her face, and the only one who took her no as if it were worth having. The thought of him warmed the palm she had on the paper, and her voice came out steady.

"A day is the least a step takes," Aisha said. "The checklist never says it's the most. At step two, sixteen days out, fourteen minutes into the bike, the patient's hand went up to his head. That was more than the checklist lets a physician ride through, so the checklist stopped him, not me. I stepped him down, and his clock started over. Past two weeks it wants a physician to look again, and I did, in January. His family booked Birmingham themselves. I sent the whole chart there on my own. What Birmingham told them is theirs to say."

She said *the patient* every time. She had said Trey to his face every day of the season, to his mother in the hall, and at the top of every page in his chart, because he was a person and not a number. Here there was a transcript, and a transcript was a thing a scout could ask to read some spring, so Trey's name stayed out of it. Nobody in the rows noticed. It cost her the one warm word she had in that boardroom, and it ached going, low in her stomach. She let it go and kept on.

"Walk the review through the chart."

She did, from August's baseline to his mother's seven o'clock pages, and every rung of it had a date. She had built it that way in November for a morning like this one and hoped the whole time she would never have to stand up in front of a county and need it. Her voice held. Her heart did not, quite.

"And the annex's records?"

"Forty-one boxes," Aisha said. "They've been locked at Mt. Nebo Missionary Baptist since December, and my count matches the church's. Delores Fountain-Mabry keeps the church's count on a legal pad."

"The review would like to see that pad."

"It doesn't leave the church," Aisha said. "She says the review is welcome to drive over and ask her for it."

A few people along the back wall laughed, and one of them said *Delores* as if that settled it. The reviewer's mouth moved at one corner. The pen kept going. Aisha's breath eased a notch.

A trustee in the second row lifted a hand, and the reviewer nodded.

"I'd like one question in the transcript," the trustee said. "If she'd cleared that boy and he'd gone down on that field, what would all of us be saying about her this morning?"

Nobody answered it. The trustee had asked it for the record, not for an answer. Aisha's neck went hot. Two rows back, a man in a feed-store cap looked at the floor between his boots.

Aisha sat down. Her palm came off the top page damp.

Marisol Pruett stood up at the end of the second row at half past ten and asked to be heard. The reviewer looked at her. "The review will hear you."

Marisol moderated the Ashford parents' board, and the eleven families had put their letter together on it. Her own son, Wes, was a walk-on, and the letter's questions about the roster were questions about boys like him. She carried her folder up the aisle in both hands and held it where the back rows could see it.

She laid two sheets side by side on the reviewer's table, under the lamp.

"This is the families' letter," Marisol said. "It was organized on the board I moderate, so I have their own copy. This is a page of the consultant's printed materials. The trustees put them in the February agenda packet, for the public." She put one finger on each sheet. "Eleven families signed this, and I know every one of them. They're good people, and they signed what they believed. But the middle of it says *proactive risk posture*, and I've never heard one of them say that at a ballgame. It's in these materials word for word. The materials came to town with the consultant the pledge brought. I can't prove who put his words in their letter. I can show you they're his."

The sound that went through the rows was not loud. A woman in the fourth row who had signed covered her mouth and kept it covered. From her table Aisha could see the two sheets under the lamp, and the same three words sat in the middle of both like a phone number copied off one page onto another. Her stomach went cold.

The athletic director's office had sent Dan a paper copy of that letter, and she had got it by email the same week, and neither of them had said a word about it to the other. Her rule was that her calls got talked about across the sheet, with a trainer in earshot, and nowhere else. Dan had kept it. Her throat closed. Her heart went over once, hard.

Marisol went back down the aisle with her empty folder and sat.

"Who wrote a letter isn't evidence about a call," the reviewer said. "The call is what this review is for. Last Monday this review asked the head coach whether he would have played his quarterback on the nineteenth. His answer goes into the record. 'Every coach in the country plays him. I'd have played him.'"

She had heard those eleven words once before, on the radio, in a stranger's flat voice.

The reviewer laid the typed page on the front table. "Dr. Cole, your own head coach says he'd have played him. What do you say to that?"

Aisha stood again.

"I can only answer for the call," she said. "I signed the no-go. At step two the checklist stopped the patient, and I held him from there."

Anything she said about Dan would go into the transcript as something about the two of them.

Behind her the rows settled back, chairs creaking all the way to the back wall, and she heard it more than she saw it. The man in the feed-store cap nodded slowly. The university's lawyer eased back in his chair, and she heard that too. Missy Gault's hand went to the clasp of her purse and stayed there. In the front row Earlene, who had an opinion out loud about most things, had gone quiet. Eleven words off a typed page had turned the room in under a minute.

Aisha sat back down. The palm of each hand had gone cold on the tabbed record, and sweat ran down her spine under the thin jacket. For once she was glad Dan was not there to see it.

The reviewer's pen was still moving when the door at the back of the boardroom opened. Aisha turned in her chair, and so did every row behind her.

Dan Merritt stood in the doorway in a suit. He hated a suit, and anybody could see it from how he stood in this one, with his shoulders set against the cloth as if it were a hit he had seen coming all week. His jaw was set hard. He did not look at the rows, or at her. The cold went down the backs of her knees.

The lawyer was half out of his chair with one hand up. He stayed like that, half standing, as if he could hold the door shut from across the room.

"Coach Merritt," the reviewer said. "You aren't on this review's list."

"No," Dan said. "I'm asking to be heard."

The reviewer looked at him, and at the lawyer, and down at the typed sheet. "This review said it would hear anyone it chose to. Go ahead, Coach."

The lawyer sat back down slowly.

Dan came up the aisle. She knew the price of every step of it. If he said what she thought he had come to say, his extension would never reach the trustees in February, and every man on his staff had come to this town on that contract. He walked past the university's lawyer, and she had to sit still and watch him walk into it. Her pulse went hard in her wrist. He stopped an arm's length from her, and she could smell the cold of the lot on his suit.

"My quarterback got hurt on the twenty-eighth of November," Dan said, "and Dr. Cole held him from that night on. She signed her no-go for the nineteenth on Thursday the seventeenth of December. That's eleven days before the families' letter got to the athletic director."

His voice was low. It carried to the back wall anyway. It came from just past her shoulder, and her whole back went tight, from her spine out to her arms.

She knew what he was doing by his second sentence. Her hand came up off the chart. She corrected people for a living. She had corrected men twice his size in hospital hallways and on sidelines. Every muscle she had was built to stand up now and say *Coach, that's not necessary*, and then take the record back and say it better herself. She had never let a man pay for her. Not a dinner, not the rent, not one word on her behalf she had not asked for. Her mouth opened on *Coach*. She shut it. She put the hand back down on the chart.

"I pushed her," Dan said. "All season I argued her Friday sheet at the medical check, and I lost on the merits every time. When my quarterback got hurt, she gave him twenty minutes in our meeting room with no film, because film works a hurt brain as hard as an exam. I went around her to the athletic director for the whole meeting with film on, and he told me no in writing. Everywhere I pushed, it broke on her and not on the boy. She held."

Heat went up her chest into her face. Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in the palm on the chart. In the third row Missy Gault's notebook had shut on her thumb, and her other hand was still on the clasp of her purse, and beside her Denny's face had gone dark red above the buttoned collar. At *not on the boy* he shut his eyes, and his throat worked, and he kept them shut longer than a blink.

"My staff did what I told them," Dan said. "Whatever came at Dr. Cole from my side of the lot came from me first. Put it on me, and leave them out of it. She cost me my starting quarterback for a playoff game. If he walked in hurt tomorrow, she'd cost me him again, and I'd lose that argument too."

The laugh went through the rows all at once, surprised out of them. In the front row even Earlene's shoulders went. Aisha did not laugh. Her eyes had gone hot, and she kept them on the edge of the table so they would not blink.

"Could I have my answer from last Monday?" Dan said.

The reviewer held the typed page out across the front table, and Dan leaned and took it. The page rattled once in his hand, loud in the quiet.

"Every coach in the country plays him," Dan read. "I'd have played him."

It was the first time she had heard those eleven words out of his own mouth.

"That much got on the radio," Dan said. "Here's the rest of it. It's the part that matters."

He looked down at the page. Then he looked up at the rows. Every face in the forty seats was turned up to him, and every face along the back wall, and she could not hear one of them breathe. The boardroom went quiet enough to hear the radiator tick under the windows. A chair creaked behind her, and whoever sat in it went still. The lawyer's hand came up off his knee again and stopped in the air. Aisha could not get a breath all the way down. Her own blood was louder in her ears than the radiator. He let the quiet run on past where anybody else would have filled it.

"I said I'd have played him. That's exactly why the rule says I don't decide."

He turned his head then and looked at her for the first time. His face was pale under the new haircut, but the hard set had gone out of his jaw. His eyes on hers were warm and sure, and there was relief in them, as if he had put down something he had carried since the radio. He said the rest to the reviewer with his eyes still on her.

"Dr. Aisha Cole decides. She held my quarterback in December, and she was right."

Behind her the room let its breath out. A woman along the back wall said *Amen*, low, as if she were in church, and the man in the feed-store cap had taken his cap off. Farther down the wall a big man in a work coat stood with his arms folded and his hat on his head, and at *she was right* he kept both where they were.

Missy's eyes had filled. Her knuckles had gone pale on the clasp. She looked at Denny and then at Dan, and Aisha could see the pull in her, her boy safe on one side and her husband on the other.

On the fourteenth of January he had said *her protocol* on the radio and left her name off it. Last Monday he had said *I'd have played him* and stopped. Now her whole name was in the transcript in his voice, with the rest of his sentence in front of it.

Her breath went out of her and did not come back in. Her chest had gone so tight it hurt, and under the hurt it was warm, and she could not tell one from the other. Her hands were on the chart and shaking, and she left them where anybody could see them shake. She had not asked him. Nobody had ever stood up in a room for her without being asked, and she had spent her whole working life making sure that nobody in any building she worked in would ever have to. Under all of it, where she did not look at it straight, she was glad. A man had paid for her in front of everyone, with his own name, and it was the one thing she had never let herself want. It was sweet enough to shame her. Under the table her knees would not keep still.

Everything in her wanted to stand up and tell him he had said too much. She did not stand. She sat still in the good jacket with her hands on the chart, and she let him pay it in front of the whole county, and it was the hardest thing she had done all winter.

She was so proud of him it hurt, down the inside of each wrist, and she had never said that to a man in her life and could not say it now to this one.

He held her eyes a second longer, and then one more, long enough for her to feel the lawyer watching and the whole county at her back, and she could not be the one who looked away first. Her skin went hot. Her palm slid on the chart. Then he looked back at the reviewer and laid the page on the front table.

"Thank you, Coach Merritt," the reviewer said. "The review will take fifteen minutes."

Dan left first, before anybody else had stood up. He went out the back door, and through the glass in it Aisha watched him turn left in the corridor, toward the back of the building, where nobody in the room had any reason to go.

Chairs scraped at the recess, and the whole room went out the front doors toward the coffee, talking before they were through them. The athletic director went out ahead of them with his phone already at his ear, and through the double doors Aisha watched him walk past the urn and out the front of the building. She waited at her table until the lawyer was at the urn with his back to the boardroom. Her spine ached when she stood. Then she went out the back door and turned left, the way Dan had gone.

Forty people and a lawyer were two thin walls away.

The back stairs were behind a fire door at the end of the corridor, concrete, with a window of wired glass on the landing. It was colder there than in the hall. Dan was on the landing four steps down, with his back against the cinder block and his hands at his sides. He looked up when the fire door opened.

She let it close behind her on its arm, slowly, so it would not bang. Neither of them said anything. A word would only have been something for the hallway to overhear. She went down the four steps to the landing. She stopped a foot from him. His eyes came up to hers and stayed there. Through two walls she could hear the coffee crowd, a laugh, a spoon on a cup, a man calling across the hall to his wife. His hand came up an inch from his side, and hers came up at the same time, and their knuckles knocked together, and for a second neither of them knew what to do about that.

The corner of his mouth went, and hers went with it. It was nearly a laugh, two grown people knocking knuckles on a back stair, and neither of them let it out. Then his eyes went down the good jacket and back up, and she watched him see it. She had not worn it for him. She was glad he had seen it anyway. Then his fingers turned over and closed around her hand.

The warm of his palm went up the inside of her wrist and into her chest. Her breath caught and would not come back right. His thumb moved once across the backs of her knuckles, and her stomach turned over slowly and kept turning. Her heart went hard and high under the good jacket, and she was sure he could hear it. Heat came up into her face, and she let it, because there was nobody on those stairs to hide it from. Her skin had gone awake everywhere at once. Her knees had gone loose under her. She locked them.

She wanted him, right there on a concrete landing, with the lawyer and the county and a transcript on the other side of two walls. She was angry at him for what he had just thrown away, and she was holding on to his hand with everything she had, and she could not make those two things into one thing. Her head was clear. His was too. She could see it in his eyes.

They held on while she counted one, slowly.

Then his fingers opened, and hers did. He went on down the stairs without a word, and she heard the outside door at the bottom let him out and shut on its closer. She stood on the landing with her hand still a little out from her side until the heat went out of her face. When she went back through the fire door, the lawyer was still at the urn with his back to the boardroom.

The review heard two more people and adjourned at three, to sit once more on Wednesday morning and give its findings. Marisol drove her back to the Court.

The light was going off Verna's gravel by five. Aisha sat on Verna's office step, still in the good jacket, with an unopened can of sparkling water beside her. Across the lot the RAV4 sat loaded in front of nine. The acceptance was still typed under the chair's email on her phone, unsent.

Verna came out of the office behind her with her phone in her hand.

"I said I'd hear it all by noon," Verna said. "Earlene had me at your recess, by a quarter to. She says you stood up in front of that whole county, and it listened." She lowered herself onto the step beside Aisha, one hand on the rail. "I'm glad, my winter doctor. I've been glad since a quarter to twelve."

Aisha's shoulders came down for the first time since the coat rack, and she laughed once, short and surprised, and her breath went up white in the cold. She let herself have it. Dan had said her name, the whole of it. For a minute on Verna's cold step she was glad all the way to her fingers, with somebody glad beside her.

Then Verna turned her phone over in her lap.

"They posted the trustees' February agenda over again this afternoon," Verna said. "I had this morning's saved, so I read the two of them line against line. The pledge for the new building says *re-sequenced to a later sitting*. That's their word for moved back, with no date on it. Your coach's extension isn't on it anymore. They struck it."

The pledge was Rex Boyd's money, for the building the survey flags had marked out on top of her clinic. Why it had moved she could only guess. His consultant's phrase had been read into a transcript this morning in front of a trustee, and money like his did not like a transcript. For one breath she let herself think the ground under her clinic was not going anywhere this month. The rest of that sum was her own job, and she was not going to do it tonight.

The extension was Dan's, and by the afternoon of the day he said her name it was off the page. Now she felt what it weighed, an ache across her shoulders and in both knees. Every man who had ever helped her in a building had wanted a thing for it. The good ones had just taken longer to say what. This one had stood up for her in front of the whole county and gone down the back stairs without a word, and she was still waiting on the step to hear his price.

"I know what it cost him," Aisha said. "I watched him pay it."

"He's a grown man," Verna said. "He knew the price when he walked in."

They sat on the step in the cold, and neither of them went in. Aisha's right hand lay open in her lap, palm up, the hand he had held on the back stairs, and she left it open.
