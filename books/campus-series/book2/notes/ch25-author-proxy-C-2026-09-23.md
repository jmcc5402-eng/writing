# AUTHOR-PROXY — ch 25 candidate C (2026-09-23, C3, blind, before the panel)

author-proxy 1.1.1 (the third trial), card C3 (dialogue-only first pass, then the rest). Filed by the orchestrator from the proxy's final message; the text is the proxy's.

Read blind. Card C3: I read the dialogue alone first, then the whole chapter. Read that way, Aisha says four things out loud all day, and all four are errands. Delores is the only person who says out loud that she was right. The Verna exchange is the one I could not follow.

**l.1–457, the romance level.** I'd put romance at about a 4 and heat at 0, because they are never in a room together. Every romance moment is her alone with something of his: the empty stool (l.46), the slice, his handwriting on the last box (l.225), his truck across the lot (l.250–254), the gravel with no tracks (l.352), the sleeve (l.421–432). There is real conflict between them this time, because he said a thing and she's furious. But it only runs one way. She fights with him in her head all day and he never gets a word back. His truck sits across the lot at one in the afternoon and nothing happens with it. The best romance in the chapter is the sleeve at the very end, and it's a memory. The card aimed at a 6. Right now it reads like her longing plus a grudge. Is that enough romance for this chapter? I don't think so. [MORE]

**l.83–122, the radio.** Her body is there: her neck, her face, her hand closing on the counter, her throat, her knees (l.83–92). And "*I knew it.*" is there (l.94), which is what I asked for. But then "It was almost a relief" (l.96) lets the air out right away. After that, the narrator tells me she's angry ("She was angry, all the way down to her hands," l.113) more than I hear her being angry at him in her own head. Within a few lines she's working through the department's statement (l.119–122) and being fair to the man two stools down ("The man was not wrong to think it," l.136). This is the ultimate wound-tearing-open moment. I want her head to be ugly for a minute, "once again a man is doing me wrong," before she's fair to anybody. [MORE]

**l.385–395, why she goes.** In the morning she's angry because he threw her under the bus. By night she's taking the job because "Going also cost Dan nothing. That settled it" (l.385). She has always meant to be the one who pays. So she's leaving to protect him, and that lets Dan right off the hook. I said on 24 that I want the reader really mad at Dan. This chapter gets them mad at the counter and then hands him back his halo by bedtime. The card says she leaves because nobody asked her to stay, and because the one man who might have just told the county he'd have played the boy. That isn't the reason on the page. Also, why decide tonight? The findings that could clear her come Wednesday the third (l.69). The answer isn't due until Saturday the sixth (l.159). A reader will ask why she doesn't wait three days to hear if she was right. [POINT]

**l.151–159, the offer.** Would a department chair really put a directorship on letterhead for a doctor six days into a suspension, eight days before the review says whether she was right? That's the same problem as the suspension before it: I'm not sure a real institution does this. And "Rex Boyd had passed her name along three weeks ago, so that she would go" (l.153) is stated as fact. How does she know that? The engine behind this setback is offstage. The TK at l.155 says so: the university, the chair and how the chair reached her are on no page. [POINT]

**l.64–66 and l.131–133, the other side's case.** It's good that somebody at the counter says it: "Most any other doctor, that boy plays in that playoff game." And the coach's "Every coach in the country plays him" makes her call arguable. But both are a verdict, not a reason. Nobody on this page says what she did that the other doctors wouldn't have done. I still can't tell why an honest person would think she was wrong, only that they do. [WHO]

**l.212–217, the trainer.** "He wanted to help her, and he wanted his job, and he stood in the door where both of those could see him." That tells me his want, but it reads like a riddle, not like a man. His only lines are "if it was up to me" and "It isn't." This is the guy who rode a foot away from her in the cab down Millrow. He owes ten thousand dollars and is stuck between her and the coach. He's the one person who knows exactly what happened, and he's a flat face in a doorway. [WHO]

**l.335–344 and l.441–445, Verna and the rent.** I don't understand this scene. Verna hands the rent back and says "The heater rattled." Aisha says it doesn't rattle. Then Aisha "thought Verna meant the furnace in nine." Is Verna making up an excuse to let her stay free? Does Aisha not get it? Then at the step: "That one took you nine minutes. I counted." Is nine minutes fast or slow? Is Verna asking her to stay? This might be the only moment in the chapter where somebody asks her to stay, and I can't tell whether it happened. [CONFUSING]
