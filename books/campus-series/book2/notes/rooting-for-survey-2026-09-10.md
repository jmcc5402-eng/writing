# Rooting-for survey, second run — ch 13–16 (developmental-editor, card E4, 2026-09-10)

## The shape I found

Sixteen chapters in, this is the story of two careful people who kept every rule they made — hers on a printed sheet, his in a locker room — and lost the game anyway, and who now have seven weeks of a dark lot and one lit window. That matches the premise, the arc docs and the romance arc's stage 4. On the author's question — are they rooted for — the four chapters since the last survey are better than the thirteen before them, and the reason is simple: the rule in B2-D16 (4) put a kindness before the first romance beat, and where it was obeyed it worked. The finding this run is narrower than last time and it has two halves. First, both leads' kindness has drawn in toward the plot: since ch 13 every one of Dan's acts goes to his backup or to her, and every one of Aisha's goes to the Gaults or to thirty-four's leg. Second, the "seen" rungs — the ones the other lead watches — are being spoken aloud to the doer's face, and the book is one line away from praising its leads through each other's mouths.

What is genuinely working and must survive: the first-team line at six in the dark with its price in one clause (`ch14:21–28`) and the chapter that opens on it and closes on the kid's feet (`ch14:417–418`); Missy's "What step does it say he's on." and Aisha's private credit to a notebook (`ch15:63–74`); a coach who bends and shuts a stranger's cooler on his way by and gets no line for it (`ch16:19–24`); the tape held up between two fingers "so he could see the nothing" (`ch16:341–347`); the ball with the name up, drying (`ch16:505–516`). Every one of those is the Scout's blister: competent, small, nobody important watching, nothing back.

## 6. For the author

Yes, they are rooted for now, and more than they were at chapter 13. The small kindnesses did it: a coach who puts a kid's name on the first-team line at six in the morning and tells nobody, and a doctor who calls the boy's mother before the paper goes to her boss and gets asked "What step does it say he's on" instead of thanked. Those are the Band-Aid at the bonfire, and the readers will like these two for them. What is missing is variety. Since chapter 13 everything kind Dan does goes to his backup or to Aisha, and everything kind Aisha does goes to the Gaults or to one boy's leg — so the reader is starting to think of him as generous and of her as merely steady, and steady is the thing you admire, not the thing you root for. Give her one kindness in the gray season that has nothing to do with the case — thirty-four on her stool at the Checkerboard, or Verna's phone on Christmas Eve — and give him one in January that the plot can't use. And watch the one habit that has crept in: in the last three chapters each of them ends the night by telling the other what a good thing the other did. Twice that was the romance doing its job. The third time, "that's yours, and nobody up on that fence is going to say so," is the book paying its own compliment, and it should come out.
