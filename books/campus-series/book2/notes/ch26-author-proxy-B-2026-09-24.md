**1.** l.336–367: "Her eyes came up to his and held, bright and angry"
/ "clear-eyed and furious." At the end of 25 she's furious — "I knew
it," a man is doing me wrong. Here the anger is only in her eyes. What
comes out of her mouth is all noble: "Keep quiet, Dan. Sign what they
put in front of you in February… Let me be what it costs you, and call
it cheap." She never says to his face what he did: you told the whole
county I was wrong. This is the only room they share all chapter. Let
her be mad at him out loud for a few lines first, then the offers. The
kiss will hit harder for it. [MORE]

**2.** l.113–144: "He would do what the lawyer said." The AD tells him
she has a job out west with an answer due the sixth. He gets about
three lines (keys in his palm, heat up his neck, chest hollow), then it
turns into a spreadsheet: the dream job, the coordinator, Ty's truck
payment, the both-fired rule, and the decision in one line. Where's the
part where he realizes she's gone in nine days and he may never stand
in a room with her again? For the rest of the day he never feels that
she's leaving (the step at l.199–200 is lovely, but it's the only
time). Five scenes in a row sit at 3 or under. The decision in one line
after a list is what I said about 24: too easy. Make him go back and
forth, with her in it and not only the job. [MORE]

**3.** l.60–66: "There was a second half, and Dan had it ready… The
rest of it stayed where it was." I never get to hear the second half.
This chapter's job is for the reader to understand him — an honest
mistake, because his head was on her. The page never tells me what he
actually meant, so part of me still thinks he meant "I'd have played
him." Put the half he means in his head, in words; then when he says
it out loud in 27 I recognize the sentence he didn't finish. Then one
plain line of him understanding what those words do to her — "Something
had gone wrong at that table, and he did not know what yet" (l.72) is
never come back to. [WHO]

**4.** l.95–96: "Dan had heard the shape of that sentence before, from
a man in his own office door who would not sit down." Who is that? I
believe it's Boyd, from 20. The July line only makes the reader hate
Boyd if the reader knows it's him. Dan knows, so let him think the
name. [WHO]

**5.** l.123: "He had not called her since the radio had it Tuesday."
How did the radio get it? A closed room, a reviewer and the
university's own lawyer — by morning his words are on 1340, and Dan
never once wonders who put them there. A real person would be sick
about that, or furious. I don't need it solved, but the page has to
notice it. [POINT]

**6.** l.433–436: "At the road he put the turn signal on, and it was
the wrong side for home." Where's he going at nine at night? If this is
him deciding to speak Monday, I need a line in his head saying that's
what the turn means. Also lost as a stranger: l.136–137 "together in
the season" — two pages later Tick says "No game till fall"; say the
rule runs through signing day. l.132 "Ty went with his truck payment" —
Ty who? l.45–46 "the coat on the hook by his door, the mate of hers" —
what coat? l.235 "Nineteen to nothing, and all nineteen of them were
his" — read it twice. l.311–312 "The lamp in nine was off and the
curtain was open. None of it was for him." — I don't know what that
means. [CONFUSING]

**7.** l.342–343 and l.426–427: "If there's a second half to that
sentence, it isn't mine. It belongs to the people who wrote down the
first half." That's the writer talking about the sentence, not a woman
in a stripped motel room — and Dan repeats it in the truck. Something
plain, like "Say it to them, not me," does the same job. [NOSE]

**8.** l.261–265: "Tell him to wear a coat, Coach." / "Wear a coat,
son. It's January." / "Nobody on the porch laughed." The one funny
moment in the chapter, and the page kills it. Second chapter running of
a lead's worst week. Give the porch its laugh and let Dan almost have
one. [FUN]
