Verna's gravel was loud under Dan Merritt's boots
at half past nine on Sunday night,
the only sound on that end of town,
and his hands were in the pockets of the sideline jacket
with nothing in them but the cold.
The truck was up at the Fieldhouse where he had left it.
Those were the terms, and they were his own:
nothing anybody could see until the first Wednesday in February,
and a two-door F-150 in a motel lot was a thing anybody could see,
once there was anybody.
The county was home Friday.
Until then the town was one lit diner, one motel office,
and the lamp in room nine with the curtain open and Aisha Cole behind it.
He had walked down to that lamp again
through a square with no one on it.

He had thought about her the whole way down, which was new,
and he had let himself.
Most of it went on the thing her mouth did
while she was deciding whether to laugh at him,
and the rest on what to say at her door
so that it came out right this time.

The bolt on nine went back while he was still on the gravel.
Aisha opened the door and stood in it in her socks,
with the lamp behind her and her arms folded against the cold.
She waited for him to cover the last of it,
and her eyes were laughing before he got there.

"You walk loud for a man trying not to be seen," she said.

"There's nobody to hear it but you and Verna."

"Verna's light's on."

"Verna's light's been on every night I've known her."
He stopped on the step below hers, which put their eyes level,
and he had not planned that and liked it.
"I did the math on the way down.
Five nights, counting this one, and then the county's back.
I didn't like the number, so I counted it again.
It came out the same."

"I did it on the square at five this morning," she said.
"I got what you got.
Now come in. I've got one chair and I'm not standing in a door all night."

She stepped back and he came in past her and she shut the door.
The sound of that door shutting was the week, near enough,
a motel door closing on the two of them
with the whole county somewhere else.
The night went as Saturday's had gone,
with the lamp on until neither of them was looking at it
and the furnace running behind under the north wind.

At five on Monday morning Dan walked back up through the square
in the dark, the truck keys in his jacket pocket,
on his way to the Fieldhouse.
The square was a sound before it was anything to look at.
The Checkerboard's hood fan ran over the griddle
behind the one lit window on the block.
Under the streetlights on the far side of the loop
a pair of running shoes hit the pavement, steady, and got closer.
Aisha came around the courthouse corner with her breath showing
and her curls tied back off her face, and did not slow down.
She lifted one hand off her stride going past him,
and he lifted his.
The cook's window was the only pair of eyes on the square,
and a man walking past a woman running was worth nothing to it.
"Morning, Coach," she said over her shoulder,
in the voice she used across a sheet.
It was a joke, the public name with nobody to hear it,
and he heard her laugh at it herself before she got to the corner.

The letter came on paper Monday morning, a courtesy copy
from the athletic director's office in a campus envelope.
Dan read it once at his desk with his jacket still on.
Eleven parents had signed it, a column of out-of-state names,
asking the department for clarity on medical decision-making
and roster priorities before the window opened.
The phrase *proactive risk posture* was in the second paragraph.
Denny Gault's name was not in the column.
She had the same page by email across the lot,
and what it meant was simple enough to say once.
A letter about the doctor and the roster was in his file now,
beside a first-round loss,
and the loss was what a table in February was going to weigh.
He put it in the top drawer under the depth chart from the nineteenth
and shut the drawer.
Her rule covered her calls, and a letter was not a call.
They were not going to talk about it anyway,
because the week was not for that.

Monday afternoon he ran the scout-team film from the week before
the loss, with the January roster on the marker tray by the clicker,
one printed page.
Marisol Pruett's boy took the snap for the scout team
in the third period of a December Wednesday
and got the ball out on time against a pressure
the first defense had been told to bring.
The window opened Saturday, and every name that came in through it
needed a spot.
A coach made a spot by drawing a line through a walk-on
below the scholarship block and initialing the page.
"Wes Pruett," Dan said to the empty room, the whole name, once,
and initialed the page and drew a line through nobody.
He told no one, because a walk-on's feet were not a thing
he was going to explain to anybody.

Monday night she fell asleep first, for once,
with her face against his arm.
He lay in room nine and listened to the furnace catch up with the wind,
and did not sleep for a while on purpose.
Verna's light was on across the gravel when he came and when he left,
and Verna never said a word.

Tuesday night at eight Aisha sat in his film room.
The RAV4 was at the warm end of the annex where it belonged,
a doctor's car at a doctor's building.
She had crossed the lot in the parka and come in the open door.
She had taken the chair by the wall,
out of the line of the one lit window,
without his saying a word about it.
He had the clicker in his hand and he did not shut the door.
Every door in the building stood open, his rule,
and the heat went down the hall through all of them.
She watched him watch feet for twenty minutes, and then she said it.

"You don't look at the ball. Not one time."

"Feet first. The ball goes where the feet already went."

"You look like me reading a scan," she said.
"Nothing on your face for four seconds and then all of it at once."
He turned in his chair, and her eyes were on him and not on the screen.

Down the hall the ice machine let a load go into its bin,
loud in an empty building.
She looked at the door.

"That's the rest of my staff this week," Dan said.
"He's on time, he doesn't ask for a raise,
and he's the only one in the building that's ever agreed with me."

She laughed at that, the whole one,
loud enough to go out the open door and down the hall,
and then again, at herself, for jumping.
He ran the cutup back and did not watch it.

He walked down at ten that night with her taillights long gone
ahead of him, and room nine had two chairs in it when he got there.
Neither of them had asked Verna for the second one.
Aisha sat in one and put her feet up in the other
and told him to find his own.

Wednesday at ten to seven Dan had the stool nearest the register

and on the second stool, beside him, thirty-four was eating.
Two fingers of thirty-four's left hand were taped together,
white, the annex's tape.

back for the window, on the two couches and the counter.

on a clipboard on his knee, and Ty Beaumont was on the near couch.

about somebody leaving the Mule in a coach's truck.
The board had spent a night sure the truck was Dan's
and the somebody was the doctor.
Then Earlene Tatum had counted the doors in the picture
and said Coach drove two, and the board had gone quiet.

"Ty, you get that fourth door fixed yet,
or is Earlene still counting them?"

"Same four," Ty said. "She counted them for the whole county.
Saved me the trouble."

They laughed, all of them, and Dan did too.

"Who was in the passenger seat, though, Ty. You never did say.
Was it the—"

Dan put the marker in Ty's hand before the title got out.
He took the clipboard off the coordinator's knee
and put it in the other one.

"Red zone. It's yours. Go."

The woman in the passenger seat was the admissions director,
a title in a building on the other side of campus.
He had watched a staff room do this to a woman's title once,
in another building, and had stood in the door and not stopped it.
This was his building.
This morning it stopped with a marker.

Ty set the clipboard face down on the tray
and drew the install without it,
and the room that had come in to make a morning out of a truck
went quiet and watched him.
The marker squeaked the whole way through it.
When Ty capped it the coordinator looked at the board a while.

"That's the check," he said.

Then they turned it on Dan.
A room like that had to turn it on somebody,
and the head coach was standing in the door.

"Coach never got his truck on the board," the far couch said.
"He got a coat on it, though.
The doctor's been in a staff parka since the freeze,
and there's a second coat on his hook.
That's the board's story. I'm just reading it."

The board had been right about that one,
and nobody in the room knew it.
They laughed, and Dan drank his coffee and let them,
because a man who answered that joke had heard it.
It cost him the rest of the morning.

Ty came back along the couch to his end of it,
and slowed by Dan in the door, and did not look at him.

"She called her own office in the Monday after the picture
and told them what it was before anybody could tell her."

Dan did not ask how. It was hers to have done.

"It's done, Coach," Ty said. "She did it herself.
I just drove the truck."

He went back to the end of the couch
with the marker still in his hand, and kept it.

Odell Fountain had Dan in the barber chair at Fountain's
on Fountain Row at four on Wednesday,
the cape up to his chin and his hands under it,
and nobody on the bench.
The clippers were the loudest thing in the shop,
and under them the radio on the shelf ran WDSS low.
Eleven days of beard was coming off Dan's face onto the cape,
because the county came home Friday and so did the razor.

Odell did not talk while he was on the jaw, and he was on it a while.
When he came off it and tipped the chair back for the neck,
he wiped the clippers on the towel and looked at Dan in the mirror.

"Eleven families, I hear."

"Eleven names on one letter."

"Eleven. That board of theirs has got twelve thousand people on it.
I don't read it.
Eleven of the twelve thousand wrote you a letter,
and the rest of them didn't write a thing.
Count both, Coach. The eleven and the ones that didn't.
Count both and you'll sleep."

"Yes sir."

"All right."

The clippers went back on at the neck, and Dan shut his eyes.
What he had with his eyes shut was the bed in room nine
on the twenty-sixth, after, with the lamp still on.
She had been on her side with her head on his arm.
She had asked him a question in the voice she used
when she was not going to let a thing go.

"Why do you walk it? You could park at Delmar's.
Nobody counts a truck at a barbecue place."

"Verna counts everything. And I like the walk.
Twenty-five minutes of knowing exactly where I'm going.
I don't get a lot of that."

"That's the most you've ever said about yourself."
Then she had been quiet a while, and then she said the other thing,
in the same voice, like a fact off a chart.
"I've never been married. Nobody's ever asked me.
I'd stopped waiting on it a long time before I stopped wanting it,
and I wanted you to know that before you knew anything else."

He had not had anything good enough to say to that for a while.
Then he had said, "I know something else now,"
and she had laughed into his arm,
and that was how the twenty-sixth had gone to sleep.

Nobody in this town knew that about her, and he did,
in a barber chair with the clippers at his neck.
He thought about what he was afraid of, and it was not the table.
A year on the bench had not killed him.
He was afraid that when the county came home
she would go back to saying Coach across a counter
easier than he could go back to Doc.
He was afraid he would be the one who broke, in a diner,
in front of Earlene.
He had had a week of her,
and a man did not come back from a week like this one.
He wanted her at his counter, eating off a plate he had put down.
He had not said either one to her yet.

Odell finished with the hot towel and the bay rum
and took the cape off him one-handed.
Dan paid and stood on Fountain Row in the wind
with a face she had not seen yet.

Wednesday night in room nine she put her palm flat on the shaved jaw
before he had his jacket off, and left it there, and looked.
"Odell got all of it," she said. "I'd have paid him to leave some."
The furnace ran behind, the lamp was on,
and Verna's light was on across the gravel.
It was the last night in room nine, and neither of them said that.

His kitchen at seven on New Year's Eve had the light on over the stove
and a cast-iron pan heating on the burner.
Dan stood at the counter with a dish towel in his hand, alone,
listening to the street for a car that was not going to come up it.
She would leave the RAV4 two streets over and walk,
because a doctor's car in a coach's driveway on a holiday
was a picture that needed no caption.
He had not argued. He had wanted to.

The house was as clean as he could get a house.
He had lived in it about two seasons,
a one-story rental the department had found him when he came,
and had never done one thing to it but sleep there and eat standing.
The walls had nothing on them,
no plaque, no team photo,
no framed anything from any of the places before this one.
The counter was clear. The game plan was in the truck.
The TV over the couch had a cutup paused on it
because he could not think what else to put there.
By the door the headset bag sat squared under the hook,
and the hook had the navy coat on it,
the one with nothing stitched over the patch.
He had thought about moving the coat. He had left it.

Her boots were on the front step at ten past.
He heard them before the knock, three steps up and a stamp
to get the cold off.
He had the door open on the first knock
with the towel still over his shoulder.
Aisha stood on the step in the parka with a knit hat pulled down
over her curls and her cheeks red from the walk.
She looked past him into the house before she looked at him,
and then at him.
Her face was the one from the gravel, glad first, then careful,
then glad again because there was nobody to be careful for.

"You're going to let the heat out," she said.

"Get in, then."

She came in past him, and past the hook, and stopped at it.
The parka she had on was the mate of the coat hanging there,
navy, team issue, the staff patch on the chest.
Hers had A. COLE over the patch and his had nothing.
She looked at the coat, and at him, and unzipped the parka
and handed it to him.
He hung it on the hook over its twin,
and the two coats hung there together.

"You told me the order went missing," she said.

"It did. It went missing onto that hook."

She laughed, the short one,
and touched the empty patch on his coat with two fingers,
and did not say anything else about it.
That was the coat settled, after a month of it hanging there.

She stood in the middle of his front room with her hat in her hand
and looked at all of it, the bare walls, the couch,
the film paused on the TV.
He watched her do it and could not tell what she was seeing.

"You cleaned," she said.

"I did."

"It smells like my whirlpool room."

"I used what was under the sink."
He heard how that sounded and could not take it back.
She had her lips pressed together to keep from laughing at him.
He stood in his own house with a dish towel on his shoulder
and no idea where to put his hands,
the second time she had seen that in a week.

He put two steaks in the pan,
because that was the one thing he could cook for a person besides chili.

and the smoke alarm in the hall went off before the first one had turned.
"Damn it," Dan said, and got a chair under it
and stood on it and took the battery out.

"I've never seen you do one thing wrong.
Not on a field, not on a sheet, not in a room. That was it."

"It's a bad alarm."

"It's a good alarm. It's a bad cook."
She let go of the chair and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist.
"Turn the steak, Dan."

They ate at the counter,
because the house had one stool and a chair that had just been stood on.
She took the stool and he took the chair,
which put his eyes below hers for once,
and she noticed that too and did not say it.
The glasses were two, and one of them had a price sticker
still on the bottom,

She turned hers over and read it and set it down,

Her eyes said all of it.

"Yours. I know mine."

He told her.
Monday at five he had walked up through the square in the dark
and heard her shoes on the pavement before he saw her.

*9:00. Lamp's on. Curtain's shut. Both on purpose.*
