The lid of an ice chest would not go down over the cans in it,
and a man Dan Merritt had never met was leaning on it with both hands
in the lot on the north side of the stadium at eleven in the morning,
in the sun, in a scarf,
with a plate from two states off on the truck behind him.
It was the nineteenth.
Ashford kicked off at six, under the lights,
in the first round of a playoff this county had never been in,
and Trey Gault was going to watch it in street clothes,
a student, on a printed form
with the doctor's whole name at the bottom of it.
The town ran on seven of these a year.
This one was an eighth, in a month that had never had one,
and every room from the Magnolia Court to the hotel block
had been sold on it.
Dan's own price was quieter.
The trustees met in February with his extension on the agenda,
and the word they would use for it
was going to get decided out here by half past nine tonight.

He had come through the lot on foot from the Fieldhouse
because the lot was the county's part of the day
and he had chosen, back in June, to be in the county's part of it.
The RV city was out on the fairgrounds, where it had grown overnight.
In here it was trucks and tailgates,
out-of-state plates down every row,
coats and scarves, cans in coolers,
a phone in every glove with a group chat lit on it,
and the breath of the lot going up white in the sun.

"Coach," the man said, without letting up on the lid.
"I've got a kid on the two-deep
and a wife who says there's too many in here."

"There's too many in here."

"That's what she said."

Dan put the heel of his hand on the lid beside the man's
and leaned, and the lid went down an inch and stopped on a can.
Somebody behind him laughed, short,
and it came out white in the cold,
and he knew it from a lounge on a third floor in November
before he knew where she was standing.
Aisha Cole had walked the emergency plan at eleven
before every home game since June,
the cart at the northeast tunnel,
the ambulance backed in under the north stands,
the spine board on the fence, the airway kit,
and the tailgates lay between the dock and her building,
so she came back through them.
He leaned, and the lid went the rest of the way and latched,
and the sun came off the white of it into the lot at once,
and on the far side of the table a phone was up in a glove.
Phones had been up all over the lot since the gates opened
for the Playoff Album thread on the parents' board.
She went past behind him with the tablet under her arm,
the parka's cuffs pinned flat at both wrists,
close enough that the cold came off her,
and it had the dock in it, diesel and concrete,
and he kept his hand flat on the lid until she was by.
That was all of her he was going to get before six.
It was more than the terms had room for, and he took it.

"Coach, you want one at ten tonight? I'll keep it cold."

"Keep it shut till kickoff," he told the man,
and went on toward the staff gate.

Sonny Dillard had the staff gate in two coats with the clipboard.
"North lot's froze on the shade side, Coach. I told them.
Nobody listens to the feed store."

"Sonny," Dan said, and Sonny wrote him down.

***

At six the kick went up under the lights,
and Dan stood at the forty with the script cards in one hand
and the headset around his neck,
and Aisha stood at her north edge of the bench,
forty yards up the sideline,
with the trainer beside her and the gray tent behind her,
and neither of them looked down the sideline at the other.
That was going to be the night, and he knew it at the kick.

The bowl was full to the upper deck and had its coats on.
Ninety thousand people had come to watch one boy play football
in December, and he was standing behind the bench
in a gray hoodie with the hood up and a coat over it,
a student, a shape,
and Dan did not say a word to him all night, and did not need to.
Up in the glass of the suite over the fifty the lights were on
and the people who had paid for a rendering stood in their coats,
and one shape among them stood with its hands behind its back
and did not move for the first quarter,
and Dan knew which one was Rex Boyd without a face to go by,
which was all the suite's glass gave him and all he wanted of it.

The visitors came in white,
and the first quarter went like most first quarters in the cold,
punts, a field goal for them, the county stamping its feet.
The backup took the snaps.
The kid had gotten one live rep a day behind Trey since August
and a week of all of them since Tuesday,
and in the second quarter he put a ball on a post route
that a boy with one week of first-team reps
had no business putting there,
and the bowl came up out of its seats and stayed up.
Seven, three.
The kid came off and Dan tapped the front of his helmet
with the cards and said nothing,
because nothing was what the kid could carry right then.

At the half it was ten, seven, theirs,
and Dan went up the tunnel behind the team
without turning his head toward the north edge of the bench,
and the not turning was a thing he did with his neck on purpose,
and he did it again coming back out.
Nobody had gone down, and nobody had needed her.
Forty yards was the distance the terms had put on it,
two days old,
with ninety thousand people who would have watched him walk it,
and he did not walk one yard of it.

The third quarter was long and theirs.
Seventeen, seven, and then the kid brought Ashford
back down the field in eleven plays, slow,
none of them pretty, all of them in bounds,
and handed the ball to a back at the two,
and it was seventeen, fourteen, with a quarter to play.
He had known where the north edge of that bench was
from every yard of the sideline all night
without turning his head,
and nobody in the ninety thousand
was ever going to hear that from him.

Then the visitors kicked a field goal with nine minutes left,
twenty, seventeen,
and Dan spent the timeouts.
All three, on defense, to stop the clock and get the ball back,
because a kid with one week of first-team snaps
could not be asked to drive a field with no clock to help him,
and Dan was going to ask him anyway.

The county found her name in the fourth quarter.
It came off the chain-link behind the bench,
one voice, not loud, meant to carry the ten yards it carried.
"Doctor's got a healthy boy in a sweatshirt back there,
and we're down three."
Nobody answered it, and nobody had to.
It was the county's sentence by then,
and Dan squared the cards on his palm, twice,
and did not turn around,
because there was nothing back there a coach could say
that the county would not have found a use for by morning.

He looked once.
Late, with four minutes on the clock and the ball theirs,
he looked up the sideline forty yards to the north edge,
and what he could read of her at forty yards
was a fact and not a face.
She was standing. She had not sat all night.
The tape on her left wrist showed white under the lights
and had nothing written on it that he could see from there,
the cuffs were still pinned,
and the trainer beside her was watching the field, and she was not.
She was watching the bench.
She watched people and not the ball; he had known that from the first sheet.
He put his eyes back on the field and kept them there.

The defense got the ball back with a minute and forty
and no timeouts, on Ashford's own twenty-five,
and the kid took it.
He hit a slant. He hit a back out of the flat.
He took a sack and got up and threw the next one away,
and hit the same slant again,
and they were across midfield at the forty-four
with thirty-one seconds.
Then he hit a curl to the thirty-nine, in bounds,
and the receiver went down in bounds with a man on him,
and the clock ran.
Twenty seconds. The kid got them to the line.
Fourteen, the video board said.
The thirty-nine in a December wind was a fifty-six-yard kick,
and Dan's kicker had never made one, and the sideline knew it,
and one more first down was a kick anybody could believe in.

"Spike it and kick, or throw it," the coordinator said in his ear.
"It's yours."

"Throw it," Dan said, and sent the play in.
The kid took the snap and threw it to the sideline,
to the thirty-two, a first down, in bounds,
and the receiver was tackled in bounds with a man on his legs,
and the clock ran, four, two, and did not stop.

"Shit," Dan said, to nobody,
with the headset down around his neck.

The press box said the final and thanked everybody for coming.
The handshake line went by in gloves.
He did not look up the sideline at the whistle.
Everybody in the bowl looked at the bench then,
at the boy in the hoodie,
and Dan looked at the kid in front of him and walked.
He walked him in with one hand on the back of his neck
and did not say a word to him the whole tunnel,
because the kid had thrown it to the thirty-two
like a man being asked to,
and Dan had asked him to.
Trey went in with them, hood up, a shape in the line of shapes.
Up in the suite over the fifty the lights stayed on
and the people in it were putting their coats on,
and down at the mouth of the northeast tunnel
a man in a university pullover
was walking a microphone back up
with the cable wound around his hand.
The athletic director stood at the tunnel mouth
with his hands in his coat and said, "Monday,"
and Dan said, "Monday."

***

At twenty past ten Dan came out of the tunnel mouth into the cold
with his cap in his hand and the sideline jacket zipped to the neck,
and the county was still coming down out of the bowl on every stair.
He had given the TV truck's camera four sentences at the tunnel
with the kid in every one of them and the doctor in none.
From the stadium to the Fieldhouse was the north lot
and the low end of Millrow,
and the county was on both of them, going the two ways it went.
The locals went up Millrow on foot toward the square,
past the annex, past the Fieldhouse, past Delmar's,
and the parents from out of state went the other way,
to the lots and the hotel block,
with coolers on wheels and kids asleep on shoulders,
and both halves of it were talking.
Not loud.
The county went up Millrow conversational,
at the volume of a Tuesday,
and Dan had walked out of stadiums in towns that yelled,
and yelling meant the town was still arguing.
This one had finished. That was worse.

Missy and Denny Gault were at their car at the edge of the north lot,
Missy with the door open and the notebook against her coat,
Denny with the keys in his hand and nothing to say,
and Missy lifted a hand and said, "Coach," and Dan lifted his,
and Denny looked at him and put the key in the door.

"Boy was standing right there in a hoodie all night," a man said,
near enough, to the woman he was walking with.
"Healthy as I am. All it took was one signature, and she sat on it."
Dan did not turn to see who.
He could have turned around and said what a night held
and what a step was,
and that the boy was healthy because she had made healthy mean something,
and he had said none of it to a camera and said none of it now,
because the county did not want it from him
and she had not asked him for it.
Go out in the first round with Trey sitting and it's a review.
His own sentence, from a cold room with a box in his arms,
and on Monday the athletic director would say it back to him.

A woman ahead of him on Millrow had her phone out in her glove
with the speaker on, and it was WDSS,
and Tick Moran was saying the score.
He said it in the flat voice he saved
for weather that could kill you and for final scores,
twenty to seventeen, visitors, and he did not dress it,
and then he came off the flat.
"That's the number, friends, and there's not a thing I can do to it.
Win or lose, this county walks home together.
Always has. Good night, now."
The woman put the phone in her coat with the speaker still going,
and Tick played a song into her pocket,
and she went on up the hill.

***

At a quarter to eleven Dan stood in the film room at the Fieldhouse
with the light switch under his hand and the room lit behind him.
The poles over the practice field had not come on today.
There had been no practice under them on a Saturday,
and what had lit the lot between his window and the annex all night
was the stadium, the banks of it,
throwing light over the north stands onto the pavement and her door.
The banks went off one at a time as the lot emptied,
and when the last one went
the lot was as dark as it was going to get,
and he killed his own light and stood in the dark a while
so she would see the window go, and then he crossed.

The warm-end door was unlocked,
and Aisha was at her desk with the heater going
and the parka on the back of her chair,
and Dan came in with the cap in his hand.
A can of seltzer stood at her right hand with the tab up.
Her curls were down, all the way, with the wind still in them,
and the lanyard had left a line on the side of her neck
where it had hung since eleven.
She reached past him and shut the door.

"The stadium took its time going off," she said.
"I thought you'd gone up to the staff room."

"I stood in the window with the light on
so I'd have something to turn off."

She opened the desk drawer and set a second can in front of him,
and he turned it once on the desk by the top and did not open it.

"Drink that or don't," she said. "It's the last one in the drawer.
Sit down, Dan, before you fall down in my office."

He sat. She looked at him across the desk
as she had never looked at him across a sheet,
with her chin down and her eyes up, and waited.

"I had forty yards of sideline and a clock on my wrist," she said,
"and I watched that boy clap for the ball at fourteen seconds
like he'd had the first team since August."

"He had one week of it."

"He didn't play like one week. That drive."

"The kid put it in bounds," he said.
"I sent that play in.
I could have let him spike it
and kicked from fifty-five into that wind
with a kid who has never made one.
I threw it to the thirty-two and the clock ran.
That's mine.
It'll be the county's by Monday, but it's mine."

"I watched you walk him in."
Her eyes had not moved off him.
"You had your hand on the back of his neck from the field to the locker room
and you didn't say one word to him.
I've watched you talk to every person in this county all fall.
Where does a man learn to walk somebody in and not talk."

He turned the can a quarter turn on the desk.

"I left coaching and did relief work overseas."

She looked at him through it and past it and did not blink,
and she did not ask him where, or when, or for whom,
and her face did not go anywhere,
and the not asking was the thing she gave him for it.
She put her thumbnail under the edge of the tape on her left wrist
and peeled it off, four quarters of it, blank,
and dropped it in the wastebasket by the desk.

"Nobody went down tonight," she said. "Not one.
Four quarters and nothing on it.
I stood there the whole game for—"

She stopped, and looked at the wastebasket, and let it lie there.
He let it lie there too.

"Nobody went down," he said. "I'll take that one."

"Take it. It's the only clean line anybody wrote tonight."

Something went across her mouth at that, not a smile, near one.
She got up and came around the desk
and put her thumb along his jaw, the smooth side of it,
where Thursday there had been five days.

"Shaved," she said.

"For the cameras."

"For the cameras," she said,
and he kissed her once with the can still in his other hand,
and that was the ceiling, and they both knew where it was.

"Monday the poles come back on at six," she said,
"and I'll be at this desk when they go off."

"Monday I'm in that window at six, Aisha,
and there's not a thing the athletic director can say to me
between now and then that moves it."

He left the can on her desk where she had put it,
with the tab still down,
and went out by the warm-end door he had come in by.

The equipment cage at the Fieldhouse was empty at midnight
except for Dan and the ball in his two hands,
and Friday's sheet, Aisha's, rode in the jacket's inside pocket
with the rest of the week's,
the one that said *Step four. Holds overnight.*,
and he had not taken it out to look at it and was not going to.
The equipment manager had gone for the night
and left the last load turning in the dryers.
The cage had the detergent in it and the dryers' heat,
and under the dryers the radio on the counter was on 1340
with nothing on it but a song, Tick gone off the air.
The ball had come in off a manager's cart with the rest of them,
belonging to nobody, because nobody keeps a loss ball,
and Dan had taken it off the cart on his way past.
He took the marker out of the cup on the counter.
He wrote the backup's name on the ball, under the laces,
the name he had typed onto a first-team line
at six on a Tuesday in the dark,
and the kid was never going to see it,
and he capped the marker and put it back in the cup.
He had lost in a lot of counties and had never put a name on a ball.
He set it on the shelf beside the box of heat packs
with the name turned to the wall, and left it there.
