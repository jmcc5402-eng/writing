The cook came down the counter with Dan's order on a plate, the Coach's Slice,
and set it at Aisha's elbow unasked.
She had not ordered it.
By the time her fork came up from her eggs, the cook was at the griddle again,
with his back to the room.
Her face went warm over the eggs anyway, from the griddle or from the plate.
It was Tuesday the twenty-sixth of January, the sixth day of her leave,
eight days before the review said in writing
whether she had been right about Trey,
and the cook had just said more to her with a plate
than anybody on the square had said out loud since Wednesday.
At the window table Earlene Tatum had the pot.

It was half past seven.
She had walked the three streets over from the Magnolia Court
in her own wool coat, and the wind had come through it the whole way.
The team's parka with the staff patch was on the rod in nine,
because a doctor on leave from the staff did not wear it to breakfast.
Outside, the last of the night's rain came off the awning, fine and cold.
The radio on the shelf over the pass-through was turned low under the griddle,
and on the half hour 1340 read the morning news.

[TK the Checkerboard's radio: on no page; one clause; record at the fold]

[TK who reads 1340's morning news: on no page]

She had the point of the pie on her fork, halfway up,
when the radio said his name.

"Sources close to the independent review tell 1340
that the review called head coach Dan Merritt in on Monday.
Asked whether, knowing what he knows now,
he would have played his quarterback in the playoff on the nineteenth,
Coach Merritt told the review, and we're quoting him,
'Every coach in the country plays him. I'd have played him.'
The athletic department answered the report at seven this morning.
Its statement, in full.
'Coach Merritt has full confidence in the independent review process.'"

The fork stopped where it was.
Her hand put it down on the plate, and she made sure there was no sound.
The heat came up out of her collar and filled her face to the ears.
Her throat shut on a swallow of coffee, and she got it down hard,
like something with corners.
Her right hand went around the cup and held on,
and the heat of the cup went into her palm,
and she let it, because it was a thing she could hold.
Under the counter her knees had locked.
Her breath had gone up high in her chest and would not come down.

*I'd have played him.*
On Monday Dan had sat across a table from the review
and said it about Trey, and saying it about Trey was saying it about her.
She had held Trey on fourteen minutes and a hand going up under a hood,
and Dan had folded the sheet that said so into his jacket in December.
On the twentieth the department had promised one statement and no other.
This was a second, with Dan's name on it and nothing in it
to take his sentence back, and it did not say her name.
Dan had let it go out that way.

*I knew it.*
She had known it the whole time, under everything,
since the first night she let him in through her door.
Nothing was ever really going to happen for her.
Not here, and not with him.
She had let one man close, one,
and the first time standing beside her had cost him anything,
he had gone and stood on the far side of her with the county listening.
A man had decided what she was worth when it counted, once again,
and it had come to less than one sentence.

She was angry, and not the cold kind she kept for a sideline.
It was hot, and it was in her hands.
She pushed the plate a half inch away with one finger and made herself stop.

Twelve days ago, on this same station, he had said her protocol.
It was the Thursday of the ice, with the county home and the radio on,
and she had sat on the end of the bed in nine
and heard him say it to every kitchen in the county at once.
She had kept those two words for twelve days like a thing in a coat pocket.
This morning the station had his other sentence,
and the county would keep that one.

On Wednesday Dan had stood at her car window in the rain
with his hand flat on her door and told her film was at four,
and she had not asked him for one word more.
He had not had a sentence for her at that window.
He had found one for the review.

She still wanted him, and that was the worst of it.
She wanted his wet face four inches from hers and his hand on her door,
and she was sitting at a counter wanting a man
who had just told the county she was wrong.
It went through her chest hot and low, under the anger,
and she could not stop it.
She kept her face down and did not let the room have any of it.

A minute later a man two stools down said it to the man beside him, not loud.
He was a counter man in a work jacket, and not one of the Table.

"Every other doctor in this league would've had that kid dressed
for the playoff. We lost it by three with the backup.
I'm not saying she's a bad doctor.
I'm saying the coach just told everybody he'd have played him, and he'd know."

"She's two stools down, bud," the other man said.

"I know where she's sitting.
I'm not saying it one bit louder than I'd say it to her face."

He was not wrong about the three.
She did not answer him.
Her face went hot again, and she held it still over the plate.
Her hand stayed around the cup and the heat went on into her palm,
and she looked at the pie with its point cut off
and did not pick the fork back up.

The cook had heard it at seven, and that was why the plate.
The man in the work jacket had said what half the county thought,
and the cook had answered him before he said it.
Both of them were the town.

Earlene came up the counter with the pot she walked for the Table and Dan.
Aisha had always carried her own cup to the urn.
Earlene filled it to the top without a word,
looked at her once, straight, and went back to the window.
The cup was hot in both of Aisha's hands, and her breath came down at last.
When Aisha stood to go, the cook slid her ten back across the counter
with two fingers, and nobody took the pie away.


By nine Aisha had walked the three streets back to room nine
and sat on the bed with the tablet, the furnace running behind the north wind.

The letter had come while she was at the counter,
an email from the chair of a department out west with a letter attached.
She read it once, top to bottom, as she read a scan.

[TK the university and its chair: unnamed; how the chair first reached
her after ch 19; on no page]

*I am pleased to offer you the directorship of the department.*
*We would be grateful for your answer by Saturday, the sixth of February.*

The word went into her low, under the breastbone,
and it sat there warm for as long as it took her to read the letter again,
while her hands went still on the tablet.
A department was a place that kept a person.
Somebody out west had asked for her in writing, by name, on purpose,
and wanted the answer in eleven days.
Her breath went out of her slow.

Rex Boyd had passed her name along so that she would go.
The ground he wanted was the annex,
the building she had been asked to leave by noon on the twentieth,
and his glass building went where her clinic stood.
The group that rode in with it took her job at the same February table.
It was a good offer anyway, and it was real.

The phone was in her hand before she had decided anything,
with Dan's name at the top,
and her heart came up into her throat and beat there.
She wanted to say a building out west wants me by Saturday,
and hear what his breath did before he answered.
Her chest ached with it, low and hard, where the word had gone warm.

She did not call him.
Dan had chosen the job at her car window on Wednesday, and she had let him.
This morning Dan had told the county he would have played Trey.
A doctor on leave who called the head coach eight days before the findings
handed the county its caption, whatever she said on the call.

If a coach and his team physician were caught in a relationship in season,
they were both gone that same day,
and nobody would stop to ask which one of them had dialed.
The terms had eight days left in them
and had already cost Dan a sentence at her window.
He worked fourteen hours a day, eleven months a year,
at the job he had picked for himself,
and she was not going to be the call that took it away from him.
She put the phone down on the blanket, screen up,
and took her hand off it one finger at a time.


At one she drove up Millrow to the annex,
with the sun come through thin and the hill drying in stripes,
and let herself in at the warm end with the key on her own ring.
The department had asked her out by noon on the twentieth.
Nobody had asked for the key.

The man covering her clinic got up from the desk in the whirlpool room.
He wanted no trouble from anybody, her least of all.

"Dr. Cole. Nobody told me you'd be up today."

"I've come for my own things, and nothing else. I'll be an hour."

"Take what's yours." He sat back down. "I'll be right here."

Behind him the trainer stood in the door to the rehab rooms
with a roll of tape and did not come out,
because the door was as far toward her as he could come
and still tape ankles for Dan's team.
He said "Doc," and she nodded, and that was all either of them had.

The clinic's boxes stood along her office wall, numbered in her marker,
and the last one in his. Those stayed.
She put her palm flat on his number going by
and kept it there longer than it needed.
Her heart went hard under the wool coat, and she took the hand away.
What was hers went into two boxes,
the diploma box still taped from the move
and the second boots and the charger and the lamp off the desk.

[TK the office lamp: hers or the clinic's; on no page]

The mug had gone down the hill on the twentieth,
but its ring was still on the desk, and it did not come up under her wet thumb.
Some nights, with the lot dark and the building empty,
Dan had stood on the other side of that desk with his coat still zipped
and said nothing he did not have to.
Six months of hers fit in two boxes
and the back of the car she had driven into this county.

Trey Gault's paper chart was in the shut drawer,
with the form from December inside and her whole name at the bottom.
She put her hand flat on the folder.
It was the clinic's paper now and the review's,
and the scouts' file on her tablet was the only part of it that was hers.
It cost her something in the wrist to shut the drawer on it.
The man covering her clinic had come as far as her door,
and he saw her do it and went back up the corridor.

She counted twelve of Verna's fourteen towels off the whirlpool room's shelf
with the trainer watching, and left the two that had always been the annex's.
She carried the boxes and the towels out to the RAV4.
Across the lot the survey's orange flags stood along the fence line in the wet,
and at the Fieldhouse Dan's truck was nose to the fence,
where it always was by one in the afternoon.
The sight of it went into her chest and sat there, hot,
half the want and half this morning.
Her breath came short in the cold, and she did not cross to it.


At three she drove across town to Mt. Nebo Missionary Baptist.
The forty-one boxes from the records room had stood there behind one key
since December, and her copy of the count had to match Delores's pad
before the findings, because the review would want to know
those boxes had never been out of anybody's hands.
Verna had made the call for her,
and Delores had the hall that afternoon anyway.

The church was setting its February program.
The agenda on the pass-through said it was a business meeting
on what the church had lost at Canaan Bottom,
the neighborhood by the river the town condemned in 1968 for stadium parking.

Delores Fountain-Mabry unlocked the door with the key on its ribbon.
She wanted the town's numbers in the town's own hand.
She read her legal pad and Aisha read the tablet, number for number,
to forty-one, and every number matched.

Dan had carried box one through that door in December,
saying every number out loud as he passed Delores.
Aisha could hear him saying thirty-one, low and patient,
and it went into her chest before she could get the tablet up against it.

Then Delores locked the door and looked at Aisha instead of the pad.

"I had the radio on this morning, Doctor.
A man can say what he'd have done.
You kept that boy off a field with his head hurt,
and you were right, and I'll tell it to anybody who asks me."

Nobody had said it to her out loud since Wednesday.
Her throat went tight,
and she looked at the key on its ribbon until her eyes quit stinging.

"Yes ma'am," Aisha said.

Odell Fountain came in through the kitchen door at a quarter past,
late from the shop, with hair on his sleeve
and a stack of fifth-grade essays in a rubber band under his arm.
He cut hair on Fountain Row and was a deacon here,
and the essays were his to judge.

"I've got a man in my chair with half a haircut, Delores.
Whatever I'm signing, point at it, and nobody read me an essay."

Delores pointed, and Aisha went out while he signed.


By four she was back at the Magnolia Court with the twelve towels,
and the two boxes stayed in the back of the RAV4.
The sun was out and it had got up to forty,
so Sonny Dillard, who did Verna's repairs,
was on his knees at her cracked office step with the quick-set at last.

She washed the twelve in Verna's washers and dried them,
and folded them in thirds on the table by the dryer, the way Verna's came,
while the sun went down the gravel toward the road.
She had not lost one.

While the dryer ran she looked at the gravel
where Dan's truck had sat the night of the ice with the engine running.
She wanted it there again, and her hand over his on the seat,
and she felt her pulse in the wrist his palm had closed under.
Heat came up her neck at it, standing in Verna's door.
She could not have it.
The terms stood between her and that truck, and now so did the radio.

Verna Poteat came out when the dryer quit.
Verna wanted two things from room nine,
the rent on the first and the doctor sleeping in it.
She looked at the corners and the thirds.

"Mm," Verna said.

Aisha held out February's rent in an envelope.
Verna weighed it in her hand without opening it
and looked past her at the boxes in the back of the RAV4.
Then she put it back in Aisha's hand and closed Aisha's fingers on it.

"Furnace in nine ran behind all January," Verna said.

Aisha heard the furnace in it and nothing else,
and she let Verna square that account.
Sonny had seen all of it from the step.
He went on working the trowel,
and Verna went back in with the towels against her chest.


At nine that night Aisha sat on the bed in room nine
with a can of sparkling water open on the sill.
The wind had gone north after dark, and the sky was clear and cold.

Kat's text came at five past.
Kat was her friend from up the road, and she wanted Aisha to stay.

*Honey, the farmhouse with the bad roof dropped its price again.*
*Friday I'm bringing the wine and a ladder.*

Aisha laughed once, out loud, and it hurt in her chest,
and she did not answer.

She took the wool coat off the rod and unbuttoned the inside breast pocket,
where the letter had ridden against her ribs all day.
The envelope was small and gone the color of tea,
*For the night nurse* on the front and Cordelia Hartwell on the flap,
Hartwell like the House.
She read it again now on the bed where that woman had slept,
with her thumb holding the old fold flat so the sheet would lie open.
Most of it was a woman telling the night nurse
she had been needed in that building.

*You keep asking what I stayed for, as if a town were a reason.
It was never the town. It was being needed by it on purpose.*

She read the last line twice with her thumb under it,
and her breath shook going out.
Somebody out west had asked for her on purpose, in writing, with a date.
This county had set down a pie and handed back a month's rent
and said one kind word,
and not one person in it had said the one word out loud
that would have kept her here.
The one man who might have said it had told the county this morning
that he would have played Trey.

So she was going.
The brave thing was to stay with no word from anybody and ask for one.
The thing she had wanted her whole life was a place that asked for her first.
They were not the same thing.
Only one of them cost Dan nothing, and paying had always been hers to do.

She typed the yes on the tablet, four lines,
and saved it to send on Saturday, as the chair had asked.
She had chosen. The sending was only a date.

Then the phone was in her hand again with his name at the top,
and her heart went hard enough to feel in her thumb on the glass.
She put it down on the blanket and left it there.

She took the parka off the rod last.
It was the team's coat, and it went back to the equipment cage before she did,
where she had tried to send it back the day it turned up on her office chair.
She folded it flat on the bed, and the nylon went warm under her hands.
Her arm had been in this sleeve against his,
in his cab on the night of the ice,
with the trainer a foot away on the door side
and the dash light up under Dan's jaw.
The warm went down her left side from the shoulder to the hip,
where the bench had put him.
Her heart came up into her throat and beat there.
Her breath went short, and her hands pressed flat on the nylon to keep still.
Her face went hot with nobody there to see it.
She was angry with him, and she wanted his arm along her side,
and both were true at once.
That frightened her more than the radio had.
She turned the folded coat so the name was up.

She packed the two suitcases, and tonight she did not time it.
She carried them out to the RAV4, set them beside the boxes,
and laid the parka flat on the top box.
A car with boxes in it, in Verna's gravel,
was a thing the county could read by morning.

Across the gravel Verna's storm door opened,
and Verna stood in it with the office light behind her.
Aisha went across to her step.

"I've watched every car that ever got loaded in that gravel
since the sign went up, my winter doctor," Verna said.
"The quick ones never had anybody asking them to stay.
I rent rooms. I don't ask a soul to stay in one,
and I'm too old to start tonight."

"It sits here till Saturday, Verna," Aisha said.
"The county can read it all it wants till then."

Verna looked at the car a long while
and did not say the other thing, and neither did Aisha.

Aisha stood beside her with her hands in the wool coat's pockets
and thought about Dan.
He did not know yet, and he would hear it from somebody who was not her.
What she was afraid of was that he would come down that hill after all and ask,
and it would be Saturday, and she would already have gone.

The light from Verna's door went out across the gravel
and lay on the RAV4's back glass.
Through the glass lay the coat she had worn beside Dan all season,
folded flat on the top box,
with A. COLE up in block letters over the staff patch.
