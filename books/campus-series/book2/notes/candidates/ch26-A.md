The ceiling vent knocked once and started to blow,
and Dan Merritt lost the front of the question under the sound of the air.
The independent review had sent for him to say whether the doctor
had been right to hold Trey Gault out of the playoff in December,
and he sat across a table from it with the university's lawyer at his elbow.
This was Monday the twenty-fifth, the day before the radio had any of it.
By Tuesday morning the whole county would have the next two sentences he said.

[TK who conducts the review; the room's building]

The room had a long table and a window with the blind half down.
For forty minutes Dan had given the short answers
the lawyer asked for on the stairs,
and heard himself give them from a long way off,
like a man on a phone in another state.

His head was not at the table.
It was in the Fieldhouse lot last Wednesday, in the rain,
with four inches of Aisha's face through the glass of her car.
He kept hearing her window go up.
It had gone up with a thin whine from the motor in the door,
and her face had gone behind the water on the glass,
and he had stood there in the ruts and let it go.
His chest had been tight since Wednesday, like a hand pressed flat on it.
That morning he had reached for the truck keys on the hook by his front door,
and the navy coat had been hanging there under them,
the kept one, the other one of the pair.

"Coach?" The reviewer had stopped writing.

"Sorry," Dan said. "Again."

"Knowing what you know now,
would you have played your quarterback on the nineteenth?"

His palm was flat on the table, and he took it off.
It was a fair question, asked fairly,
and he did not hear the trap folded up inside it.
He heard a coach being asked about a game,
and he answered as a coach, before he had thought at all.

"Every coach in the country plays him," Dan said. "I'd have played him."

He had more.
The rest of the sentence was right behind the first half,
and it was the half that mattered, and his breath was already in for it.
Then the vent shut off, and in the sudden quiet
he heard the reviewer's pen going across the page.
Dan was looking at the blind and seeing her face go behind wet glass.
The pen stopped.

"All right," the reviewer said, and turned the page.

He did not go back for the rest of the sentence,
and nobody at the table asked him for it.

In the hall the lawyer walked him toward the stairs.

"That was fine," the lawyer said. "Short and true."

It did not feel fine.
Something had gone wrong at that table,
and Dan's stomach had it before his head did,
so he went back over the forty minutes step by step
and could not find the place.
His neck was hot under his collar.

"The hearing is Monday the first," the lawyer said.
"The review sits all day and hears the people on its list.
Findings on the third.
You're not on the list, and that's the best news you'll get this week.
Don't go. Don't call anybody who's on it. Don't call her."

"And if I've got something it ought to hear?"

"Then you tell me, and I tell you no."


On Thursday the twenty-eighth the athletic director shut his office door
himself with Dan in the chair across the desk,
and the radiator under the window started to tick as it came up to heat.
The athletic director wanted a quiet February.

"Two things, Dan," he said. "Then you can get back to your class.
The partner comes in July. The group.
Whatever the review finds on the third, cleared or not,
team physician is theirs in July.
So tell me why I spend February defending a line that's gone by July."

Dan knew whose sentence it was.
Nobody said a name, and nobody had to.
It was a real argument, and that was the worst of it.
A careful man could make it with a straight face,
and this one had liked Aisha's work.

"The second thing," the athletic director said.
"A department out west called me this week about her. A reference call.
They've offered her a directorship,
and they want her answer by Saturday the sixth.
I told them the truth. She's the best physician I've had in this job."

The radiator ticked, and Dan heard every tick of it.
His chest pulled tight, a strap taken up one hole too far, and stayed there.
His throat had shut on whatever he had been going to say,
and his breath went out slow through his nose.
His palm was cold on the arm of the chair in a warm room.
He was hearing it from his boss.
She had not called him, and he knew why.
He had chosen the job through four inches of glass,
and she had watched him choose it.
He did not know her answer, only what he was afraid it was.

He could not go to her about it.
The lawyer's rule was that anything he said about Aisha made it worse for her,
and the terms they had made in December still had six days to run,
to signing day on Wednesday.
If the school could prove a coach and his team physician together
before the class closed, it fired them both the same day.

Men waited whole careers for a head job at this level,
and most of them never got the phone call.
He had got it, and he had to win it again every week of the year
against the next man who wanted it.
It was the one thing he had ever wanted as much as he wanted her.
Every man on his staff had been hired on his contract.
If the trustees let his extension go in February,
the next head coach would bring in his own staff,
and Ty's truck payment would come due on a job that Ty no longer had.

[TK the western school; the chair — unnamed]

At eleven a freshman came down wrong off a box in the weight room,
and Dan walked him across the lot to the annex himself,
because the kid was eighteen and scared of the word ankle in January.
The man the department had sent to cover Aisha's clinic
stood in the whirlpool room's door with a phone at his ear.
The tank was running behind him, loud as an engine idling in a shut garage.
The trainer had the freshman on the far table with the shoe off
and the knee bent.

"Whit, is it broke?" the freshman said.

"It's rolled. You'd know broke. You'd be louder."

His name was Carl Whitlow, and the staff called him Whit.
The tape came off the roll in long tearing pulls.

"I've got a lab at one," the freshman said. "Chem. If I miss it I'm done."

"You'll make it on a crutch." Whit tore the strip off clean.
"My girl's a sophomore here. She says that lab takes roll, and she'd know.
Her spring bill's on my kitchen table right now."

All fall Dan had seen a pair of hands in her doorway.
Now he saw a man with a daughter somewhere across campus,
who wanted her through this school and wanted to keep the job that paid for it,
and wanted nobody looking hard at either one.

Dan left the freshman to him and went out the warm-end door by her office.
The RAV4 had nosed up to that door every weekday of the fall.
He could see her on its step in the parka with the tablet under her arm,
not looking up.
He stood on the step a second longer than the cold wanted.
Then he saw the stakes.

The orange flags last week had been wire and plastic,
pushed into the wet ground by hand.
These were survey stakes, wooden, with their tops painted orange,
driven in hard enough to crack the frozen crust.
They ran in a line from the near corner of the annex toward the fence,
and the near corner was the whirlpool room,
where he could hear the tank running through the wall at his back.
The flags had marked the ground. The stakes marked where the building went.
A strip of orange tape on each one ticked in the wind,
and the cold went down the back of his neck and stayed.
When those stakes had walls on them, her clinic would be under the first one.
The trustees would vote the building and her job at the same February sitting,
and the group that came with the money would be doing her work by July.


At three Dan was in Odell Fountain's barber chair on Fountain Row.
Signing day was Wednesday, and the class would stand for its picture
with Dan in the middle,
and a head coach did not stand in the middle of a class
with the hair on his neck gone to seed.
The clippers buzzed at his ear, and the chair's lever sighed under Odell's foot,
and the shop smelled of talc.
A man in a highway department vest sat on the bench under the window,
waiting his turn.

"That doctor was right about 7," the man said.
"I'd hold my own boy out for a lot less than that."

Dan said nothing back.
The man looked at him in the mirror and waited,
and Dan gave the mirror nothing.
Heat came up the back of his neck under Odell's hand,
and his throat shut on the answer.

He wished Aisha had been on that bench to hear it,
and he knew the voice she needed on her side was his,
and it was sitting under a barber's cape saying nothing.

Odell switched the clippers off,
and the shop went quiet enough to hear the space heater.

"I've had nine heads in this chair since that radio Tuesday, Coach,"
Odell said. "All nine could say your part back to me word for word.
I asked every one what the doctor said.
Not one of them could tell me, because she hasn't said a word, anywhere.
One of you's been on that radio all week, and it wasn't her."
He switched the clippers back on. "Head down."

Dan put his head down.


From the shop Dan went back up the hill to his desk until dark.
At seven he was in the guest chair on Delmar's screen porch,
with the heaters humming orange and Tick Moran's mixer humming under them.
Tick had called a Table for signing week,
the third ever held out of a season, because the class signed on Wednesday.
The lawyer had cleared Dan for it
to talk football and the class and nothing else.
The station's tone came down the line,
and the bulb over the card table went on,
and the first two callers wanted to know about Wednesday,
and Dan told them everything he was allowed to.

"Coach, before the next line." It was the voice Tick used for the weather.
"Tuesday morning this station read a line of yours about 7.
It's your line. The air's yours too, if you've got something to put with it."

The porch went quiet.
Dan could hear every heater on it
and the smoker's lid coming down at the yard end.
On the fourteenth he had sat in this same guest chair
and said *her protocol* to every radio in the county.
He heard it again now under the heaters, in his own voice,
and under it Tuesday's voice saying *I'd have played him*.
The whole county could hear the gap between them as well as he could.
His throat was dry, and his heart was going slow and hard.

The lawyer had said a coach correcting himself on the air
was a coach talking about Aisha,
and anything he said about her made it worse for her,
and the lawyer was right.
So he let it stand.
He wanted to say her name into that microphone
so badly that his jaw ached with it.

"I'm here about the class, Tick," Dan said.
"Wednesday's a big day for a lot of families."

"Then we'll talk about the class," Tick said, and put up the next line.
There was no opinion in Tick's voice at all.
The Table ran its hour, and at eight the tone came down again.

When the Table was done he sat in the truck at the corner of the square
with the engine off, and at a quarter to nine
he drove to the Magnolia Court anyway.
The gravel talked under his tires the whole length of Verna's lot.
At the office end Verna's blind came up an inch and went down again.
He left the truck by the office and not in front of nine.
A truck with two doors in this gravel after dark had been a post once already.

The RAV4 stood in front of nine with its back seat full to the windows.
Verna's office light came through the side glass and lay across the top box,
on a navy coat folded flat with A. COLE up in block letters
over the staff patch.
Aisha had folded it to go back to the equipment cage.
A woman who meant to stay did not fold that coat.
This morning he had not known her answer, and now he did.
His stomach went down and did not come back up.
His breath went white in front of him and hung there.

The curtain on nine was open,
and the lamp inside showed the whole lot a room with the rack bare
and the bed made tight.
The lamp on and the curtain shut had been her word to him since New Year's.
This was a room anybody could look into,
and she had left it that way on purpose.

The door of nine opened before he had his own door shut,
and Aisha stood in it in a sweater with her arms crossed,
the lamp behind her and her face out in the cold.
She had heard the gravel.
Her eyes were dry and steady, and they were furious.
His heart was going before she said a word.

"You shouldn't be in this gravel, Dan," she said.

"I know it."

A man on her step in the lamplight was worse than a man inside it,
and they both knew it,
so she stepped back and let him in and shut the door on the gravel.
Then she crossed the room and pulled the curtain across the window.
That was for the lot and not for him, and she did not look at the lamp.
The furnace was running under the window and not keeping up.

"Doc." His voice came out low. "Monday, at the review. When they asked me—"

"Don't."
It came fast, and her chin came up with it.
"Whatever the rest of that is, it isn't mine.
The county has the half somebody wrote down.
If there's another half, it goes to the people who wrote down the first one.
Said to me, it doesn't cost you a thing."

She would not let him spend it on her.
He saw her refuse it, and it went through his chest like cold water.
Her mouth was set, and her eyes had gone bright.

"You heard about the west," she said.

"The athletic director had a call this week."

"Then here's the easy way." Her voice was level, and her eyes were not.
"You keep quiet, and you keep your extension.
The class signs Wednesday, and the trustees get a clean page in February.
I'm on the interstate the morning of the sixth.
Nobody has to pay for me, Dan. You least of all."

"No. Let it cost me. Whatever it costs, I'll pay it.
You're not carrying this out of the county on your own."

For a second her face was not angry at all.
"You'd pay it, and I'd watch you pay it," she said.
"I've watched enough people pay for standing near me. I won't watch you."

"Then we're both saying no."

Her mouth moved at the corner as if it wanted to smile and would not.
"Then we are."

She put her back to the door and her hand behind her on the knob,
to let him out.
He came across the room to go,
and the suitcase rack caught his hip and rang against the wall
loud enough for the whole Court.
She laughed.
It came out of her at the worst possible time, one short helpless sound,
and she put her free hand over her mouth.
Her eyes over it were wet.
He stopped a foot from her.
Neither of them had had a thing to drink,
and both of them knew exactly what they were doing.
He could hear her breath, fast and quiet, over the furnace.
His own breath had gone short.
He lifted his hand slowly and laid the backs of his fingers along her jaw,
and she did not turn her face away from them.
Her hand came off the knob.

Then she had a fist in the front of his jacket, and she pulled, and he came.
His heart went hard against his ribs, and he felt it in his throat.
Her back was flat against the door, and his hands were at her waist
over the sweater, and she came up on her toes into him
until he could feel the whole length of her.
The heat of her came through his shirt.
He forgot to breathe, and then he could not get his breath back.
Her other hand went around his neck, and her fingers were cold,
and the skin there jumped under them.
He wanted her.
She was leaving on the sixth,
and he had just told her he would let it cost him,
and he wanted her anyway with his whole body,
and none of it would go together.
Her mouth opened under his, and he heard the sound she made low in her throat,
and his knees nearly went.

She ended it.
Her palm came flat against his chest and pushed, not hard, an inch.
He went back the inch.
His chest was heaving under her hand, and she could feel it.
Her face was flushed, and her eyes were steady on his, and wet again.

"Go home, Dan." Her voice shook, and she let it.
"Please. Go home."

She opened the door behind her,
and the cold came in off the gravel between them.
He went out into it.
Behind him the door shut, and he heard her lock turn, and then nothing.

He sat in the truck with the engine running and his hands on the wheel,
and he could still taste her.
The lawyer said don't.
On Monday the first the review would sit all day with its list,
and his name was not on it.
If he walked in anyway and finished the sentence,
the extension was gone in February, and his staff's jobs went with it.

That was not what he was afraid of.
She would go down the interstate the morning of the sixth
believing the one man she had let close had told the county she was wrong,
and every mile of it he would know he could have fixed it and had not.
The thought sat in his stomach like a stone.

Her fist had been in the front of his jacket.
He could still feel the place, warm over his heart,
and the heater had nothing to do with it.

He put the truck in gear and let it roll to the mouth of the lot.
He put the turn signal on, away from home.
It ticked in the dark cab, steady as a pulse, and he let it.
