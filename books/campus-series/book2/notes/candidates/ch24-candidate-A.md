The wipers could not keep up on the slow setting,
and Dan Merritt put them on the fast one halfway up Millrow.
It was the first rain since the ice, and it was only rain.
It came straight down, warm for January,
and it hissed on the roof of the truck like a shower left running.
The gutters ran brown and full down both sides of the hill.
This was Wednesday the twentieth of January, before light.
He put the truck nose to the fence in the Fieldhouse lot
and ran for the side door with his collar up.
The phone went in his coat pocket while the key was still in the lock.

He read it there, with the door open an inch and the rain on his neck.
It was from the athletic director,
with Dan's name in the address line and hers under it,
and nobody else on it, no header, no signature.
Dan read it with the water running off the edge of the phone.

*Dr. Cole is suspended from her duties with the football program
as of this morning.
This office has asked for an independent review
of the December medical determination,
with a report to this office within fourteen days.
The report will be released when this office has it.
Inquiries come to this office and nowhere else.*

He read it twice.
On the second read he did the arithmetic,
because the athletic director had done it first and had not said so.
Fourteen from a Wednesday was the third of February,
and that was signing day, the morning the class put its names on paper.
Somebody had counted that.
Her name under his went through him
like a hand laid flat between the shoulder blades, and stayed there.
Nobody else knew yet, not the Checkerboard, not any room on Grapevine,
not the building behind the door.
Two names on an email knew, and one of them was standing in the rain.

"Suspended" was the polite word, and "review" was the paper.
Nobody had fired her.
In February the trustees sat down to a page with the building on it,
and the group that rode in with the building was on the same page.
This review was how her job went to that group as a process fix.
Not one person at that table would have to say she had been wrong.

Monday night Dan had opened the board at his kitchen table,
read Rhonda Sipes's post and every reply under it, and slept four hours.
Tuesday he had done fourteen hours in the Fieldhouse on the two quiet families,
and the aldermen's vote had reached him at nine that night from Ty.
If the county ever proved the coach and the team physician were more than that,
in season, the school would not need a review for either of them.
Both of them would be gone by dark the same day.
A second time did not sit a man down for a year. It ended him.

He went in and shut the side door on the rain,
and left every other door in the building open, the way it was kept.


The lawyer came up the corridor before eight,
with the press woman a step behind him,
past every open door in the building.
Dan was at his desk with the January roster,
and the window on the lot side was running with rain.
The athletic director's office had sent the two of them up.
The lawyer was the university's, a man in a raincoat with the hood down,
and he wanted no statement from anybody, today or ever.
The press woman was the department's,
the one who put the athletic director's words where the county read them.
She wanted those words to be the only words there were.
Neither of them was wrong. That was going to be the trouble with them.
The lawyer reached behind him and shut Dan's door,
and the latch took, and it was the one shut door in the Fieldhouse.
The lawyer sat. The press woman stood.

"You've seen the email," the lawyer said.

"I've seen it."

"Then this won't take long.
Between now and the third,
you don't discuss Dr. Cole, the hold, or the review.
Not to a reporter, not on the radio, not to a parent in a lot.
The department puts out a statement at ten. It's his words."

"Ten," the press woman said.
"It goes up on the program's accounts,
and the county will have it by ten past. You don't add to it."

"What's in it," Dan said.

"What you read, shorter," she said.

Dan's hand went to the marker on the desk and turned it end over end.
He put it down with the cap toward the door.

"I'll say the part you're not going to like," the lawyer said.
"You backed her on the radio last week.
Anything with her name in it now is that porch again,
and the review gets asked why the coach is vouching for the doctor
who held his quarterback.
It helps her for a day. It hurts her for a month.
Any statement from you makes it worse for her."

It was true.
Dan had known that since the rain.
That was the trap in the sentence. There was no lie in it to push on.

He had the other sentence whole in his head by then, and it was short.
*Dr. Cole was right in December.
I backed the hold then and I back it now,
and the review can put my name under hers.*
He could have said it to the two people in front of him,
and the press woman would have typed it.

The other thing was also true.
He was in the Fieldhouse fourteen hours today,
and fourteen hours every day for eleven months of the year.
The job was not a thing he had. It was what he was.
He knew what he was choosing, with his hand flat on the roster,
and he chose it.

"All right," Dan said.

The lawyer looked at Dan a second longer, as if expecting more,
and got up.
The press woman had her phone out before she reached the door.

"If anybody calls you, it's my number they get," she said.

The lawyer had seen the corridor coming in,
and left the door open on his way out.
Anybody on the far side of that door would have wanted the coach
to say the sentence.
Dan would have wanted it himself, watching a man he did not know.
He had said all right instead,
and the two people who had heard him say it were already on the stairs.


At ten the statement went up where the press woman had said it would,
on the program's own accounts, the ones the county followed for kickoff times.
Dan read it on the desk screen.
It was four sentences in the athletic director's voice.
It had "suspended" in it and "independent" in it and the number of days,
and it did not have her first name in it, or his.
By ten past the county had it.

By eleven it had jumped rooms.
Dan opened the town room at eleven out of a habit ten years old.
A board had known what he was once before his own school did.
Since then he read every room the town typed in
for the football under the ordinary talk.
This morning he was reading for what the town knew about the review.
The town room was where Ashford went for lost dogs and road work,
and it had never cared who the quarterback was.
Somebody had carried the parents' board into it anyway.
DeeAnn Prewitt was local.
She sat in her car at practice every day with the heat on,
and she posted under her own name.
She had wanted to be first at something since the fall.

> **DeeAnn Prewitt** So the school reads the parents' board after all.
> Team doctor's off the job as of this morning, it's on the program's
> page. Rhonda Sipes called it Monday and got jumped on for it. "If
> it's who I think it is, this explains a LOT about the hold."
>
> **Lonnie Hatch** DeeAnn, this is where I find out when they're
> grinding the stumps on Fountain Row. Take it back where it came
> from.
>
> **Bev Tolliver** Some of us are on here for the recycling schedule.
> It's still Thursday, in case that's what anybody came for.

There were eleven under her by the time Dan got to the bottom,
and every one of them was about DeeAnn.
Not one of them was about the doctor.
DeeAnn had walked in with a story from the wrong building,
and the room had looked at her and not at the story.
Nobody took it down. The town room had nobody to do that.
They left her up there with the recycling schedule under her,
and it cost her the room, and she had known the price when she typed it.
The county had wanted a name said out loud since October,
and when the school said it on letterhead, the town's own room shrugged.
The shrug was worse than the dogpile. A dogpile was at least about her.

The vote was a little way down the same room,
under a post about Tuesday night.
Three replies, and all three were about parking.
Where Delmar's customers would park once the street was gone,
whether the deck would be free on game days,
and whether the Mule's lot counted.
He said its meaning over once, plain, since nobody in the room had.
On Tuesday night the aldermen had voted to change
what could be built on the town's half of Millrow.
Now the glass building could come across the street from the campus.
The dirt under her clinic was not the town's to vote on.
It was the university's, and the trustees voted on it in February,
in the same sitting as her job.
The street had gone Tuesday night and the doctor on Wednesday morning,
and nobody in the town room had put the two in one sentence.
He shut the screen and looked at the rain on the lot instead.

At noon the RAV4 was in the annex lot with its engine running.
Dan saw it from the film-room window that looked down on the lot.
The exhaust hung white behind it in the rain, and there was one person in it.
He went down the stairs and out the side door and across.
The lot had water standing in every rut the ice had left,
and he went through the water instead of around it.
The rain had not let up since dark.
It came down straight and loud on the lot,
and off the annex roof in a sheet where the gutter was gone.
Monday's orange survey flags stood in the wet between the annex and the fence.
The trainer stood in the warm-end door with his hands in his pockets,
on the sill and not on the step.
He had ridden down the hill in Dan's truck with her in the middle,
and he had a job in the building she was leaving.
The sill was as far as a man with both of those could come.
A staff car Dan did not know sat at the fence with somebody in it,
where a person could see the whole lot and the road.
Whoever had her clinic for the fortnight was not up here yet.

Aisha was in the driver's seat with the heat on and both hands on the wheel,
in a sweater, her curls damp from the three steps out the door.
The parka was folded over one box on the seat beside her,
the day's box and not the office.
The tablet was on top, and a mug from her desk was in the corner.
He put one knuckle on the glass, once.

The window came down four inches and stopped.
The four inches of her face came down to him with the car's heat behind it,
and his breath went out of him and did not come back.
His heart came up under his coat, hard,
in the place her name had gone in that morning.
His right hand wanted the door handle under her window.
It wanted the handle and the seat beside the box,
and it went into his coat pocket instead and shut on the key.
The rain was going down inside his collar and he could not feel it.
He could feel the warm air off her vent on his face at the gap,
and under it the smell of the car, which was her.
His face was doing something he could not stop,
and she read it, because she read faces for a living.
Her eyes came up to his and held.
One corner of her mouth went up and did not get all the way there.
He wanted her.
He wanted her with a man on the sill behind him and a car at the fence,
and he was not going to say the one sentence.
He knew both of those at once, with the rain running off his chin.

"Film's at four," Dan said.

That was all a coach could say at a car window at noon.
He had known it going down the stairs, and he said it anyway.

"I know," Aisha said.

She looked at him through the four inches a while longer.
Her eyes went over his face once, the way they went over a chart,
and came back to his eyes and stayed.
She could have asked him for it.
One sentence with her name in it, and she knew he had it.
She knew the man.
She did not ask.
He watched her not ask, and he knew what he was watching.
Nobody had ever spent anything on her in her life,
and she was not going to start by requesting it, through four inches of glass.
Her hand came off the wheel and went to the switch,
and the four inches went back up to nothing.
The glass had rain on it again where her face had been.

She backed out of the warm end and went across the lot, slow, through the water,
and took Millrow down.
It was noon, in daylight, with the county at its windows the length of the hill,
and her taillights went down through the rain until the first curve took them.
Dan stood in the lot until they were gone.
Then he walked back across it, through the same water,
past the staff car with its wipers going.
He was a coach who had gone out to a car in the rain
and said nothing anybody would have crossed a lot to hear.
The trainer had gone in off the sill by the time Dan reached the side door.


Film was at four, in the film room,
with the staff in it and the rain on the window.
There was no practice to film until spring, so it was the bowl cutup again,
and the coordinator had the clicker.
Ty Beaumont came in last with a chair and set it by the door.

"Coach," Ty said, and that was all Ty said.

Ty's truck was paid for out of this job, a month at a time.
Ty knew how to be on a staff, and on a day like this that was one word.
Nobody on the staff asked about the statement.
The cutup ran.
Dan stood at the marker tray with the sentence whole in his head,
in the same words it had had at eight and at noon.
He had the sentence. He was keeping it.
Both of those were true in the same hour.

At six the rain was still coming down on the square,
and the Checkerboard's front glass ran with it from the top of the letters down.
Dan took the stool nearest the register,
and the cook set the Coach's Slice down in front of him without a word
and went back to the griddle.
Earlene Tatum was at the window table with the pot in her hand,
standing, not sitting, with Tunk Ferrell's ear beside her.
Earlene wanted the county counted straight.
Tunk wanted to be the one who told it.
The cook wanted the griddle, and had it.

[TK Tunk on the board — not seated or voted on this page]

The vote had reached the counter by mouth on Tuesday night,
before the aldermen were out of the building.
The statement had reached it at ten this morning the same way.

"Pie at six o'clock at night," Tunk said, to the window.
"I've been coming in here longer than you've had that griddle,
and I never got pie at six."

"You never asked," the cook said, to the griddle.

"I'm asking."

"It's a morning item."

Tunk laughed, and it was at the coach.
Dan had nothing to put back on it.
He ate the pie. That had been the cook's point, and the cook did not say so.

"Rain got the stumps on Fountain Row," Tunk said.
"They'll grind Friday now, if it quits."

Nobody answered that. Earlene poured.

"I'll tell you what I counted," Earlene said, to the pot,
"and then I'm done with it.
Nobody up that hill fired anybody today. Read it again.
They set her out on the step for two weeks
and asked the county to finish the job,
and the county has never yet turned down work like that.
That's one.
Tuesday night they moved a street. That's two.
Anybody in here can count to two,
and I don't need to tell you what it adds up to."

Tunk looked at the window a while.

"That's two things, Earlene."

"It's two things. I can count."

Nobody at the counter said her name, and nobody said the man's
whose building it was, and everybody at the counter had both.
Dan kept his back to the window, and the Table got that much of him.
The sentence was still there.
Earlene had counted to two with a pot in her hand,
and he could have made it three from the stool, and he ate his pie.


At dark Dan drove up Millrow to the annex and not to the Fieldhouse.
It was still raining, lighter now, a fine one the wipers took on slow,
and the water in the gutters had gone down to a sound.
The clinic was lit at the warm end.
Whoever had her clinic tonight had the lights on for it,
and the warm-end door was unlocked because the clinic was open.
He went in out of the rain, and the corridor's heat was on him at the door.
The tank in the whirlpool room was off.
Somebody down the corridor was running a sink, and did not come out.
Her office door stood open at the end.

He stood in the door and did not go in.
The chair was pushed in under the desk, square, the way she left it.
The desk had a clean place on it the size of a tablet
and a ring at the corner the size of a mug, and nothing else.
The file drawer was shut.
Whoever sat here tomorrow would pull that chair out and not know
it had been pushed in by her.
He had the sentence whole one more time, standing there,
in the same words it had had in the rain at the door and in the lot at noon.
*Dr. Cole was right in December.
I backed the hold then and I back it now,
and the review can put my name under hers.*
It was still one sentence, and it was still true, and nobody had heard it.
It would cost the job.
He had counted that at eight and again at noon and got the same number twice.
He was afraid of that number.
He said that to himself plain, and it did not get any less true.
She was down the hill somewhere with the box on a floor,
and he did not know which room the tablet was in tonight.
The chair was hers. The desk was hers.
The sentence with her name in it was his,
and he stood in her door with it in the pocket where the key was.
That was as far as it had gone all day.
Down the corridor the sink stopped.
He took his hands out of his pockets, left the chair where she had pushed it,
and did not go in.
