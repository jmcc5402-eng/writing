> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **GrammyInSectionC:** My grandson called to see if I made it through
> the ice. I did. He says he's fine and he sounds fine. His good coat is
> on my hook, where it has been since October.


Aisha Cole's thumb went down four hundred pictures in the dark of nine,
boots already on, looking for the one the other room had found first.
Monday the eighteenth of January was the first morning of the thaw,
and the phone's light on the blanket was the only light in the room.
Across the gravel Verna's office was still dark.
The thread was pinned at the top of the parents' board,
*Playoff Pics — Post Here*, and had been since seven on the nineteenth.
Tents, coolers, a boy asleep on a tailgate under a flag.
Four hundred of them, from mothers in three states, and she was in one.
She had been sent to the album by the smaller room on Grapevine,
the one that was not about football,
which she read at six because the county did.

The other room had put it up overnight,
under the local handle that room had had since the fall.
No name under it. That room had never needed one.
One line, and the picture.

> **AshfordBornAndBred:** Playoff album, eleven in the morning, the
> nineteenth. Somebody went through the whole album for this one. Worth
> the scroll.

It was the lot at eleven on the nineteenth of December.
A boy of eight or nine stood on an ice chest
with a poster-board sign over his head, WE DROVE 11 HRS FOR THIS.
Dan was leaning in toward the sign to read it,
and she was at his shoulder, laughing.
The lid of the ice chest had thrown the sun back up into all of them,
one hard white flash, and the flash was in the picture too.
She knew what she had been laughing at.
His mother vetoed the ticket, the father had said,
with his phone up for the boy,
and the phone had got the two of them instead.

The album put a time under every picture, the time it went up,
and under this one the time was eleven, the nineteenth of December.
The father had posted it before she was out of the lot.
So the county had it now in one line, with a picture to hold it.
The doctor and the coach, laughing together at eleven
on the morning of the game Trey Gault sat out.
What the county did not put under it was the date on the paper.
She had signed Trey out of that game on the Thursday, two days before,
on a hold she had decided the Monday before that.
The decision was made and papered before the lid ever flashed.
The picture said the laugh was the reason,
and a picture taken at eleven does not know what a form said on Thursday.

She had gone past him after the flash, close,
her shoulder and the parka's nylon going along his sleeve,
and she had not looked back.
Her body had that before she had a word for it.
The skin of her upper arm went warm under the sweater,
the same stripe of it, as if the sleeve were there now.
Her breath went short and stayed short.
Her heart came up hard once and did not settle.
Her throat was tight around nothing.
Her hands, holding the phone over the blanket, were not steady,
and she pressed her thumbs flat on the glass so they would be.
Her face was hot in a dark room with nobody in it.
The whole left side of her still had the bench going down Millrow in it,
and that had been six nights ago.

That was the thing under the picture that nobody had typed.
It was true.
She had laughed like that because it was Dan leaning in at her shoulder,
and she wanted what her own face in the picture said she wanted.
The county was right, and it was right about the wrong thing.
It had the laugh and it had the hold,
and it had put the one under the other.
The laugh was hers.
The hold was Trey's brain, and the paper said so, with the date on it.
Nobody in the county was going to read the paper now.

She read it once.
She did not go back up to the album to look at it again,
and she did not go back to the other room.
She put the phone face down on the blanket and stood up in her boots.

Outside, the ice was going off the Magnolia Court in water.
It ran off the edge of the roof onto the gravel in one line,
steady, like a tap left on.
The gravel showed through in the ruts where the cars went.
The RAV4 was in front of nine again with the thaw's grit up its sides.
The hill had been clear by Saturday.
She had walked up Millrow for it that morning, alone, and driven it down.
At a quarter past six Verna's office light came on
and lit a full space in front of nine.
Verna Poteat was a shape behind the blind and did not lift it.
Verna wanted her rent and her quiet,
and this morning she had a tenant the town was talking about
on her own gravel.
Aisha put the parka on and picked up the tablet and the keys.


Aisha drove up Millrow at eight with the tablet on the seat beside her.
The road was wet and bare to the first curve, and then all the way up.
Below Delmar's the surveyors' blue chalk was out from under the ice
on the curb, the same blue it had been in December.
At the top, the lot between the annex and the Fieldhouse
had water standing in every rut.
The F-150 with two doors was across it, nose to the fence,
where it had been the night of the ice.
Dan was in his building.
She did not cross the lot to it, and she did not look at it twice.
She parked at the warm end and went in with the tablet under her arm.

The clinic opened at nine.
By ten the tank in the whirlpool room was running,
and the trainer had a kid on the near table with an ankle up on a bolster.
The corridor floor had wet boot prints down the middle of it
from the door to the rehab rooms.
Aisha came down the corridor from her office with the tablet
and stopped in the door of the whirlpool room.
The trainer's phone was on the taping table by the kid's foot, lit,
and as she came in his hand went out and turned it face down.
He did not look at it while he did it.
The kid on the table looked at her, and then at the ankle.

"Doc," the trainer said.
There was something under the word, and he left it there.
"Left ankle, lateral. He rolled it on the ice Saturday
going down the dorm steps.
Swelling's down from what he says it was. I've had him in the tank."

"Let me see it."

She set the tablet on the table, on the side away from his phone,
and put both hands on the ankle.
The kid had a phone of his own on his chest, dark.
She went over the joint the way she went over every ankle,
the outside, the ligament, the bone above it,
and watched the kid's face and not his phone.

"There?" she said. "And there? Push against my hand. Good.
Now pull it back toward you. That's fine. That's all I need."

He shook his head twice and nodded once, and she nodded back.

"Second degree, low end. There's no fracture in that.
Tape it. Boot on the stairs. No running until I look at it Thursday,
and I mean no running, not less running."
She said the next part to the kid.
"Take the elevator in the dorm this week. Let people look at you.
It's a week. You'll live."

"Thursday," the trainer said,
and wrote it on the back of his hand with the marker from his pocket.
"Doc, about the—"

"The ankle," she said. "Tell me about the ankle."

"Ankle's what I said it was." He looked at the ankle and not at her.
"He's got a lab at one. Can he walk to it in the boot?"

"He can walk to it in the boot. Slow, and not on the ice side of the
quad." She looked at the kid again. "Ice on it tonight, twenty minutes,
then off. Not on it all night. Twenty and off."

The picture was in every phone in the building.
She knew it from the phone going over,
and from the word Doc with the something under it,
and she did not need a third way to know it.
What she did with her face was what she did for a mother in a hallway.
She kept it on the ankle.
It cost her the back of her neck.
The neck had gone hot when the phone turned,
and she let it be hot and did not put a hand to it.
The trainer wanted his job,
and he wanted to be on nobody's side of the lot between the two buildings.
This morning both of those wanted him to say nothing about a phone,
so he said nothing, and taped.

She stayed longer than the ankle needed.
The tank ran. The tape came off the roll in short pulls.
The kid looked at the ceiling.
Nobody said anything for the length of a whole ankle,
and she stood in the door through all of it with her tablet
and did not go.
The trainer smoothed the last strip down and did not look up.

"Tank's good for another twenty on him tomorrow, if you want it."

"Twenty. Then the bike, no resistance, if he can stand on it."

"He can stand on it. He walked in here on it."

"He walked in here on it wrong."

"7's on the bike Wednesday, same hour as last week," the trainer said.
"You want me on the wall again?"

"On the wall. Same as last time."

"Same as last time," the trainer said, to the tape.
"Thaw's going to make the lot a lake by Wednesday.
Tell them to park at the cold end."

That was all of it, and it was flat, and it lay there.
She went back down the corridor to her office.
Her office at the warm end had the desk and the boxes along the wall,
the last one with Dan's marker on it.
She opened Trey's file on the tablet
and put the date on a new line under the twenty.
It was Trey's way back through the hold,
every step dated, in her typing, from December to last Wednesday.
It was the work of her two hands.
By tonight the county would read every line of it as bought,
because of a laugh at eleven in a lot.
She typed the date anyway, and initialed it.


At noon Aisha was in the corridor with a roll of tape on her wrist,
coming back from the records room.
The trainer came out of the rehab room with his phone in his hand.
He held it out to her, screen up, and said nothing.
He meant it kindly. She could see that he meant it kindly.

It was a screenshot, forwarded into whatever chat he was in,
and it had been cropped by somebody in a hurry.
The picture was whole. The name at the top of it was not.
It read *onda Sipes*, and the time under the picture was whole too.
Under the screenshot in the chat somebody had typed one word,
and the trainer's thumb was over it.

"I've got it," she said, and gave him back his phone.

"Doc, I'm not—"

"I know you're not. Go tape somebody."

Then she went into her office and shut the door
and opened the parents' board on the tablet, once.
It had gone up at a quarter to twelve, under a real name,
in the room where mothers posted the weather and the dorms.

> **Rhonda Sipes** This is from our own playoff album, posted at eleven
> in the morning on December 19. That is game day, with 7 already sat.
> I said it in December and was told I was speculating. Here is the
> picture. This explains a LOT about the hold.
>
> **PomPoms&Prayers** Praying for the boys and for every family on
> here today. That is all I am going to say.
>
> **Tim Brasher** For the record, the letter was never about this. It
> was about clarity on the medical decisions, and it still is.
>
> **DossCoDadx3** I'm not going to pretend I didn't look. I looked.
>
> **Kendra Voyles** Rhonda. There are kids who read this board.

Rhonda Sipes drove in from out of state for every home game
and had wanted the hold explained since October.
She had typed the same sentence in December without a picture,
and now she had the picture, and she had put her name over it.
Aisha could hate the post and be sorry for the woman in the same breath,
and she was.
The replies were small and polite and came in from three states,
and every one of them was for the record.
Whatever the county was saying about it was being typed
somewhere smaller than this board,
and traveling from phone to phone with the names cropped off.
The trainer's was the first to reach her.
It would not be the last.

Marisol Pruett moderated that board.
Her son, Wes, was the walk-on at the bottom of the roster
that the same board had wanted off it since the fall.
Nothing under Rhonda's name went gray by half past twelve.
Nothing went gray by one.
Marisol had not texted, and she had not called,
and the post stood under Aisha's face in the room her friend ran.

Aisha typed one text and sent it.

*Leave it up. Don't spend Wes on me.*

Her thumb was on the arrow a while before it went.
Then she held her breath for the answer,
and there was no answer,
and she let the breath out at one and held it again at two.
The post stayed up.
Marisol did not answer.
Both of those things were still true at three,
and Aisha did not send a second text, and did not open the board again.
She carried the accusation the rest of the day where she carried a bad
night in an emergency room, in the jaw, with her teeth together.


At three Aisha went out the warm-end door with her keys in her hand,
to move the RAV4 out of the standing water.
Delores Fountain-Mabry was in the lot with the survey crew.
Delores was the woman who had counted the annex's forty-one boxes
into Mt. Nebo's fellowship hall in December,
with a pen and not with her face.
She had the same legal pad against her coat now.
The crew was two men with a tripod at the cold end,
setting orange flags in the wet ground along the wall where the ice had been.
The chalk on the curb was bare again and the same blue.
Delores wanted the town counted honestly before it was sold,
and she was here to count it.

"Doctor," Delores said.
"The aldermen sit tomorrow night, the third Tuesday.
I've been told a number for what this ground is worth
and I've been told where their line runs.
I'd rather have walked it myself before I say either one out loud
in front of the aldermen and a phone recording it."

"It runs where the flags are."

"It runs where the flags are today."
Delores wrote something on the pad without looking down at it.
"Last week it ran under the ice, and they couldn't find it either."

One of the crew looked up from the tripod at that.

"Ma'am, we're setting the same line we set in December," he said.
"Chalk's still on the curb. You can go look."

"I have looked. I'm asking where it goes after the curb,
and the chalk doesn't say."

He went back to the tripod.

"How far down the hill does it go?" Aisha said.

"To the church lot, on their drawing.
I have not seen it get there yet, and I'm the one with the key.
They can draw a line through my parking and I'll count the spaces
they drew it through, and say the number tomorrow."

"Will the number change anything?"

"Not tomorrow." Delores wrote. "Numbers don't. People do,
and it takes them a while, and they like to have the number first."

The flags went in along the wall, one every ten feet,
and the wet ground took them without a sound.
Delores counted them onto the pad as they went in,
as she had counted boxes past a door,
and she did not look at Aisha while she did it.
Then she did.

"I've seen the picture, Doctor. I'll say that so you don't wonder.
It came down the pew at Mt. Nebo yesterday morning on a phone
before the second hymn, and I looked, same as everybody.
A photograph doesn't lie.
It just repeats whatever it's told."

She wrote the next flag on the pad.
Aisha stood in the wet lot with her keys and let it sit.
Delores had said all she meant to about it, and went back to counting,
and Aisha did not ask her anything.

"You moving that car, Doctor, or standing in the water with it?"

"Moving it."

"Then move it. I've got nine more of these to count
and the light goes at five."

The third of February was a Wednesday. That was sixteen days.
After it, the terms said, there was nothing left to hold for.
It went through her once, low, under the ribs,
where the road dropped at the top of Millrow,
and she stood in the water and let it.
In February the trustees sat down to one page with three things on it.
The pledge for the glass building was one,
and Dan's extension was the line under it.
The third was the group that rode in with the pledge
and took over the team's doctors, which was her job under a politer name.
She had known that since December, from him.
The letter had never been the thing.
Eleven polite families asking for clarity were one piece of paper.
The picture was the paper that let a polite agenda do it in one sitting
and call it a process.

Kat's text came while the last flag went in.

*Honey. Three, all with a February in them. I've seen it. Call me
when you can.*

Aisha did not call yet.
She moved the RAV4 out of the water and went back in to the clinic.
Delores counted the last flag and went down the hill to her aldermen.


At nine at night Aisha sat on the bed in nine with the light off
and the phone in her hand.
The furnace was running behind again, because the wind had gone north.
It came around the corner of the building in a long pull
and went off across the square.
Outside the window the RAV4 was the one car in Verna's gravel.
The picture was open on the phone.
Under it the count said eleven hundred views, and then it said more,
and the replies were still coming in, one every few minutes,
small, polite, from three states.
The county was in nine with her,
the way it was in every room with a phone in it.

His phone had the same picture on it.
Dan Merritt read the parents' board every night before he slept,
every line of it.
He read it like it was a chart, she had told him once in the fall,
and he read that board, not the other room.
He was up the hill in the Fieldhouse, or he was at his house,
and she did not know which building he was in tonight.
She did not know if he had opened it.
That was the thing about a picture on two phones.
She could not see his face, and she could not see the phone,
and she could not walk up the hill and look.
A doctor who crossed the lot to his building tomorrow
wrote the caption under the picture herself.
She would not write it, and she would not spend him on it either.
Under him the county had a caption too,
and it would not be the first one it had written under him.

She wanted him, and there was no new way to say it,
and she said it anyway, to the dark.
Her mouth wanted his, and had since the lot in December,
and she could feel exactly where.
Her hands wanted the back of the bare hand that had come off the wheel,
and they had the phone instead, and they held it too hard.
The whole left side of her, shoulder to hip,
still had the shape of his arm through the parka from the bench.
It went warm when she let it, and she let it.
Her heart went like she had walked up the hill instead of driving it.
Her throat was tight, and her face was hot in a cold room,
and there was nobody to hide it from and she hid it anyway.
Sixteen days had been possible on Wednesday,
in a cab, with his hand closed on hers under the dash.
Tonight the county had said it for them, with a picture.
Sixteen more days of not saying it back were not possible,
and she was going to hold every one of them.

She had let one person close, once, on terms nobody could see.
It was costing her everything she had built up that hill,
the rooms, the chart, the name on the form.
Nobody had ever spent anything on her.
She had made sure of that, all her life, and she was good at it.
She would rather be the one who paid.
She would not have said that to anybody, and it was true.
If the county was going to write a caption, it could write it under her.

The picture was on her phone in nine.
It was on his, up the hill or at his house,
in a building she could not see from this window.
Her laugh and his lean, side by side, on two phones in two buildings,
and the county reading both.
The count under it went up by one while she looked, and then by one more.
