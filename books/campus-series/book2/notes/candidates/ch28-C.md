# Chapter 28 — Signing Day

POV: Dan, third person limited, past tense.
(DRAFT — blind candidate C, card D4, per plots/brief-ch28.md.)

---

> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Rhonda Sipes:** Mine signed three years ago in a hat two sizes too
> big. He called this morning to ask if I still have the hat. I do. He
> cannot have it.


The drink machine in the high school's front hallway
spat the athletic director's dollar back out for the third time,
and he smoothed it flat on his knee and fed it in again, a little harder.
Dan Merritt stood beside him with his hands in his coat pockets
in a hallway that smelled of floor wax,
while the cold came in under the gym doors in a thin line along the tile.
It was half past eight on Wednesday, the third of February, signing day.
WDSS 1340 went on the air from the gym at nine for its signing-day show.
Across town at the same hour, the independent review sat for the last time
to say in writing whether Aisha Cole had been right
to hold Trey Gault out of the playoff in December.
If it said she was wrong, that went on her record and stayed there.
And by the end of the day the class closed.
Any boy who had not signed by then was not coming.

The college would have charged the station for a room with bleachers in it.
The high school let 1340 have its gym for a mention on the air,
so signing day happened under a high school scoreboard.

The athletic director had come to be in the first picture.
Then he would drive across town to the review
and sit at the university's table, the way he had on Monday.
He wanted the new building on the annex ground,
and he wanted a quiet February in front of the trustees.
On Monday afternoon he had lost both.
The trustees had moved the building's pledge to a later sitting
with no date on it.
A pledge that size was an athletic director's to land,
so when the trustees asked in February where it had gone,
they would ask him and not the coach who had made it move.
He had signed Aisha's leave in January
because eleven families and a board had put it on his desk,
and he had signed it the way a man signs for a package
he did not order.
Dan had not forgiven him for that.
He did not think the man was a crook, either.

The machine took the dollar and lit up.
The athletic director did not press anything.
He stood with his eyes on the lit buttons.

"They'll ask me in February where that money went," he said.
"It came to this department through my office,
and it moved on my watch."

"It didn't move on your watch," Dan said.

The athletic director looked at him then.
He had shaved close for the pictures,
and under the shave his face was gray from no sleep.

"It moved on my testimony," Dan said.
"You didn't lose the money. I did."

Nobody else was in the hallway to hear it.
The janitor's cart stood by the trophy case with nobody at it,
and behind the gym doors Tick was testing his microphone, one, two.
The athletic director opened his mouth and shut it.
His shoulders came down an inch under the good coat.
Then he looked at his watch.

"That doesn't put it back on the page," he said.

"No," Dan said. "It doesn't."

The athletic director went out the front doors to his car and the review.
He had pressed nothing, and the machine kept his dollar.

Dan would have spent the pledge again.
He would have spent his own extension and every hard look
the trustees had left to give him in February,
all of it for one sentence on the record with her whole name after it.
He would never say that to anybody alive.
He would say it least of all to Aisha,
who would tell him the price had been too high and be right.


Inside, the gym was two-thirds full by nine,
with the bleachers pulled out along one wall only
and the heat coming up through the floor vents
with a smell of dust and old popcorn.
Dan had a folded index card in his coat pocket
with the class on it in his own hand, for when Tick asked.

Tick Moran had a card table at center court
with his mixer on it, the one older than most of the roster,
and the on-air bulb clipped to a mic stand at his elbow.
Tick hosted the Coach's Table on 1340 from Delmar's porch,
and this morning's show was his too.
He wanted the county talking about it at the Checkerboard tomorrow.
He wanted it fair as well, and most mornings he got both.
Beside the mixer sat a fax machine,
its cord run across the floor to an outlet under the scorer's table.
Tick said a signing day needed a sound, and a fax was the sound.
Dan had not seen a signing paper come through a fax in his whole career.

Most of the class had signed in December,
so the morning was mostly a party for boys who were already his.
Three papers were still due by the close.
One belonged to the quarterback Dan had flipped out of the transfer portal
in January.
The other two belonged to the families who had said yes in the fall
and had not picked up a phone to Dan or anybody on his staff
since the first week of January.

The Liars' Table had the third row of the bleachers,
the way it had the front window at the Checkerboard most mornings.
Tunk Ferrell sat on the aisle.
He wanted every story in town before anybody else had it,
and the aisle was closest to Tick's table.
Earlene Tatum sat beside him in a red knit hat.
She had sat in the front row of the review on Monday,
and she wanted one thing out of this morning,
the doctor's name cleared out loud where the county could hear it.
Peanut Kyzer, who ran the taps at the Mule on the square,
had his coat zipped and his keys in his fist.
He wanted to be behind his own bar by noon.
Sonny Dillard stood at the end of the row in half his gear,
the feed store's counter by day and the pumper when the pager went.
He wanted one morning the pager left alone.

The bulb went red at nine.

"Signing day, friends, from center court," Tick said.
"Most of these boys signed in December,
and we're going to clap for them again, because they earned it twice.
I've got a coach and I've got coffee.
I've got a fax machine the station bought when I still had hair."

The bleachers laughed.
Tick read the class off his sheet name by name,
and the gym clapped for every one of them
as if it had not clapped for the same names in December.
A boy in the top row stomped the bleacher for each name,
and by the fourth name his whole row was stomping with him.

A girl from the high school's yearbook had the camera for the station's page.
She stood Dan in front of the banner with the class's pictures printed on it
and put a pen in his hand.

"Sign something, Coach," she said.

"There's nothing to sign."

"It's signing day. Sign something."

He signed the back of Tick's rundown, and she took the picture.

"He's signing his lunch order," Tunk said.

The girl took three more.
Dan stood for them in the new haircut, which still felt a size too short.
He had got it last Thursday at Fountain's from Odell,
the barber on Fountain Row, who gave a man a straight line and nothing extra.
That night Aisha had laughed at it behind her wrist.
He could hear it now if he let himself, one short sound in a dark room.
The girl said, "Smile, Coach," and for a second he did, at that.

At a quarter past nine he took his phone out and looked at it.
The review had been sitting for fifteen minutes.
Aisha would be at the small table on the left
with her own copy of the record tabbed at her elbow,
in the one good jacket, with no lawyer and nobody at the table beside her.
Nothing had come.
He put the phone away.
By half past nine he had looked four times.

"That's four, Coach," Earlene said from the third row.
"It won't come any faster for you looking."

She meant the review.
She was looking at him down the row over her folded hands,
and she meant the doctor too, and she let him see it.
Heat went up the back of his neck.
He put the phone in his inside pocket, where he would have to work to reach it.

At ten to ten Tunk held his own phone down the row to Peanut.

"Town room's got a picture up," Tunk said.
"That pledge crew's out on the annex ground with the long tape.
Ground's froze hard, and there's three of them out in it."

Peanut looked and handed it back.
The trustees had moved the pledge back on Monday.
The crew was out on her ground anyway, working to the eleventh,
the day her annex had to be boxed.
Either nobody had told them about Monday,
or somebody had told them to work as if it had not happened.
Dan did not like either one.

At ten past ten Tick's phone lit on the card table beside the fax.
He read it with the mic still open,
and Dan watched his face go from the show's face to his own.
Tick looked across the floor at Dan once.
Then he leaned in to the mic.

"Friends, I'll give you this one straight, because you'll see it anyway,"
Tick said.
"The quarterback who came to Ashford out of the portal in January
has signed with the school he said yes to first.
They put it up on their own page ten minutes ago.
Coach is standing right here,
and I'm not going to ask him about it on my air."

The stomping in the top row stopped.

Dan had known since the father stopped calling in January.
He had told that father on the phone that his doctor held kids out here
and would do it again if the numbers said so.
On Monday he had said it again in front of the county, with her name on it.
A father two states off wanted a program that would play his boy,
and there was nothing crooked in that.
The father had read about Monday on the board like everybody else.
Then he had taken his son where the coach did not say things like that.

Tick went on, because the show went on.

"And the two families we've waited on since the fall
haven't sent us a word," Tick said.
"They've got till the close.
I wouldn't sit up nights by the fax."

The fax sat by the mixer and did not ring.
Three papers had been due today, and not one of them was coming.
The class had three holes in it now,
read out on the radio in the county's own gym,
and his staff would hear it in their trucks on the way in to the Fieldhouse.
His extension had already come off the trustees' page on Monday.

He stood there.
He stood under the printed faces of a class that was mostly his
while the whole county looked at the three that were not,
and he kept his hands open at his sides
because a fist would have been up on the town room by noon.
His face had gone hot, and he let it.
The yearbook girl had her camera up.
She looked at him over the top of it and lowered it
without taking the picture.

He was glad Aisha was across town and could not hear any of this,
because she would have known what it cost him before Tick finished the sentence
and then carried it too.


At five to eleven the phone on the card table lit again,
and this time it was Marisol Pruett.
Tick had called her on Monday night
and asked her to ring him from the review
if the county ought to hear anything before noon on signing day,
and Marisol had said she would.
Marisol moderated the parents' board,
and her son Wes was a walk-on on Dan's roster.
She wanted the record right, and she wanted her boy's spot kept.
She had never asked Dan one word about the second.

Tick put the phone to one ear and pushed the headphone off the other.

"Friends, I've got Marisol Pruett at the review," Tick said.
"She tells me, and I tell you, as near word for word as I can get it."

The reviewer had heard Missy Gault that morning, before the findings.
Missy had asked to be heard, and the reviewer had let her.
Missy was Trey's mother.
All winter she had wanted her boy safe and wanted him on the field,
and she could not have both.

Tick listened with his eyes shut.

"Missy Gault says this," Tick said.
"'We drove my son to Birmingham in December
to find a doctor who'd say Dr. Cole was wrong.
He read every page she sent him.
On the twenty-third of December he told us she was right.
I've had his report in my purse since it came,
and I never showed it to a soul.'"

He stopped, and his hand came off the fader.

"'Not even my husband,'" Tick said.

The gym had gone so quiet that Dan could hear the floor vents.

"'Nobody asked her to,
but she built Trey a file for the scouts,
every step he took with a date on it,
so in the spring they'll see a boy she let heal.
I saw it on her screen.
I'm standing up because of what Coach Merritt said Monday.'"

Every face in the bleachers turned to Dan.
He could not have said what his own face did.
His eyes had gone hot,
and he looked at the free-throw line to hold them still.
His hands shook at his sides.
He closed them and opened them again.
Monday had cost him his extension and a quarterback,
and it had bought Missy Gault on her feet in front of the county.
Across town Aisha was hearing it at her table with her own ears.
He would have given the class twice over to see her face.

Tick listened a long time.

"Marisol says Denny didn't know," he said.
"He was sitting right beside his wife, and he didn't know."

Denny Gault, Trey's father,
had been the loudest man on the parents' board all fall.
He wanted his boy on a field and the doctor gone,
and he had said both anywhere a man could say them.

"She says he put a hand over his eyes and kept it there," Tick said.
"Then he asked to be heard, and the reviewer let him."
Tick listened again.
"'They brought me that January letter to sign, and I told them no.
I wrote one of my own in December, and I'll answer for that one.
I wasn't putting my name under another man's words about my boy.'"

In January Dan had stood beside Denny under the heater
in the corner of Delmar's porch
and not asked why his name was missing from the letter the eleven had signed.
Now the county knew.
Denny had been the town's loud man all fall,
and all along he had been a father who would not sign.
This morning it had cost him the corner he used to hold court from,
and he had paid it out loud in front of the review,
with his wife's news still in his ears.

"She'll call when they read it," Tick said,
and he put the phone face up by the fax.


At twenty to twelve the phone lit again,
and Tick had it at his ear before the second buzz.
He listened and wrote on the back of his rundown, under Dan's signature.
Then he read it out slowly,
the way a man reads a thing he means to get right the first time.

"'The review affirms the call,'" Tick said.
"'Dr. Cole's leave is lifted as of today.'
And her documentation, it says here, was exemplary."

The gym did not know what to do with it.
A murmur went down the bleachers,
and a man down front said, "Affirmed what?"
Tick had his mouth open to say it, with the phone still at his ear.

Tunk Ferrell stood up in the third row.

"Affirmed means she was right," Tunk said,
loud enough for Tick's mic to catch it.
"They took the whole thing apart and looked at every piece of it,
and she was right the first time.
Now it's wrote down, and nobody can take it back."

Dan had heard Tunk have most of the town's stories first,
and he had not heard him have many of them right.
He had this one exactly.
Earlene turned on the bench and looked up at Tunk
as if she had never seen him before.

Then the gym went up.
The top row stomped, and then every row did.
The noise came off the bleachers and the wood floor all at once,
loud enough that Dan felt it in his shins.

Sonny Dillard took his cap off first.
He did it without a word at the end of the row
and held it flat against his coat.
Peanut pulled off his watch cap
and stood with it in the fist that had his keys.
Tunk took his own off, still on his feet,
and looked at it as if he had just found it on his head.
Earlene reached up and pulled the red knit hat off.
Her gray hair came up with static and stayed up,
and she did not fix it.
At center court Tick set the phone down,
took off his cap and laid it on the mixer.
The whole Liars' Table was bareheaded in a high school gym for the doctor.
Down the bleachers a man Dan did not know saw them and took his off too,
and then a woman two rows up did, and then others.
The money that wanted her rooms had a crew out on her ground this minute,
and the county had just taken its hats off for her.

Dan laughed.
It came out of him before he knew it was coming,
one short sound, and his eyes stung with it.
He was so glad for her he could have sat down on the gym floor.
His hands would not keep still,
so he put them in his coat pockets, and they shook in there.
He thought of her hearing *affirms* in the review's cold room,
her palm flat on the tabbed record and her knees locked,
and the breath going out of her all at once.
He wanted to be the first face she saw when she looked up,
and he would not be, and he was glad anyway, all the way down to his hands.

Her record was clean.
A clean record meant the offer out west still stood, he guessed.
He had no business guessing at it.
Whatever she did with it was hers.

Tick pulled the mic close and did not look at his sheet.

"Friends, I've had a fax on this table all morning
waiting on a signature," Tick said.
"The one that counted this year didn't come today.
It got signed on Thursday the seventeenth of December,
and it wasn't a boy's.
It was the doctor's, and it said no.
Every one of us has argued with that signature at one time or another.
I have.
It held."

Tick's voice caught at the end, on the air, and he let it.

Earlene put the red hat back on crooked and leaned over the rail.

"Your doctor's clean, Coach," she said.
"Go on and look glad. Nobody's watching you but me."

The whole gym was watching him.
He looked glad anyway.
He could not have stopped it with both hands.

"Forty-six days since 7 sat," Peanut said down the row.
"Odell counted it this morning. He'll want it on the radio."

Tick heard him and put it on the radio.


The bulb went off at noon.
For half an hour Dan shook hands on the gym floor with people he did not know,
and they said the doctor's name to him like good news
they had been saving since December and had only now been let to spend.
Peanut went out first with his keys, and Sonny's pager did not go.
Tick found him at the scorer's table with a loop of cable over one shoulder.

"I read that boy going the other way on my own air, Coach," Tick said,
low, for Dan and not the floor.
"I'd rather have read anything else."

"You read it fair," Dan said.

Tick coiled the cable twice more and nodded,
and his mouth pulled down at one corner.
He had wanted the county to remember this show.
It would, both halves of it.

Dan took the index card out of his coat.
Under the class he wrote one line, *Wes Pruett — spot holds*,
and he put the card back where it had been.
Marisol had not asked him for it.

He was back up the hill at the Fieldhouse by one
and sat in his office with the door open on the corridor
while he wrote the class on the whiteboard in marker, name by name.
Three lines at the bottom stayed empty.
He looked at them and was glad anyway,
and it was the best hour he had had all winter.

He did not call her.
Nothing between them had been settled on Monday.
She had told him to go home, and he had gone,
and the terms held until the class closed at the end of the day.
He had kept them for seven weeks.
He could keep them one more afternoon.
He let himself think of her at the Court instead,
on Verna's step in the good jacket, with Verna glad beside her.
He sat at his desk with three holes in his class
and thought about a woman on a step,
and for an hour he did not want one thing he did not have.


At about three the athletic director came up the corridor
and stopped in Dan's open door.
He had come back from the review before noon
and had been at his own desk since, by the look of him.
He was still in the good coat from the morning,
and he had his phone in one hand, face down.
He did not come in and sit.
He looked past Dan at the whiteboard,
at the three empty lines at the bottom of the class.

"I heard the radio," the athletic director said.

"Most of the county did."

The athletic director came one step into the office and stopped.
He looked at the phone in his hand, and then at Dan.
His mouth had gone tight at one corner.
Dan knew that look.
It was the one a man brought out of a meeting
with news for somebody who had not been in it.
Dan stood up, and he did not know why he stood.
His hands found the edge of the desk.
A radio down the corridor had gone to a car commercial,
and a door shut at the far end of the hall.
The athletic director looked at the whiteboard again, and back at Dan.

"The chair out west called me at two," he said.
"Dr. Cole said yes, Dan. She took the job."

It went into him low, under the belt, like a hit he had not seen coming.
The breath went out of him and did not come back right.
His hands had gone tight on the desk's edge,
and he could feel the grain of the wood through his palms.
The office went very clear and very far off,
the whiteboard and the empty lines and the marker cap on the floor.
His knees wanted to sit him down.
He did not let them.
His heart was going hard and slow, a long way down in his chest.

"She sent it this afternoon," the athletic director said.
"The chair wasn't calling for a reference this time.
She wanted a release date.
Dr. Cole wants to start early, and we have to let her go."

Dan heard every word of it.
He could not have said one of them back.

"The chair said one more thing," the athletic director said.
"A donor here called her in January to speak well of Dr. Cole.
The man behind the new building's pledge."

The athletic director did not say the name.
He did not have to.
Dan knew it the way he knew a formation from its first step.
Rex Boyd had called out west and said kind things about her.
A doctor who took a better job out west was a problem that packed its own car,
and that was the kind of problem the man liked best.

"I thought you'd rather hear it from me than off the radio,"
the athletic director said.

Dan nodded.
He could not find anything to say to that.
The athletic director stood a moment longer
with the phone face down in his hand,
and then he went back out and down the corridor slowly,
the way a man walks when he has one more call to make.

Dan sat down.
The terms lifted today, when the class closed, in a couple of hours.
He had kept every one of them for seven weeks,
across the square and at her car window and on Tick's air,
and they ended on the afternoon she said yes to somewhere else.
He had said her whole name in front of the county on Monday.
He had not asked her to stay.

The clean record he had bought her was the thing that let her go.
He had wanted it for her more than he had wanted the class,
and he would want it again tomorrow.
What he was afraid of was not the job out west.
He was afraid she would pull out of Verna's gravel
with the car loaded to the roof,
believing nobody in this county had wanted her to stay,
because nobody had said it to her.
He had not said it.
The gladness from noon was still in his hands.
The yes sat under it, low and cold, and would not move.

His hand went to the back of his neck,
to Odell's line, six days old and still straight.
She had laughed at that haircut behind her wrist in a dark room,
at the worst time in the world,
and for a second they had only been glad of each other.
It was the last time he had heard her laugh.
