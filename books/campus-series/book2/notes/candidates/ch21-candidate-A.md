Aisha Cole carried the chair out of her office one-handed,
the chest strap in the other hand,
and set it in the annex hall for Missy Gault.
It was Wednesday the thirteenth of January, a little after two,
and the sky over the practice field had gone the flat gray
the board had promised.
Missy sat on it outside the quiet room, put the notebook on her knee,
and looked at the strap in Aisha's hand and not at Aisha.
Her face was the one she brought to every check,
level and shut, a face that had driven up the hill to be told a number.
Inside the room Trey Gault sat the annex's own bike
in the gray hoodie with the hood up and his back to the door.
The trainer stood in the room with him, against the wall,
where he had said he would be.

The bike was not a step.
There was no practice until spring workouts,
and nothing on her ladder to put him in,
so today was the check she owed his chart before there was one.
Twenty minutes under load, with a number on it,
so that when a practice existed the page would already say what he was.
She said that much to Missy in the hall,
and Missy wrote nothing yet.

"The cap's the same," she said to the trainer. "One-forty."

He nodded. He had heard it in December and did not need it twice.

She went in and put the strap on him herself,
her hands quick under the back of the hoodie where it rode up.
The tablet in her other hand found it and gave her a number.
Trey did not turn around.
She had the tape on her left wrist, fresh, nothing written on it,
and she went around the front of the bike to where he could see her.

"Go," she said.

She asked at four, and at eight, and at twelve.
He answered short, the same word each time, and she did not write the word.
She wrote the number beside each minute on the tape,
one twenty-nine, one thirty-three, one thirty-one, all under the cap,
and the cadence did not move.
At fourteen she watched his right hand.
In December it had come off the bar at fourteen and gone up under the hood.
Today it stayed on the bar.
Fifteen. Sixteen.
She wrote 16 and looked at the trainer, and the trainer was looking at the hand.
Eighteen. Twenty.

"Time. Feet down, and stay put."

She took the number off the screen with her thumb
and wrote 20 on the tape, and 2:31 under it.
Then she typed it standing, with her back to the room, before she said a word.
Twenty minutes at the cap, no symptom, cadence held.
Six more than December. Re-evaluation under load, complete.
Not cleared, because there was nothing to clear him into,
and she typed that too, so the page could not be read as anything else.

Dan was across the lot in his building with his staff in it.
The trainer would carry this to his door at half past with the cart,
and say it standing, and Dan would say all right and nothing else.
She knew the face he would say it with.
It had taken a fourteen in December from the far side of a sheet,
and she would have liked to be in the hall when it took a twenty.
That was the one thing about this room she could not have,
and she had built the room that way herself.

"He's done," she told the trainer. "Take him out to Missy."

"I'll walk 7 out," the trainer said, and did.

Missy stood up in the hall with the notebook open and the pen out.

"What step does it say."

"It doesn't say a step. There isn't one to say till spring.
It says twenty minutes at the cap, no headache, and the time.
Two thirty-one."

Missy wrote. Aisha could read it upside down.
The first word was *Twenty*, in the straight hand,
and Missy underlined it once, which she had never done to a number.
Her mouth stayed where it was.
Her shoulders came down about an inch,
and she did not look up to see whether Aisha had seen them do it.

"Then whatever he tells you tonight, and when," Aisha said.

"I know the order."

The hood went down the corridor ahead of the trainer,
and Missy put the pen in the spiral of the notebook and went after it.


Back in her office at the warm end of the annex, at a quarter to three,
Aisha sat at the one desk the boxes had left her.
The tablet was flat on it with Trey's chart open.
The room was cardboard on three sides, taped and numbered in his marker,
and the desk was still a desk because she had said it stayed.
The twenty would go on the chart, and the chart would go where charts went.
That was the work.
The thing she did next, nobody had asked her for.

She opened a new file beside the chart and put his whole name on it.
Then she started building it, in order, every page a scout would want.
A scout was a man the teams that paid players sent to watch one,
so he could go home and write down what the player was worth.
By spring there would be one at the fence with a notebook of his own.
The first thing he would want to know about Trey Gault
was what had happened to his head in November and who had fixed it.
So she gave him both scans, August and December, her own order both times.
She gave him the tape times, fourteen and twenty, with the dates.
She gave him every rung of the ladder in her own typing,
each one dated, each one held overnight before the next.
The hold in December she wrote where a man could not miss it.
Read straight, the page said a program had sat its quarterback for a playoff
because his doctor would not put a healing brain on a field.
Read straight, that was the reason a team could draft the brain
and not the reason it could not.

It would cost her the evening, and it could cost her more than that.
If the board ever learned what was on this screen,
the board would say she had sat Trey in December to raise his price.
She knew that and typed the next line.

Missy Gault was in the door with the chair.
She had carried it back down the hall herself, one hand on the back of it,
the notebook flat against her coat in the other.
She stood there holding it and looked past Aisha at the screen.
The screen had Trey's name at the top of a file that was not his chart,
both scans under it, and the word *scout* in the first line.
Missy could read upside down too.

"That's for who," Missy said.

"For whoever's at the fence in the spring. A scout.
Nobody's asked me for it."

"No," Missy said. "They wouldn't have."

She came in and set the chair down where it had come from,
square to the wall, and took her hand off the back of it.
Then she stood there a minute longer than the chair needed.
She did not say thank you. She did not say anything.
She nodded, once, at the screen and not at Aisha, and went.
Her car went down Millrow with Trey in it
while the hill was still only wet.

The file stayed on the tablet.
It was not done, and it was hers until it was.
When it was whole it would go into Missy's hands and nobody else's.
Birmingham had needed a release with Trey's name on it before it saw a page,
and no scout at any fence would have one.
Nothing left this room without that paper.

Dan had started to ask her about the bike on Friday, at his own table,
with his spoon in the air, and she had stopped him.
Here was the answer to more than he had asked,
on a screen in a room he would not come into.
The one person who could not tell him it existed was building it.
That was the wall doing what she had built it to do.
It did not make the evening any shorter.


At four the window over the desk had the practice field in it,
and the field had gone white along its edges.
Aisha stood at the window with the tablet under her arm.
It had started as rain, and it was not rain now.
It came down fine and slanted and did not bounce,
and where it landed on the lot it stayed.
Across the lot the Fieldhouse had sent its people down before dark.
The staff cars had gone off that side of the hill one at a time
while she typed.
Dan's truck was still nosed to the fence over there, alone.
The film-room window was lit, and he was in it.

The trainer came to her door at half past four in his jacket.

"There's nobody left over here but us," he said,
"and nobody over there but Coach.
I'm leaving my car where it sits. I'd leave yours."

He had driven up at noon in daylight, the same as she had.
Nothing had been down Millrow since the rain turned.
She knew that hill with the sun on it, both directions,
and it had never been anything but a road.
Dan had gone down it every winter night he had lived in this town,
in the dark, and come back up it.
That was the reason, all of it, and it was a person's reason,
and she said it to herself once and left it there.

At five to five Dan came across the lot on foot,
no hat, the sideline jacket zipped to his chin.
He came to the warm-end door and not the other one.
She had it open before his hand was on it.
His face was wet from the walk and red across the cheekbones,
and there was ice in the front of his hair.
He looked at her in the door with the corridor light on her
and did not smile, and then did, with the cold still on his face.

"Doc. I'm the last one up here.
The truck's warm, and it'll do the hill.
I'll take you both down, him first, and I'm not going to argue about it."

"Nobody was going to argue with you," the trainer said behind her.

She did not want to be in his truck where the county could see her,
and she wanted to be in his truck, and there was no other truck.
She got the parka off the hook and put the hood up and went out to it.


The cab of the F-150 was a bench seat, a heater going full,
and one light on the dash.
It smelled of him and of cold vinyl.
Aisha had the tablet in her lap.
Dan Merritt was behind the wheel with one glove off and one on,
the bare hand on the wheel and the gloved one on his knee.
The trainer had taken the door side without a word,
so she was in the middle,
with nowhere to put her knees that was not against a man.
There was a belt for the middle seat.
She found the buckle under the trainer.

"Nobody's ever used that one," Dan said.

"Nobody's ever been in this truck," the trainer said.

It was flat and true, and Aisha laughed out loud,
and the trainer looked at her as if he had not meant it to be funny.
She reached for the buckle, and her hand came down on Dan's thigh instead,
and stayed there a second before she knew what it was on.
She took it back.
He looked straight ahead.
Her face was hot inside the hood before she had decided anything about it.
Neither of them said a word, and then he took the truck out of the lot.

He took it slow, nose down toward Millrow,
and the wipers went, and the ice on the glass went with them.
His face in the dash light was set, eyes on the road and nowhere else,
and the bare hand stayed at the top of the wheel.
Past the stadium's north side the hill fell away under the headlights,
the road one gray sheet with the center line gone under it.
There were no tracks on it in either direction.
He let the truck walk.

"You've driven this on ice before," she said.

"Not with anybody in it," he said, and did not look away from the road.

At the bend above Delmar's the back end moved a little,
and his hand moved a little and then stopped,
and none of the three of them said so.
Her shoulder was against his the whole way down.
There was no place else for it to be,
and the trainer's shoulder was against her other one,
and the heater blew on her knees.
She could not have said afterward which side of her was warm from the heater.

At the square the streetlights were on and nothing was under them,
the whole square shut and white, the diner's window dark.

"Corner's fine," the trainer said. "I can walk from the corner."

"On this?"

"I've walked on worse."

Dan stopped the truck there, and the trainer got out into it.
"Coach," he said, and gave her a nod, and shut the door,
and went off along the storefronts with his hands in his jacket.

Then it was the two of them.
It was a minute from the square to the Magnolia Court, less,
and Dan drove it at the speed the road wanted and not one bit faster.
She did not slide over to the door.
She stayed where the trainer had left her, against him.
He turned into Verna's lot and stopped in front of nine,
and left the engine running, and his hand came off the wheel.

The heater ran. The wipers had stopped.
Verna Poteat was in the office window at the end of the row,
a shape with the light behind it, and the shape did not move.
Dan looked at the windshield, and then he looked at her.
His face was the one from his own kitchen with the spoon in the air,
the one that had lost the sentence.
He took the other glove off with his teeth and put it on the dash.
The bare hand came down on the seat beside hers, not on it, an inch off.
Her hand had gone still on her knee on its own,
and her pulse was in her wrist under the tape, and it was over the cap.
She looked at his hand, and he looked at her looking.
Then she turned her hand over and put it on the seat, palm up,
and his came over it, and closed.
Nobody on the square or in that office window could have seen the hand.
It was below the dash, between two coats,
and while it lasted it was the only thing in the truck.

"I'm not getting out," he said.
"I want you to know I thought about it."

"I know you thought about it."

"Verna's at her window," she said.

"I know where Verna is," he said, and did not take the hand back.
"After signing day. That's February.
It's three weeks from tonight, and I've been counting,
and I'm going to keep counting from right here."
His thumb went once across the back of her hand and stopped.
"Three weeks, Doc. Go in before Verna has to pretend any harder."

She laughed, and it came out wrong, too high for the cab,
and he grinned at it, and the grin stayed on his face.
She took her hand out from under his to get the door,
and the cab was colder for it.

"Go up slow, Dan," she said.

"I've got the hill. Go in."

She got the door open and the tablet under her arm
and got down into the sleet, and shut it.
He did not back out until she had her key in the door of nine.
Then the lights swung off the row of doors,
and the truck went out of Verna's lot and up Millrow, slow.
She stood inside nine with her back on the door.

Nobody had broken the rule.
The trainer had been in the cab for the hill, and the weather had put him there.
It had been the minutes it took and not one more,
and the hand had been under the dash where the county could have looked
and seen nothing.
And a two-door truck in Verna Poteat's lot after dark
was still the thing anybody could see.
That was the cost. It was not a break.
She said the difference to herself,
and went and got out of the parka.


Thursday morning at six Aisha was in room nine with her boots on,
sitting on the end of the bed under Verna's blanket.
The tablet was on her knees and the curtain was open on the gravel.
The hill was still glass.
No car had been on it since last night, and none was going to be today,
and the annex was shut because nobody could get to it.
The RAV4 was up there at the warm end with ice on it.
The space in front of nine, where it lived every night the town was home,
was empty when Verna's office light came on at a quarter past.
Anybody who looked at that space would know the car had not come home.
The furnace was running behind. The wind had come north in the night.

She read the board.
It was mothers in three states about the ice, and a flight, and a dorm,
and then it was not.

No photograph. No name under it that belonged to a face she knew.
Nothing anybody could take to a table and put a finger on.
It was the board's own kind, the kind that named nobody and meant two people.
The board had been putting Coach and the doctor in one sentence since fall,
so it did not have to say which two.
In December a truck with the wrong number of doors
had taken the two of them out of one picture,
because Coach drove a two-door and always had.
Last night the two-door truck had been in Verna's lot with her in it,
and it was the same truck, and now it put her there.
She read it twice and put the tablet face down on the blanket and held.
She did not type. Three weeks.
There was a thing she would not have said to anybody,
and she said it to herself with her boots on at six in the morning.
She would have gotten in that truck
with no ice on the hill and no trainer in the cab.
She would have gotten in it at noon on the square.

Missy's page came at seven, a photograph of the notebook,
the straight hand, *Twenty* underlined at the top of it.
Slept eight hours. Headache zero. Nothing after the bike. Held.
Aisha sent back the two words.
*Good record.*

At half past seven she put the parka on and went out to look at the hill,
because there was nothing else to do with a shut county.
Verna Poteat slid the office window open as she went by it.
Verna had her robe on, her reading glasses, and the radio going low,
and she looked at the hill and not at Aisha.

"County's shut," she said.
"They'll want that boy back the day it thaws.
Healing's the one thing in this county that won't hurry for football.
It never has, and it's not going to start for them."

She shut the window.
She had not said a word about the truck,
and she was not going to, and Aisha did not answer her.

The tracks were still there in the gravel in front of nine,
two of them, frozen in where the truck had stood with the engine running.
The sleet had filled them and not covered them.
Anybody walking to the ice machine could count them.
She stood by them with her hands in the parka and thought about Dan,
and it was plain.
He had come across the lot on foot in the ice to get her home,
and he had not gotten out of the truck.
In December he had said February, and he kept a date.
What she was afraid of was not the post. The post was a post.
She was afraid the county would read the truck
and put the ride and the hold in one sentence.
That sentence would land on a table in February with his name on it,
and she would have handed them the truck herself, by getting in.
Then it was him again.
He had taken his glove off with his teeth to hold her hand.
The tracks in the gravel were his,
and three weeks was a number she could write on a wrist.
She left the tracks where they were and went in.
