Sonny Dillard was tapping the office step of the Magnolia Court
with the edge of a trowel at seven on Monday morning,
working along the crack the freeze had put in it,
and the bag of quick-set by his boot was still shut.
Aisha came down the row from room nine with the tablet under her arm
and stopped where the gravel met the step,
and over Sonny's head the sign said MAGNOLIA COURT in pink
and said nothing under it.
On every Monday she had lived at this motel
the word under it had been VACANCY, lit.
It was the fourteenth, with five days left in the ladder's week,
and at seven forty-five the ladder asked Trey Gault's brain
its first question, twenty minutes on a bike in a quiet room,
hers to run.
If it held, the week still reached Saturday.
If it did not, the week did not,
and this town had a December booked on it.

Verna had the office window up two inches and the phone at her ear.
"No," she said into it. "Full. Through Saturday.
I've been full through Saturday since the Sunday they said our name,
and the hotel is, and so is anybody with a bed in this county."
She hung up and put her face to the gap.
"Fourth call since six. Morning, my winter doctor. There's coffee."

Aisha took the cup through the storm door, what was left of yesterday's.
The neon was still on against the gray,
and one RV sat early on the grass by the ice machine
with frost on the windshield and a plate from two states over.

"Seven Saturdays is my year," Verna said through the gap.
"Seven home Saturdays, and I run the other forty-five weeks
on what they leave behind.
This is an eighth, and it's December, and I have never had one.
You're in the back room Friday and Saturday both this time.
I've moved you already."

"I hadn't heard."

"You're hearing."
Verna looked at the parka's cuffs, pinned back where she had put them,
and kept looking.
"And the other thing.
Earlene counted the doors on that truck Saturday night. Four.
Coach drives two, and has since he got here.
The Table's done with it, and the county will be by Wednesday.
That's for you to know before they know you."

She had read B. Hollis's post herself at midnight.
She had needed somebody in this town to say it out loud,
and Verna just had, through two inches of window.

Sonny stood up off his knees and looked at the step,
and at the sky over the ice machine, going gray to white,
and did not pick up the bag.

"Not today, Doc."

"You brought the bag."

"Bag came off my own shelf on the way to open.
It'll go back on it."
He turned the trowel over.
"Water got in a hairline and froze, and now it's two steps.
Quick-set's a name on a sack.
Under forty it don't cure, it quits.
Sets up on top, stays wet under, and the first boot on it, it's gravel.
Sun's on this step by ten.
If it's forty by then, I pour. If it isn't, it waits till it is.
You don't rush concrete. Concrete don't know what day it is."

He was talking about a step.
A brain cured the same way, from the outside in, on its own clock,
and Trey Gault's had a bike waiting on it in forty-five minutes
to find out how far along it was.

The tablet lit under her arm.
Seven o'clock, Missy Gault's page, a photograph of the notebook,
the lines the same as every morning for a week.
Slept. Headache zero. No screens past what she'd allowed.
Seven nights at step one. Held.
*Good record,* Aisha sent back, the two words,
and finished the coffee on the gravel.
Let me be in the room, he had said Saturday, across her desk.
Not you.
He was not going to be in it. She had been right about that,
and at seven forty-five she would go be right about it
in an empty room with a bike in it.

***

The rehab room beside the whirlpool room held the annex's own bike
and one chair and nothing else,
and it was the quietest room she had,
because there was nothing in it to make a sound.
The cold of the corridor came under the door and stayed low.
Trey Gault was on the bike in a gray hoodie with the hood up,
the chest strap under it paired to her tablet,
and what she had of him from the chair was the back of the hood,
the shoulders, the cadence of his feet, and a number.
The trainer stood in the door with a towel and said nothing,
and Missy sat in the hall behind him
with the notebook open on her knee, level,
watching the door and not the bike,
because Aisha had asked them both for that.

"One-forty," Aisha had said, to the room, before he got on.
"He doesn't go over it. The tablet tells me if he does.
Twenty minutes. I ask how his head is, he tells me,
and nobody in this hall says the word Saturday."

Merritt had told her in a hospital lounge
that Trey would not tell her the truth about a headache,
and she had not built a test that needed him to.
She asked at four minutes, and at eight, and at twelve,
and the answers came back the same length every time.
The number sat under the cap and did nothing.
At fourteen the feet changed.
The cadence came off, not much, and a hand came up
to the back of the hood and stayed there,
and the number went on not knowing anything.
She asked. He told her before she had the question all the way out.
She wrote 14 on the tape on her wrist.

"Off," she said. "That's it. Feet down. Sit there a minute."

The trainer came in with the towel.
"Want me to log the strap?"

"Log it."

She let the number come down on the tablet and charted while it did.
*Step two, minute fourteen.
Headache, reported on inquiry. Stopped.
Stepped down to one. Clock restarts.*
Her name under it, and the time.

Missy had the pen up in the hall and had not written anything.

"What do I write."

"The time. That he stopped at fourteen, and that I stopped him.
Tomorrow he's a student again. Half days. You meter the screens.
Twenty-four hours after a symptom-free day, we try two again."

"So not Tuesday."

"Not Tuesday."

Missy wrote it and shut the notebook on the pen.
"He'll want to know what it means."

"It means his brain isn't done. That's all it means. Tell him I said so."

He had asked to stand in the back of this room and say nothing.
She had said no, and no had been right; a quiet room meant quiet.
She would not have told anybody
that for one second at minute fourteen,
with the pen on the tape,
she had wanted him in the back of it anyway,
saying nothing, seeing what she saw,
so that at three o'clock she would not be the only one who had.

At her desk at a quarter past eight,
with the corridor's cold at her back and the heater in front of her,
she put it in the chart the long way,
indication, rationale, her name,
and the whiteboard stood beside the desk on his folded card, level,
his date circled at the top of a ladder
that had just gone back to its bottom rung.
She did the count once, to herself, and did not put it on the board.
It came out where she had told him Saturday it would come out
if any day went wrong.
The circle had not moved. The ladder had.

***

She crossed the lot at three instead of waiting for five fifteen,
because the sheet was going to say it in two words
and he should hear it in more than two.
The carillon struck three up the hill as she went,
and December light lay low and flat across the lot,
an hour and a half of it left.
The Fieldhouse door was open, and the hall doors were open,
and the film room's door was open,
because Dan Merritt had a rule about doors in his building,
and she had known the rule since her first week.

Merritt's offensive coordinator was in the door with his arms crossed
and a laptop under one of them, half in and half out,
talking to the whiteboard,
where Merritt stood with a marker and his sleeves pushed up.

"Boyd's people called again.
They want the rendering cued on the video board for after the game,
and they want the suite list by Wednesday."

"If," Merritt said, to the board.

"I told them if. They heard when."

The coordinator uncrossed his arms, saw her in the hall,
and went out past her without a word.

She knew the rendering.
It was on the wall behind Merritt's shoulder,
taped beside the depth chart with one corner curled,
three stories of glass with a sunset in it,
the drawing she had looked at twice for her rooms and not found them in.
So on Saturday night, if they won, that drawing went up on the video board
over ninety thousand people,
and there were people coming to sit in a suite and watch it go up.
That much had been said in the door, and nothing else.
And at the top of his own whiteboard, in his own block letters,
DEC 19, circled once.
She had figured block letters on the first of the month
and had not needed to come across the lot to be right about it.

He put the cap on the marker.

"Doc."

"Coach." She came in as far as the table.
"I'm not waiting for five fifteen on this one.
You should hear it in more words than the sheet's got.
He rode fourteen minutes under the cap and the headache came back.
He told me when I asked. I stopped it.
I stepped him down at eight this morning and the clock started over.
Likely out. That's the most I'll say,
and I'll tell you why the word is likely."

"Tell me."

"Because I don't call a Saturday on a Monday,
and because the count calls it, not me.
Tomorrow he's a student again, and it holds overnight.
Wednesday is two, at the earliest. Thursday three. Friday four.
Saturday is five. Saturday is the game."

He did not say anything to that for a while.
He looked up at the circle on his own board,
and whatever a man does in his head with a date he has circled in red,
he did it there with the marker still in his hand,
and she could not read it.
Then he looked at her.

"Walk me through Tuesday, medically."

She had come across the lot to tell a coach
that Trey Gault was likely out of the biggest game the program had ever played,
and he had asked her to teach him the answer
instead of telling her she was wrong.
It was the first question he had ever asked her
that was not the other end of an argument.
She found that she wanted to answer it,
and that wanting to was the more dangerous thing.

She brought the tablet up and did not go to the projector.
They were Trey's pictures, and they were going to be read
at the distance one screen is read from.
The window light came in flat and the screen threw it back,
so she turned the tablet toward the wall,
and he came around the table and bent to it,
because the tablet was at her height and not his,
and that put his head beside hers at reading distance.

"The twenty-eighth, from the hospital. Clean.
Clean doesn't clear him. I told you that at the hospital."
She swiped.
"The third. Baseline-plus.
Everything August took, taken again, and the two sequences August didn't.
Also clean.
I ordered it so nobody in January could say we didn't look.
It's the best picture anybody will ever have of that brain,
and the answer isn't in it."

"Then where is it."

"In the fourteen minutes.
The bike isn't a test he failed, Coach. It's the question.
A quiet room, a heart rate that can't run off from me, nobody talking scheme,
nothing for a brain to do but move blood and see if it minds.
At fourteen minutes it minded. That's the answer.
It's not a setback.
It's the first true thing that brain has said to anybody in two weeks."

He put two fingers on the bottom edge of the tablet
to hold it out of the glare,
and the side of his thumb lay along her knuckle while he did it,
skin, for the second it took,
and it was the tablet's doing and nobody's,
and nobody said a word about it,
and she went on to the next image.
The air he breathed out went across her cheek at that distance,
every time he said anything and once when he did not,
and it was warm,
which was a stupid thing not to have expected.
From a foot off, the side of his head was a thing she had not looked at before.
Dark hair, cut short, no cap over it for once.
She was looking at it instead of the scan.

"You said he'd lie to me about a headache."

"I said he'd tell you whatever got him back in a huddle."

"There wasn't a huddle in the room."

"No," he said. "There wasn't."

"Why fourteen and not four."

"Nobody knows. The picture can't tell me.
He can, if I ask him at the right minute and he answers. He answered."

"And Tuesday. What does he do."

"He goes to class. Half a day if his mother says half.
No screens past what she allows. He sleeps.
Missy sends me a page at seven Wednesday,
and if it's clean and it held overnight,
he's on the bike again Wednesday at seven forty-five."

"And if Wednesday holds."

"Thursday's three."

"And Saturday's five."

"You can do the rest of that yourself."

Every argument across a sheet since June,
every Friday at a quarter past four and every check at five,
had been the two of them learning how the other one thought,
and this was what it had been practice for.
One screen. Two heads. Him asking.

Then a cart came down the hall outside the open door,
one wheel dragging and letting go, dragging and letting go,
and a man with a cart and a bin on it looked in
at the two of them bent over one screen,
said "Coach," and went on by,
and the wheel went with him.
Every door in this building stood open
because the man beside her had said so,
and she had not, till this minute, had a reason to mind it.

She turned the screen off.
That took the reason for the distance with it,
and she stepped back to the far side of the table,
because one of them had to and it was her tablet.
He straightened up and let her.

"Tuesday he's a student," he said.

"Tuesday he's a student."

"I've held a meeting room at seven all month
for twenty minutes that aren't coming."

"Not this week."

"Not this week."
He put the marker in the tray.
"Sheet's at five fifteen."

"Same as always. It'll have a new word on it."

On her way past the window she looked out of it,
and there was her building from his side of the lot for the first time,
the annex, the light on at her end,
and the RAV4 where she had left it by her door,
and the whole low length of the building she had until February.
It looked small from here.

Under Trey Gault's, for the first time since she had started typing it,

*Stepped down.*

She was in her office at the warm end of the annex at twenty to six,
with the corridor dark behind her and the heater going.
The tablet lay on the desk with tonight's sheet still up on it,
two words under Trey's name,
and the whiteboard stood beside the desk on his folded card, level,
with his date circled at the top of it in her red.
She had put that circle up on the first of the month
so she could watch it work.
It had worked.
It had not moved the ladder an inch, and the ladder had not moved it.

She named it, once, sitting down, in her own words,
because there was nobody in the corridor to hear.
She wanted Dan at reading distance again, with the next question,
and she wanted to be the one who had the answer,
and she wanted a door shut while he asked it,
and there was not a door in his building that shut.
That was as far as she let it go tonight.
It would keep. It was warm, and it would keep till Wednesday.

She opened Wednesday's page in the chart
and typed the time she wanted him on the bike,
seven forty-five, at the earliest,
and left it there, lit, for the morning.
