# Chapter 27 — The Hearing

POV: Aisha, third person limited, past tense.

> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Paula Driskell:** My daughter called to ask how long you boil an egg.
> Ten minutes later she called to ask how you know it's done. I told her
> it's done when you stop calling me.


Wet coats dripped on the tile of the hallway outside the trustees' boardroom,
and under the low talk of forty people
Aisha Cole could hear every drop.
The independent review sat this morning in the room behind her,
and it had one question to answer.
Had she been right to bench Trey Gault in December,
or had she cost the county a playoff for nothing?
Its findings came Wednesday the third.
If they went against her,
the record would say she had held a healthy boy
out of the biggest game of his life,
and that record would follow her to every job she ever asked for.

It was the first of February, a Monday,
and a cold rain had come in on every coat in the county.
The radiator under the hall window knocked twice and hissed.
Aisha stood with her back to the wall and the cold of it at her spine,
in the one good jacket she had brought to town in June.
She had packed it for rooms like this one and never needed it till now.
The wool coat was on the rod in room nine at the Magnolia Court.
The parka was folded on the top box in the back of her car.

Verna Poteat had driven her up in her own car,
let her out at the curb and not come in.
"I'd be no use to you in there," Verna had said. "I'd say something."
The RAV4 sat in Verna's gravel with everything Aisha owned in it.
A loaded car outside this building
would have told the county the rest before she opened her mouth.

Earlene Tatum came up the hall through the coats
in her Sunday coat on a Monday.
She stopped in front of Aisha and looked the jacket up and down.

"Now that's a jacket," Earlene said.
"All you ever did in this county was your job, Doctor.
This morning we get told whether a person gets to do that here."

Earlene wanted the county to answer that out loud,
and she wanted to hear the answer with her own ears from the front row.
She went in to get her seat.
Aisha felt it land warm in her chest, and she let it stay there.

Missy Gault sat on the bench by the boardroom doors
with Denny beside her, his coat buttoned to the top.
Missy had her purse on her knees and both hands over the clasp.
Her eyes were red at the rims.
She looked at Aisha once, and her mouth started a word and gave it up.
Missy wanted her son safe and she wanted him playing,
and this morning the county was going to make her pick one of them
out loud in front of the town she grew up in.

Marisol Pruett stood by the coat rack at the far end
with a folder under her arm.
She moderated the parents' board,
and her own boy was the walk-on that board had wanted off the roster
since December.
She caught Aisha's eye and gave her one short nod with her working face on.

The university's lawyer went by in a gray suit with no folder
and did not look at her.
The athletic director had not come.

Dan was not in the hallway, and she did not look for him.
She did not look at the stairs, or the far doors,
or the window onto the curb where a truck would pull up.
She knew his walk.
She had heard it in the annex corridor and on Verna's frozen gravel.
Twice a man's shoes came up the hall behind her,
and twice her heart went hard before she knew they were not his.
She had put this jacket on this morning thinking he might see her in it,
and she would not have told that to anybody alive,
not Verna and not Marisol and not herself out loud.
Her chest went tight on it anyway.
The terms held till Wednesday.
Nothing between them had been settled at her door on Thursday night,
and she had sent him home.

At nine the review sat.
The boardroom had a long table across the front for the reviewer,
and every one of the forty seats was full,
with people standing two deep along the back wall in their wet coats.
Earlene had the front row, center.
Aisha had a small table to herself facing the long one.
She had no lawyer.
She had never thought she would need one,
because the chart was supposed to be enough.
Everything said went into a transcript,
and she could hear the keys at the end of the long table taking it down.
Her stomach had been shut since six, and it stayed shut.

The reviewer said the rules once.

"This review sits under the rule the NCAA calls Independent Medical Care.
It gives a team physician what the NCAA's own words call 'unchallengeable'
and 'autonomous' authority over return to play.
Nobody here is asking whether Dr. Cole had that authority.
We're asking whether she used it well.
The review will hear anyone it chooses to, at my discretion.
If you want to speak, ask, and I'll decide."

The first question came before she had her hands settled on the table.

"Dr. Cole, the national checklist says a step usually takes a day,
and it lets a player ride through a mild headache on the bike.
The student didn't get on a bike until sixteen days after the hit.
When he got a headache at fourteen minutes,
you sent him back to the start.
Most programs would have had him further along by the nineteenth.
Why was yours slower?"

It was a fair question.
The reviewer wanted the true answer and did not care whose side it helped.
Her heart went up once at it, hard.
She had asked it of herself in December with the tape still on her wrist
and the bike's wheel still going around,
and she had given herself the answer she was about to give the review.
She stood up in the good jacket to answer it,
because she had always delivered standing.
Her pulse was going high under her collarbone, and she let it.

"Twenty-four hours is the least a step takes. It was never the most.
The patient's brain sets the pace, and I write down what it tells me.
The patient didn't get on a bike
until his headaches at rest had stopped and stayed stopped.
His mother's pages say when, every morning at seven."

Her voice held. Her knees were not sure they would.

"On the fourteenth of December, sixteen days out,
the patient was fourteen minutes into the bike
when his hand came off the bar and went up to his head.
It wasn't mild, and it wasn't brief.
It was more than the checklist lets a physician ride through.
So I did what the checklist says.
I stopped him, stepped him down, and started the clock over."

Fourteen.
Dan had said the number back to her in December, low,
reading her screen from a foot away,
and his breath had come warm against her cheek.
She could feel it there now, with forty people behind her.
She put it away and went on.

"Past two weeks, the checklist says re-evaluate.
I did, in January, under load, and it's dated.
The family took the patient to Birmingham for a second opinion.
I sent that office the whole chart myself, before anybody asked me to.
What Birmingham found is the family's to tell. It isn't mine."

"Walk me through the chart," the reviewer said.

She did it the long way, in order.
She gave the review the August baseline first, imaging and all.
Then came the scans from the twenty-eighth of November
and the third of December,
clean both times, because a scan does not see a concussion.
Every order was dated and signed.
The tape said fourteen minutes and seven fifty-nine.
Missy Gault's pages had come every morning at seven, in Missy's own words.
The January check under load had run twenty minutes at the cap
with nothing in it.
She said the patient every time,
so that his name would not sit in a transcript for good.
Trey was the one warm word she had brought to that table,
and she left it out, and she felt the cost of it in her chest.
Nobody noticed. Nobody was meant to.

"And the records?"

"The annex's forty-one boxes of records
have been locked at Mt. Nebo Missionary Baptist since December.
I counted them against Delores Fountain-Mabry's pad last week.
My count matches hers."

"The review will want to see that pad."

"It doesn't leave the church.
She told me the review is welcome to go over there and ask her for it."

Earlene laughed first, one hard bark from the front row.
Then the room went, all forty and the wall behind them,
and it was the first time Aisha had heard this county laugh since Christmas.
The reviewer wrote something down.
It might have been the church's address.
Her own mouth went up at the corner,
and the laugh loosened something in her chest she had not known was tied.

A trustee in the second row put up a hand, and the reviewer nodded at it.

"One thing for the record.
If she'd cleared that boy and he'd gone down on the nineteenth,
what would every one of us be saying this morning?"

Nobody answered.
The radiator knocked in the wall.
The trustee sat back and said nothing else.
The trustees would vote in February on whatever the athletic director
handed them,
and this one wanted that question sitting on the record
before anybody on that board voted on anything at all.
Her throat closed on it, and she kept her face still.

Behind Aisha a man said it low.
"Any other doctor had him back for that game."
Nobody hushed him either.
The county was split down the middle aisle,
and she could hear both halves of it breathing.
When she sat down, her palm was wet on the wood.

Marisol Pruett asked to be heard at a quarter to eleven,
and the reviewer let her.
She came up the aisle with the folder
and laid two pages side by side on the long table.

"I moderate the parents' board.
The families' letter was organized there,
so I have the families' own copy.
This other one is the consultant's printed materials.
They came out in the trustees' February agenda packet
for anybody to read."

She put one finger on each page.

"The same three words are in both. Proactive risk posture.
Those eleven families are honest people,
and they signed what they believed.
But eleven families didn't all come up with that phrase on their own.
Somebody handed it to them."

It was so quiet Aisha could hear the pages settle.

"I can't prove who wrote it.
Everybody in these seats has a name in mind.
I can't prove it's Rex Boyd's, so I won't say it."

The room made a sound Aisha had heard in hospital waiting rooms,
forty people taking a breath at once and not letting it go.
Her own breath went with theirs.
Marisol went back up the aisle to her seat.
She wanted the room to see those words had been handed to honest people,
and she had stood up in front of the whole board that campaigned
against her own son to show it.
She had not looked at one of those parents doing it.

materials in the trustees' February agenda packet]

Dan read that board like a chart he did not trust.
She had told him so on his couch on New Year's Eve.
She wished he could have heard Marisol read it back,
and the wish sat warm in her chest while the room found its voice again.


At twenty past eleven the doors at the back of the boardroom opened.
The noise of the hallway came in, and then the doors shut on it.
Aisha heard Dan Merritt's voice before she turned around.

"I'm not on your list," Dan said from the doorway.
"I'm asking to be heard."

She knew that voice all the way down.
It was the low one he used in the dark,
and it came up the room over forty heads and found her anyway.
Her heart hit the front of her chest so hard it hurt.
The skin on her arms went tight under the good jacket.

A chair scraped on the far side of the aisle.
The university's lawyer was half out of it,
one hand pushing off the table and his mouth open on nothing.
He wanted the coach silent, and the whole boardroom could see it.
The reviewer looked at the doorway for a long moment.

"The review said at nine it would hear whom it chose.
It'll hear the coach."

The lawyer sat back down. It took him a while.

Dan came up the aisle.
He was in a dark suit and a tie,
and he wore both like a man who had been ordered into them.
She had never seen him in a suit,
and she could tell from across the room that he hated it,
because it was in his shoulders and in the hand that kept going to the knot.
The haircut he had got for the signing pictures was still sharp at his neck.
He passed her table close enough that the air he moved
went across the skin on the backs of her hands, cold from the street.
He did not look at her.
He stood at the end of the reviewer's table, and he started.

"Dan Merritt, head football coach.
I want to add to my answer from last Monday.
The hold on my quarterback ran from the twenty-eighth of November.
Dr. Cole signed her no-go on Thursday, the seventeenth of December.
The families' letter reached the athletic director
the Monday after Christmas.
The picture went up on the eighteenth of January.
Her call came before either one of them."

Her lips moved on the dates with him.
She had typed every one.

"All season I pushed on her Friday medical sheet,
and I lost on the merits every time.
When she offered 7 twenty minutes in a room with no film,
I agreed to it to her face.
Then I went around her to the athletic director to get him the film.
The email that told me no is in your file. That one's mine."

Across the aisle, three rows back, a purse clasp clicked open and shut,
and open again.

Heat came up her neck into her face.
She had read that email on her tablet in December
and had not had him down as a man who went around people.
Now he was telling it to the county himself,
and she wanted to be on her feet to tell the rest of it.
He had given the meeting room back.
He had kept the projector unplugged when it counted.
She corrected people for a living.
She pressed both palms flat on the table until the wood pushed back at them,
because if she let her hands up they were going to put her on her feet
and she was going to say his name back at him across forty people
and tell him to stop.

"My staff did what I told them.
Any pressure on that sheet came from me. Put it on me."

Her chest hurt with it, a clean hard ache, and she held still.
The lawyer's pen tapped the table twice and stopped.
In the quiet after it she could hear her own heart over the keys.

"Last Monday this review asked me if I'd have played 7 on the nineteenth.
I said I would, and I let it stand,
and the county's had it on the radio all week."

He stopped.
She heard him take a breath, and the whole room heard it.

"I said I'd have played him. That's exactly why the rule says I don't decide."

The clasp across the aisle had quit clicking.

"Dr. Aisha Cole decides. She held my quarterback, and she was right."

Aisha looked across once.
Missy Gault's hand had stopped on the clasp of her purse and stayed there.
Her eyes were wet, and she did not wipe them.
Denny sat beside her looking down at his own two hands.
Missy did not get up.

On the radio in January Dan had said her protocol
and left her name off it.
Now it was on the record in his voice, all of it,
and she could hear the keys at the end of the table
take every letter of it down into the transcript for good.
Her breath went out of her and did not come back for a while.
Her throat shut on everything she might have said.
Her eyes went hot, and she kept them on the grain of the table.
An ache came up under her breastbone and spread
until her whole chest was full of it.
A muscle went in his jaw, once, and he held still.
Earlene Tatum looked from Dan to Aisha and back again,
and did not stop looking.

She had never let a man pay for anything of hers.
On Thursday she had told him she would rather be gone than be his price.
He had paid it anyway, standing up in a suit he hated,
with the university's lawyer across the aisle.
Every part of her that had ever been moved along out of a room
wanted to stand up now and take it back from him.
She wanted to say it better and make it cost him nothing.

She did not stand.
She sat with her hands flat on the table and let him.
It was the hardest thing she had ever done sitting down.
Her face was hot the whole time, and she did not hide it.

And she wanted him.
She wanted to go down the aisle in front of the county
and put her palm flat on his chest over that tie,
with Missy Gault, Earlene, and the lawyer all a few rows off
and able to read every bit of it.
She sat still with that too, and her heart would not come down.

The reviewer thanked him and called a recess.
Dan turned from the table.
He did not look at her going by.
He went out the side door at the front of the room,
and she saw him turn left in the corridor,
away from the front hall and the coffee, toward the back stairs.
She could hear his shoes go the length of it, and her heart went with them.

The forty went the other way.
They went out the back doors to the front hall and the coffee urn,
all of them talking at once.
The lawyer stayed up front with the reviewer, talking low.
Missy Gault did not get up.
She sat with the purse on her knees and her hand on the clasp.


Aisha gave it until the boardroom was half empty.
Then she went out the side door as if she wanted the restroom
and turned left.

The back stairwell was concrete and cold,
and every sound in it came back twice.
Dan stood on the landing halfway down
with his back to the wall under a window full of gray rain.
He looked up when he heard her on the top step.
Neither of them said a word.
His breath was the only other sound on the stairs.
If anybody came around that corner,
a coach and his team physician together two days before the terms ran out
was all the county would need,
and both of them would be gone that same day.
Nobody came.
The forty were at the urn in the front hall,
and from the stairwell they were a low noise a long way off.

She came down the steps one at a time,
and each one rang on the concrete and came back to her.
He did not move off the wall.
She stopped one step above the landing,
and that put her eyes nearly level with his.
He watched her the whole way, and his face was not set anymore.
It was the face from the cab, all of it.
His hand came off the wall and turned palm up between them, low, and waited.
She had put hers out that way on his couch once, and he had taken it.
She knew exactly what she was doing, and she could see he did too.

She put her hand in his.
The heat of it went up the inside of her wrist and into her chest,
and her breath stopped where it was.
His fingers closed over hers, slow, and held.
His palm was warm against the skin of hers,
and she could feel a pulse going in it.
She could not tell if it was his or her own.
Her knees went loose, and she put her other hand flat on the cold wall.
Her heart was so loud she thought the stairwell would give it back.
She wanted him, with the county one wall away.
Her car was packed in Verna's gravel.
She did not know where she would be on Sunday,
and she was holding his hand on a stair anyway.
His thumb moved once across her knuckles.
She counted it, because she counted everything.
One.

Then his fingers opened, and so did hers.
He went down the rest of the stairs without looking back,
and the door at the bottom let him out onto the street side
and swung shut slow behind him on its closer.
She stood on the step with her hand still warm
and listened to the stairwell go quiet around her.
Then they were apart again,
and she went back up to the forty with her breath not all the way down.


The review sat again after the recess and ran into the afternoon,
and the reviewer asked her about dates for an hour
while she answered every one of them from the chart standing up.
At half past two the reviewer said the review would sit again
Wednesday morning and give its findings after.
Marisol drove her back to the Magnolia Court at four
and did not ask her one thing the whole way.

Near dark Aisha was out on the new step at Verna's office,
in the good jacket, because room nine was bare to the walls
with everything of hers in the car and she could not sit in it.
Sonny's concrete had set hard in last week's sun,
and the cold came up through it into her knees.
Her breath went white in front of her.
Up the lot the ice machine let go a load of ice, and she jumped.
Dan talked to the one at the Fieldhouse. He had told her so.
She almost smiled at that, and then she did not.

Verna Poteat came out of the office in her cardigan
with her phone in her hand.

"The trustees posted their February agenda again this afternoon,"
Verna said. "It said one thing at breakfast. It says another thing now."

She held the phone out, and Aisha read it on the step.
The pledge item said *re-sequenced to a later sitting*.
The line for the coach's extension had been struck.
Re-sequenced meant the money was off the February table,
and nobody had said when it would come back.
The extension had been on that list this morning, and now it was not.
Nobody had to tell her why.

She knew what it cost him, and her chest ached with knowing it.
It was the only job he had ever wanted,
and every man on his staff had come to this town on his contract
and would be out looking for work by spring without it.
He had known that walking up the aisle in the suit.
Her stomach turned over, slow.
The palm he had held on the stair went hot and then cold,
and she shut her hand on it.

"I'd have bet money that man keeps his head down and signs," Verna said.
"I'd have lost it, my winter doctor."

Verna did not go back inside.
She wanted her winter doctor through the winter,
and standing on her own step in a cardigan in the cold
was as close as she was going to come to saying so out loud.

She had spent every job she ever had making sure nobody paid for her.
This morning a man had stood up in front of the county in a suit he hated
and paid it anyway while she sat at a table with her hands pressed flat
and let him.
She did not know yet what she owed him for it.
She did not know if owing was even the word.
On Thursday she had told him he would be paying all of it
for a woman with her car already packed.
*Then that's who I'm paying it for,* he had said.
What she was afraid of was plain.
He had spent his name in front of the whole county
on a woman who might be on the interstate Saturday morning,
and she did not know yet if she was going to be that woman.
Her heart went slow and heavy with it, and she let it.

The findings came Wednesday.
The yes to the job out west was still typed under the chair's email,
unsent, and Saturday was five days off.
The car sat loaded at the end of the gravel.
She did not go to it.

Verna stayed on the step beside her and did not say anything more.
Down on the road a truck came along slow and went on past the Court.
It was not his.
Aisha listened to it all the way out of hearing anyway.
