> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **GrammyInSectionC:** Ice is off my driveway and the car started on the
> first try. My grandson called to say he's fine, and for once he sounded
> fine. I'm mailing the sweater anyway.


The water came off the Magnolia Court's roof in one steady line
and hit the gravel where the ice had been.
It was the only sound outside.
Inside nine, Aisha Cole had her boots on already,
and her thumb was going down four hundred pictures
in the parents' board's playoff album.
Another room had found one of them first.
It was Monday the eighteenth of January, before six,
and the thaw had come over the weekend.
The hill had been clear since Saturday.
She had walked up Millrow on Saturday for the RAV4 and driven it down.
It stood in front of nine now with the thaw's grit dried on it.
The phone was the only light, on the blanket, on her two hands.
Across the gravel Verna's office was dark.

The other room on Grapevine, the one that was not about football,
had put the picture up overnight.
It sat under the local handle the room had known since the fall,
with one line typed under it and no names, the way that room talked.

> **AshfordBornAndBred:** 11 AM, December 19. Y'all remember what else
> happened that day.

Under it, from a handle she did not know, one more line:
somebody had gone through the whole album for that one.
Nobody had typed a name under either line. That room never had to.

The thread was *Playoff Pics — Post Here*,
pinned at the top of the parents' board since seven that morning,
and it had four hundred pictures of the lots in it.
One of them was this.
A boy of eight or nine stood on an ice chest in the stadium lots
with a poster-board sign over his head, WE DROVE 11 HRS FOR THIS.
Dan Merritt was leaning in toward the sign to read it.
She was at his shoulder in the parka with the cuffs pinned
and the lanyard out over it.
She was laughing with her whole face,
because the father had just said his mother vetoed the ticket.
The lid of the ice chest, tipped under the boy's boots,
had thrown the sun back up into all of them, one hard white flash.
The father's phone had caught it.
The album put a time on every picture as it went in.
The father had put his in from the lot, right then,
so the time on it was the time it was taken. Eleven.

Eleven in the morning on the nineteenth of December
was the morning of the game Trey Gault sat out.
She had signed him out of it on the Thursday, two days before,
and the hold itself had been decided the Monday before that.
The county did not care what order those had come in.
It had the doctor laughing at the coach's shoulder
on the morning of the hold, with a time on it.
It was going to read the laugh as the reason.
Her chart said what it said, dated, in her own typing.
Nobody was going to read the chart now. They had a picture.

What she remembered was not the flash.
It was the second after it, going past him toward the north stands, close,
her arm along his sleeve through the parka.
The nylon had run the whole length of her upper arm against his coat.
Her arm went warm now, under the blanket, where the sleeve had been.
The warm did not stay in the arm.
It went across her chest and sat there,
and her heart picked up under it until she could feel it in her throat.
Her breath went short and stayed short.
Her hands had gone tight on the phone,
and she told them to loosen, and they did not.
The skin along that whole side of her was hot,
in a cold room, at six in the morning, from a picture.
The picture was true.
She had wanted him at eleven in the lots with her face doing that,
and she wanted him now, and the county had it right.
It had it right about the wrong thing.
The laugh had nothing to do with the hold,
and it had everything to do with him.

She read the line under the picture once and did not read it again.
The phone went in the parka pocket.
Across the gravel Verna's office light came on at a quarter past six.
It lit the space in front of nine, and the RAV4 was in it, a full space.
Verna Poteat was a shape behind the blind for a second, and then not.
She wanted her winter tenant's rent and she wanted a quiet lot.
This morning the county was talking about the tenant,
and Verna had not said yet which of the two she wanted more.


By half past seven Aisha was driving up Millrow herself.
Every rut on the hill had water standing in it,
and the branches over the road had let go of their ice in the night
and hung there bare and dripping.
The annex lot at the top had water in it too, and mud at the edges.
Across the way, in the Fieldhouse lot,
the F-150 with two doors stood nose to the fence.
Dan was in the building it belonged to.
She parked at the warm end, where the RAV4 had sat out the storm,
and did not cross the lot, and said to herself why.
The class closed on Wednesday, the third of February. That was sixteen days.
A doctor who walked across the lot to the coach's window this morning
wrote the caption under the picture herself.
She was not going to spend him on that either.

The clinic was open.
The tank was running in the whirlpool room before she had the tablet out,
and the corridor floor had wet boot prints from the door to the near table.
The trainer had a kid on the near table with an ankle up on a bolster,
a freshman off the bottom of the roster.
It was the same ankle that had been on that bolster before the storm.
The kid's phone was on the table by his hip.
When she came through the door his hand went out and turned it face down.
Her stomach went over once, and she kept walking.
The trainer looked up from the tape.

"Doc," he said.

There was something under it, and he did not say what.
He went back to the tape.
The trainer wanted his job, and he wanted to be on nobody's side.
He had come down the hill on Wednesday
on the door side of the same bench she had.
He was not going to say one word about that, or about anybody's phone.
It showed in how hard he was looking at an ankle.
The picture was in every phone in the building.
She knew it from the one that had gone face down,
and from what was under the trainer's one word.

"Tank's at temp," the trainer said.
"Knee's in it at nine. This is the ankle from before the ice."

"I remember the ankle," Aisha said. "How does it feel with weight on it?"

"Better than Friday," the kid said. "I did the dorm stairs Saturday. Both ways."

"Two feet on every step till Wednesday, and slow.
After that you can be nineteen about it."

"Yes ma'am. Is it going to be all the way right by then?"

"It's an ankle. Ankles come all the way back if you let them.
Two feet on the stairs is letting them."

"Swelling?"

"Some at night."

"Then ice it at night. Twenty minutes, not the whole movie."

The kid laughed at that, one breath, and looked at the ceiling after.
He wanted the ankle back by spring,
and he wanted to be nobody's part of this morning,
and the ceiling was how he managed the second one.

"Coach told all the walk-ons we run in the spring
same as everybody," the kid said. "Last week. Before the ice."

"Then you'll run in the spring with two ankles," Aisha said.
"That's the plan for you."

"7's at three on the bike," the trainer said, to the tape.
"Same as Wednesday. You want the strap out?"

"I'll get the strap. Same cap as Wednesday."

"I'll be on the wall for it."

"The survey people called the desk," the trainer said.
"They're on the ground at eleven. They want the fence end of the lot clear."

"The lot's not mine to clear."

"I told them that. They're coming anyway."

That was the most he had said to her since the hill,
and none of it was about the hill.

Her office at the warm end had the desk and the boxes with her numbers on them,
and the last box had his number on it, in his marker.
She opened Trey's file, the record she had built on Wednesday for the spring.
Every rung, dated, the day it was set and the day it held.
The fourteen minutes in December and what she had done about it, which was stop.
Wednesday's twenty at the cap.
It was the way back through the hold, step by step, in her own typing.
It was the thing the county would read as bought now, if it ever read it at all.
She kept her face the way she kept it for a parent in a corridor, quiet,
and it cost her all morning to hold it there in her own clinic.
She closed the file and went back down the corridor to work.


At noon Aisha was at the desk in her office with the tablet,
and the parents' board had the picture under a real name.
Rhonda Sipes drove in from out of state for every home game
and had typed in December that this explained a lot about the hold.
She had wanted the hold explained since October,
and she had wanted to have been right about it in December.
Today she got both, and she put her own name on it to get them.
It was an ugly post, and the woman had signed it to be right,
and Aisha could pity that from a desk.
The picture sat under her name with the time typed over it,
and her December sentence under that, word for word.

> **Rhonda Sipes** 11 AM, December 19. If it's who I think it is, this
> explains a LOT about the hold.
>
> **PomPoms&Prayers** Praying for everybody in this. That's all I'm going
> to say.
>
> **Kendra Voyles** This is not about that, and I'd ask that the thread
> stay about the boys.
>
> **DossCoDadx3** Just asking for clarity, same as everyone. Nobody is
> accusing anybody of anything.

Three replies, small and polite.
No line on the thread had gone gray.

That was Marisol's room.
Marisol Pruett kept books for a living
and moderated the parents' board on her own time.
Her son Wes Pruett was the walk-on at the bottom of the roster,
the one the same board would like off it to make room for the class.
Marisol wanted her friend off that board and her son on that roster.
She could not have both, and she had priced each one.
If she cut the post, the room said by dark
that the mod deleted what she did not like, and it said it next about Wes.
If she left it, it stood.
She had left it. It stood.

Aisha typed one text and sent it before it could get longer.

*Leave it up. Don't spend Wes on me.*

Her thumb stayed on the glass after it went.
She held her breath for the three dots, and they did not come,
and she let the breath out on her own.
Marisol did not answer. The post stayed up.
She carried the sentence under her name where she carried a bad number,
low under the breastbone, and worked.

The screenshot came at half past twelve, in the corridor.
It was on the freshman's phone, the kid with the ankle,
going out on one crutch with his boot in his other hand.
He stopped and turned the screen toward her,
and he meant it kindly, and it was on his face that he did.

"Doc. Hang on.
You ought to see the one that's going around,
before somebody shows it to you to be mean about it.
I'm not showing it to be in it. I'm showing it so you know."

It was not the board. It was a forward of a forward.
The picture, and under it one line off some chat.
The names were cropped off the left edge so badly
that the first word of the line had gone with them.

*…so that's what the hold was worth.*

"I know what's in it," Aisha said. "I was in it. Where did that come from?"

"My mom," he said. "She's two states off and she had it by eight.
She wanted to know if I was in it."

"Were you?"

"No ma'am. I was in bed."

"Go do your stairs," Aisha said. "Two feet."

He went.
The polite version was on the board under real names.
The other one had been typed somewhere smaller before noon,
and it was in every side chat in the county by now.
Nobody would ever put a name to who had typed it first.


At two Aisha came out the warm-end door with the tablet under her arm.
The survey crew had been on the annex ground since eleven,
and she wanted to see for herself what they were doing to it.
The ground was mud where it had been ice on Wednesday.
Three men in orange vests had a tripod at the fence
and a line of pin flags going across the grass toward the lot.
The chalk on the curb was bare and blue in the flat light.
Delores Fountain-Mabry stood at the edge of it in a wool coat
with a legal pad and a pen.
Aisha's first thought was her boxes,
forty-one of them along a wall in the locked room at Mt. Nebo.
She thought Delores had come up the hill about her paper.
She was wrong about that.
Delores was the woman who had counted those boxes into Mt. Nebo's back room
with the pen and not with her face.
The aldermen sat tomorrow night, the third Tuesday.
She had come up the hill to count this ground herself.
Delores wanted the town counted honestly, and she trusted her own pen for that.

"How many flags have you got in the ground, son?" Delores said.

The crew chief looked at his sheet.
He wanted to be off the hill before the ground went hard again at dark,
and it showed.

"Twenty on the sheet, ma'am."

"I make it nineteen. Count them again. I'll wait."

"We set twenty. I set them myself Friday, before the ground let go."

"Then the ground let go of one."

He looked at the sheet again, and then at the grass,
and then he walked the fence line with his head down.
He came back with a pin flag that had gone flat in the mud by the post,
where the thaw had let it loose.
He set it and stepped the dirt down around it.

"Twenty," he said. "Ground's soft."

Nobody said anything to that.

"Twenty," Delores said, and wrote it.
"Now your sheet and my pad say the same thing,
and that's what I'll tell the aldermen tomorrow if they ask what's up here.
They'll ask."

"We'll be off the hill by dark, ma'am.
It's freezing again tonight, and I'd rather not set them twice."

"Nobody asked you to set them twice. I asked you to count them once."

The crew chief looked past Delores at Aisha, with the sheet still up.

"You the doctor?"

"I am."

"Thought so." He went back to the tripod.

Delores wrote one more thing on the pad,
and then she looked at Aisha over the top of the page, once.

"Doctor. I thought you'd be inside with the door shut."

"I wanted to see what they were doing to the ground.
What do the aldermen want with it?"

"Nothing yet. Somebody will stand up tomorrow and tell them what's up here,
and I'd like to know before they do whether it's twenty flags or nineteen.
Your paper's dry, by the way. I check that room Sundays.
How's the boy? The one on the bike."

"Trey's on the bike at three. That's all I can tell you, and it's all there is."

"That's what I asked.
That picture came to me twice before lunch,
from two people who don't speak to each other.
I looked at it once, and I put the phone down."

"It's a real picture," Aisha said. "I was standing right there."

"I can see that. I can see you.
A photograph doesn't lie, Doctor. It just repeats whatever it's told."

She went back to the flags, and nobody said anything else about it.

The class closed on Wednesday, the third of February, sixteen days off.
Saying the number to herself went through her low, under the ribs,
and stayed there.
The trustees sat that month with one page in front of them,
and her job was on the page under a politer name.
A partner group would run team medicine for the program,
and it rode in with the pledge for the building.
She had known that since December.
The letter had never been the thing.
A polite agenda needed a piece of paper to do a thing like that with,
and a photograph was paper.

Kat's text came while she stood there, from up the road.

*Honey. Three more. Every one of them has a February in it.
Call me when you're done being brave.*

She did not call. She went back in, because Trey was on the bike at three.


At nine at night Aisha sat on the end of the bed in nine with the phone,
and the furnace ran behind, because the wind was north.
Outside, under Verna's light, the RAV4 was the one car in the gravel.
The picture was open.
Under Rhonda Sipes's name the view count was most of the county,
and it was still going up.
The replies were still coming, one every few minutes,
praying, not about that, asking for clarity,
from mothers in four states who had never met her.
She was not alone. The county was on the glass with her.

Dan read the parents' board every night, every line of it.
She had told him once, at a rail, that he read it like it was a chart.
He was up the hill in his office or down at his house,
and she did not know which.
The same picture was on his phone in whichever building he was in.
She did not know whether he had opened it tonight.
The county had written a line under him once, ten years ago,
and that one had been true too.

She wanted his mouth.
She wanted his hands, the bare ones off the wheel,
and she felt the want in her own hands,
which had a phone in them and nothing else.
Her palms were hot on the glass.
The whole length of her right side remembered the bench and his arm along it.
It was warm there now, hip to shoulder, with nobody beside her.
The warm went down her ribs the same as it had on the hill,
and her heart came up with it.
Her throat was tight, and her breath was high in her chest.
Sixteen days had been possible on Thursday.
Tonight the county had said it for them, under her name and his,
and sixteen days of not crossing a lot would not unsay it.
Whatever they held for now would not read as holding.
It would read as hiding, and they would be hiding the one true thing.

She had let one person close.
She had done it in the lots at eleven with her face open.
It was costing her everything she had built here,
the chart, the clinic, the rooms, a job on a page in February.
Nobody had ever spent anything on her.
She had paid her own way through every building she had worked in.
This was the bill for once letting a man carry a box.
She would rather be the one who paid.
If the county wanted somebody to pay for the picture,
it could take it out of her, and not out of him, and not out of Marisol's boy.

She did not read Rhonda's sentence again. She looked at the picture.
Her laugh, his lean, the flash off the lid, the boy's sign over both of them.
It was open on her phone, on the blanket, in nine.
It was on his phone too, up the hill or at his house,
in a building she could not see from this window, opened or not.
It was the same picture on two phones in two buildings,
and the county was in both of them.
