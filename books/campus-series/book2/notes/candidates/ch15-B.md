# Chapter 15 — Precautionary Hold

> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Tim Brasher:** Wheels down Friday at four. The rental counter says
> no cars till Saturday. If you pass a man in an Ashford quarter-zip
> walking the highway in from the airport, that is me.


The pen was in her hand before the light was,
a clinic pen with the university's name half worn off the barrel,
and it had been over the same line on the same page
since a little after four,
at her desk at the warm end of the annex.
The page was paper, because the pen had decided that,
and ink was a thing nobody could open later and change.
The date at the top was the seventeenth, a Thursday, in her hand,
and the nineteenth was two lines under it.
Two days.
Trey Gault was not going to play on the nineteenth.
Wednesday's bike had held, and it changed nothing.
Wednesday was two. Today was three. Friday was four.
Saturday was five, and five had to sleep a night before it was six.
Sunday was the first day he could play.
Nobody played on a Sunday.
The county had a sentence ready for the doctor
who kept its quarterback off its one December field,
and by noon it would have her name to put in it.
She signed the page anyway, the long way, all of her name,
and set the pen down on top of it.

It said what she would have said on a field.
Trey Gault. Not cleared for competition on December nineteenth.
Return-to-play, step three of six. Each step holds overnight.
The board's phrase for what she did was not on it.
That word belonged to Friday. She had said so to Dan on Monday.
She was saying it on Thursday for a reason she could say out loud.
The family got two days with it instead of one. So did the team.

Before the page went anywhere it went to the Gaults' house,
to Missy, at the number her seven o'clock page came from.
It was ten to seven. Missy picked up,
and Aisha said it in the words she used.

"It's Aisha Cole. He's out for Saturday, Missy.
I'm signing it this morning.
Wednesday held. It doesn't reach.
Step three is today, and I expect it to hold.
I wanted you to have it from me before the board had it."

"I wrote Saturday in the book on Monday with a question mark after it,"
Missy said. "I'll take the question mark off."

That was the call. Nobody thanked anybody.
Missy's page came at seven anyway. Slept. Zero.
At seven Trey was across the lot in Dan's meeting room
for the twenty minutes she had promised him in the first week,
no film, no playbook,
his mother in the lot with the engine running,
while his Saturday sat on her desk in ink.
At eight he ran for her on the turf, step three,
the football-shaped one, straight lines with a ball in his hands
and nobody near him, and it held, and she wrote that down too.
The ladder went on. Saturday did not.

The page went to the athletic director at half past eight,
scanned with her phone
and sent to the address his email had come from in the first week,
with a copy to Merritt.
Then it went in the chart the long way.
Indication, rationale, her name.
She did not cross the lot to watch him read it.

Dan had it by nine, then.
He had told her on Tuesday, with a box against his chest,
that what he wanted out of her was nothing,
and she had put her hand on his arm for an answer.
Now a page with her name on it was in his building.
He had every reason to set it on his desk
and let his room say the doctor.
She had signed it knowing what he could do with it,
and wanting him not to,
and she would have denied the wanting in any room he was standing in.

***

Aisha read the board at noon in the annex clinic,
with the phone in one hand and a roll of tape in the other,
between a kid with an ankle
and a kid with nothing wrong with him who wanted to be told so.
The word had left the lot at nine, and by noon the board had it.

> **DeeAnn Prewitt** Interesting season. Interesting choices. Praying
> for the boys.

Under it, from the out-of-state name that had counted eleven families
on Saturday, the count was fourteen,
and the letter it was counting for had a new subject.
The subject was her.
The board was quiet in the bad way.

"Damn," she said, once, to the phone,
and put it in the parka pocket and did the ankle.

The trainer came along the corridor at one
with a roll of tape under his arm and stopped in the clinic door.
He had said forty words to her since August.
He said the next ones to the tape.

"Captains were in Coach's office at ten. Your paper was on the desk.
I was in the door with the kit.
He told them 7 sits Saturday on the doctor's call, and the call's right,
and anybody with something to say about it says it to him first."

She waited for the rest.

"Nobody said anything back," the trainer said.
"That room always says something."

He went on down the corridor.

She had not been in the room, and that was the point of it.
A thing done where she could see it was done for her to see.
This one he had told to nobody.
She had it secondhand from a man who said forty words a season,
and secondhand was how it counted.
Two weeks ago Dan had said yes to twenty minutes at her whiteboard
and gone upstairs to ask for film.
Today he had argued for her in his own room
before she knew there was an argument.
She wanted him for it.
She said that to herself plainly, and then she did the ankle.

***

Room nine at the Magnolia Court had the heat on and nothing else,
and Aisha lay on top of the bedspread at half past three
with her boots on and a granola bar she had not opened in one hand,
because the clinic's last kid had been at two
and she had been up since four.
Dan was in her head, in the shape the trainer had given him,
a man standing in his own office saying the doctor's call was right
to a room that did not look up.
She had not seen his face for it.
She would have liked to.
She did not sleep, and at ten to four she went out to the lot.

The lot was full of plates.
Georgia, Tennessee, two from Ohio,
and the pink script lit early over the office.
A man at the ice machine looked at the name on the parka
and found something in his cooler that needed him.
Verna was at the office window with the glass slid back
and a pen behind her ear, and she watched Aisha look over the lot.

"He's not in my lot," Verna said. "I'd know a two-door in this crowd."

"I'm counting plates."

"You're counting doors. It's on the board that you signed it."

"I signed it."

"This whole county wants Saturday. I want it.
I had a man once who'd have stood in this lot
and cussed a doctor he never met for a boy he never met,
and gone on in to the game.
That boy needs the next sixty years.
He doesn't want a one of them yet.
One person in this county has to want them for him."

Aisha said nothing to that.

"Mm," Verna said, and slid the glass shut.

She went back across to the annex at a quarter past four,
because the sheet for five fifteen was hers to type, and she typed it.

***

At five fifteen Aisha walked out to the near hash of the practice turf
with Thursday's sheet, one printed page, in her bare hand,
and Dan came off the drill to meet her
with the trainer a few yards off.
The poles were up white over the field.
Under Trey Gault's name, three words. Step three held.
Under thirty-four's, a change.

He read it in front of the trainer.
Thursday's jaw was on him, two days dark along the bone,
and his eyes came up off the page to her
and stayed there a second past what the page needed.

"You had the rest of it at nine," she said.

"I had it at nine."

"Thirty-four cuts tonight. For me first.
Then the sleeve comes off, and he's cleared to cut.
That's a change from Monday's this week, and it's mine to change."

"He'll run at you."

"Let him."

Nothing about the captains, not with the trainer in earshot.
She had it, and he could see she had it, and that was the field's share.

She went down to thirty-four at the hash.
He cut for her first. Left, then right, then a plant she asked for,
and she watched his face and not the leg.
No catch in it.
She slid the sleeve off the hamstring herself.

"You're cleared to cut," she told him. "Sleeve's off. Go."

He went, and did not say a word.
By six the board had it that the doctor had cleared one and not the other.
She did not read who typed it.

***

Her office at the warm end of the annex, a little after seven,
and Dan Merritt in her doorway with his cap in one hand,
come in by the outside door at the warm end, where the RAV4 nosed up,
and Aisha at the desk in the polo and the fleece
with the pen in her hand again, the tablet open to Trey's chart,
the heater running, and the parka on the hook behind the door.

The lot behind him was dark.
The poles over the practice field had gone off at half past six,
and the film-room window across the lot was dark too,
so a man could cross it with no window to see him.
She had not watched for one. She had heard the door.

He stood in her doorway with his shoulders a hand short of both sides
and looked at her before he said a word,
and his mouth did a thing she had not seen it do,
a shape that was going to be a smile and had not decided yet.

"Every door open," she said. "That's your rule. I've watched you keep it."

"It's a Fieldhouse rule. This isn't the Fieldhouse."

She laughed at that, a short one, through her nose,
and it surprised the room.

"Come in, Coach."

He came in. He stood by the chair in the sideline jacket.
The office had been built for one doctor and a desk,
and he was most of what was left of it.

"I heard about ten o'clock," she said.
"Captains in your office. My paper on the desk.
You told them Trey sits Saturday on the doctor's call
and the call is right,
and anybody with something to say says it to you first.
Nobody said anything back. That room always says something."

He did not ask who had told her.
He did not tell her it was smaller than that.
He looked at the tablet on the desk and back at her,
and his jaw worked once, and he nodded.

"That's what happened."

"What did it cost."

"A room. For a day, maybe two. They'll come back. They're nineteen."

"You told me Tuesday you wanted nothing out of me."

"I did."

"You spent your locker room on my page. That is not nothing.
So if there's a bill, I want it tonight, in this room,
and not in February off a board."

He looked at the door, open behind him, and back at her.
The shape at his mouth was gone.
What was there instead was the face he had worn on Tuesday
with a box in his arms.

"There's no bill," he said.
"There's a thing I know and you don't,
and I've known it since the Monday the class signed. Ten days.
I should have told you in the truck."

"Then tell me at my desk."

"Boyd's building has a floor for what you do. Six tables.
You've seen the drawing. Here's why you're not in it.
A group runs that floor. A sports-medicine group, under contract.
The man with the clicker called it a provider partnership.
The team's doctors work for the group,
and the university quits paying for its own.
He said it with the boards on the marker tray.
'The university stops carrying that line.'
That line is you, Doc. Your job is the price of the building.
I knew whose line it was when he said it.
I sat in my own film room and nodded at it like it was a parking plan.
That's what my quiet cost you. Ten days."

She had known her rooms were not in the drawing.
She had not known there was a sentence for it.
Now she had it, and it said nothing about her. It said a line.
The loan on medical school did not care what a consultant called it.
She felt that one where she always felt it, low, and old,
and then she looked at him.
He had carried her page into his own office for a call
that by summer would be some other doctor's to make,
and he had known it, and he had stood there anyway.

"You told me the other thing in the truck instead," she said.

"The other thing was mine to tell. This one was yours,
and I sat on it, and I'm not making that smaller either."

There was nothing on his face that was managing anything.

She got up.
She went past him to her own door
and put her palm flat on the wood and pushed it to
until the latch caught, with Dan inside her office,
and came back around the desk to where he stood.
Every room two adults deep. He still had that one.
Every door open, he did not.

He watched the door go shut. Then he watched her.

"You said there was no way," she said. "Tuesday. With a box."

"I said it."

"I put my hand on your arm. That was my answer.
So here is the rest of it, Dan.
I've wanted this since June.
I'm standing in a room I just shut,
with a page in your building that this county
is going to hang on me by Saturday night.
I know what you lost to a screenshot.
I want it anyway."

He set the cap down on the desk, on the open chart.

"Aisha," he said, and her name in his voice was a low thing,
said for one person, and he went on inside the same breath.
"If I do this, I can't stand at that hash tomorrow at five fifteen
and be a man who didn't."

"Then don't be one."

She put her hand flat on the front of the sideline jacket,
and he did the thing she had wanted since a porch rail
with two coats between them, he put his hands on her,
both of them, one at the side of her face with the palm
covering her from the ear to the jaw and the fingers in the curls
behind it, the other at her back through the fleece,
and they were warm, and they were bigger than she had guessed
across a sheet, and he bent his head and she came up to meet it,
and he kissed her, and she kissed him,
in her own office with her own door shut,
with the county two days off and the lot dark.

His hands stayed. That was the part she was going to keep.

He said it from there, close, without letting go.

"Terms," he said.
"Mine first, because I've had ten years to write them.
Nothing public. Not a look on that field.
Not my truck at this building after dark.
Nothing a phone can hold.
Until the class is closed. First Wednesday in February.
After that there is nothing left this season
that anybody can trade you for."

"That's a long time to stand at a hash."

"I've stood at it all fall."

"Mine," she said.
"One rule, and I'm writing it the same as I wrote the protocol.
We never talk about my calls in here,
or in your kitchen, or anywhere with a door shut.
You get my calls on paper at five fifteen
with the trainer in earshot, and nowhere else.
Not a step. Not a date. Not the word before it's on the page.
So nobody can say a call got softened in a kitchen.
Not the board. Not you. Not me."

He took that with his hands still on her,
and his face went for a second to where it had been on Tuesday
telling her about a screenshot, and came back.

He looked down at her hand on his jacket.
"You wrote a rule at me with your hand on my chest, Doc."

"I did."

He laughed, once, low, and she felt it in the hand.

"Then read it tomorrow," she said,
"at five fifteen, in front of the trainer,
and put it in your jacket, and I'll walk down to thirty-four,
and nobody on that field will have a thing to see."

"And tonight, in here, with the door like that."

"Tonight the door is shut, and I'm the one who shut it,
and I would like you to stop talking now."

He stopped. His hand went back to her jaw, the whole palm,
and stayed there, and she kissed him in her own office
with the lot dark and the door shut behind him.
