# Chapter 28 — Signing Day

POV: Dan, third person limited, past tense.
(DRAFT — blind candidate A, card D2, per plots/brief-ch28.md.)

---

> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **DeeAnn Prewitt:** Listening to the signing-day show at my desk two
> states away. I don't know one name they've read. I've cried twice. My
> son is a junior and a punter.


Dan Merritt carried the class into the high school under one arm,
a blue folder of letters that had all been signed in December,
and the cold came in off the lot behind him and down the hallway ahead of him.
It was Wednesday the third of February, signing day,
and WDSS 1340 ran its signing-day show from the gym here from nine to noon.
It ran it from the high school because the gym had bleachers and heat
for half the county, and nothing at the college had both.
Most of the class had signed in December,
so the morning was mostly for show.
Three letters were still real.
The quarterback he had flipped in January
was due to sign by the close of business today,
and so were the two families who had said yes in the fall
and gone quiet in January.
Across town at nine the independent review would sit again
and give its findings on Aisha Cole.
If it said she had been wrong to hold his quarterback out of the playoff,
that would sit on her record for the rest of her working life.

The hallway smelled of floor wax and old popcorn.
The trophy case ran the length of the wall outside the gym doors,
and the athletic director stood in front of it
holding a paper cup of coffee he had not drunk any of.
He had come for the first picture of the morning.
At a quarter to nine he would drive across town
and sit at the university's table at the review, as he had on Monday.

The athletic director wanted to keep his job,
and all winter that had meant wanting whatever the trustees wanted.
It was his email that had put Aisha on leave in January.
It was also his office that had told Dan no in writing,
when Dan went around her for film after his quarterback got hurt.
On Monday Dan had said so in front of the county.

Dan could see her from here without trying.
She would be at the small table on the left by now,
in the one good jacket, with the record tabbed at her elbow.
He would never tell anybody that on Monday he had sat in his truck
behind that building for twenty minutes
with his right hand turned up on his knee until the heater ran hot.
It was the hand she had held on the back stairs.
He could still feel her fingers across the backs of his knuckles.
His palm went warm on the folder now at the thought of them,
and the cold off the doors went down his spine anyway.

"They'll ask how a pledge that size walks off an agenda in one morning,"
the athletic director said.
He turned the cup a quarter turn in his fingers.
"They'll ask my office. It went on my watch."

The pledge was the new building's money.
The athletic director had spent the whole winter
arranging his office around the February sitting
where that money was supposed to come in to the university.
If the trustees decided his office had let it walk,
they would want somebody else in the office.

Dan said it low.

"You didn't lose the money. I did."

Nobody heard it.
Inside the propped gym doors a voice was saying *check* into a microphone,
and the bleachers were filling up with the sound of the whole county
sitting down on cold wood in its winter coats.
The athletic director looked at Dan over the cup for a long moment.
His mouth came open and shut again.
Then he set the cup on top of the trophy case, still full,
and went out the front doors to his car without it.
There was nothing the man could ever give Dan back for it,
and neither of them pretended there was.
Dan's heart had stayed steady the whole time.
It was the easiest true thing he had said all week.


At nine the station's tone came through the gym's speakers
loud enough to start a baby crying in the top row.
Dan stood at the end of a folding table under the home basket
with the blue folder in both hands.
Tick Moran sat in the middle of the table behind the mixer,
with one headphone pushed back.
Tick ran the Coach's Table on 1340 from Delmar's porch in season,
and every February he ran this.
The sponsors paid for three hours of parade, and Tick would give them one.
Off the air in January, Tick had told Dan what he thought of it.

A fax machine sat on the table beside the mixer,
with its cord run under the bleachers to a jack in the wall.
Nobody had faxed a coach a letter in years.
Tick plugged it in every February anyway,
because the county liked to sit in the bleachers for three hours
watching a machine on a folding table and waiting for it to ring.

Half the county was in those bleachers in its coats.
The Liars' Table had the third row at center court,
the way it had the front window at the Checkerboard every morning.
Tunk Ferrell had the aisle end of the row,
and he sat the Checkerboard's window most mornings
because he wanted every story in the county
before anybody else had it and while it was still warm.
Earlene Tatum sat beside him in a knit hat with her purse on her knees.
She walked the coffee pot at the Checkerboard
and had an opinion out loud about most things,
and what she wanted this winter was a county that was fair to the doctor.
She had sat through Monday's review from the first word to the last,
and she had come to the gym today instead of the findings
because she said a person could talk back to a radio.
Peanut Kyzer ran the taps at the Mule
and wanted nothing from a night but a quiet bar,
and he sat at the far end of the row in a ball cap
looking like a man who had been promised the gym had a bar in it.

Tick read the December class off a sheet, one name at a time,
with the hometown and the height, as if the ink were still wet.
After every third name he said the same thing.

"Signed in December, friends, and hasn't changed his mind."

The bleachers clapped for every one of them.
None of the boys could hear it.
They were in class in other states.

The photographer the university had sent
had Dan hold the December letters up beside his face, one at a time.

"Like it just came in, Coach," the photographer said.
"You're surprised. You're happy. Good. Next one."

Dan held up a letter that had sat in his drawer since before Christmas
and looked surprised about it.
By the sixth letter the smile had begun to ache in his jaw.
Odell, who cut hair at Fountain's on Fountain Row,
had squared the back of his neck on Thursday for this.
Dan had got a haircut to be photographed being amazed by paper,
and he knew it, and up in the third row Earlene knew it too.
One corner of her mouth had gone tight, which on Earlene was a laugh.

"Ought to make 'em sign it twice," Tunk said from the aisle,
loud enough for the table. "To be sure."

Nobody laughed.
Tick read a spot for the feed store over the top of it.

At the break at a quarter to ten Dan went down the floor to stretch his legs,
and Earlene put out one gloved hand
and caught his sleeve at the wrist as he went past the third row.

"She'll be sitting down about now," Earlene said.

She did not say who.
She looked at him until he looked back.
Heat came up the back of his neck, and he let her watch it come.
At the end of the bleachers he took out his phone and looked at it.
There was nothing on it, and there was not going to be.
Until the class closed today the university would fire a coach
and the team physician on the same day
if anybody caught the two of them together,
and a text on a phone could be caught.
Aisha would not have sent one from a review in any case.
He looked at the blank screen anyway, long enough to be a fool about it,
and his heart went on thumping at it as if something might come.
He put the phone face down on the table beside the folder.

At twenty to eleven Tick's phone lit up between the mixer and the fax.
Tick read it with his thumb on the fader.
Then Tick looked across the table at Dan,
and he did not push the fader up until Dan nodded.
It was news, and news was Tick's job.

"Friends, I'll read you this the way it came to me," Tick said.
"Another school posted a few minutes ago
that it has signed the quarterback who came through the portal in January.
That's the young man who told Ashford yes.
He was one of the three letters this table was waiting on."

The bleachers went quiet the way a crowd does when it is doing arithmetic.
Dan's hands closed on the folder, and the cardboard bent under his thumbs.
In January the quarterback's father had called Dan's office phone
from two states off and asked him straight out
whether this doctor sat a starter every time or had only done it the once.
On Monday the whole county had heard Dan say
that the rule said he did not decide.
The father had heard it too, and this was his answer.
That was the price of Monday, read out loud in front of the county,
and Dan had known the number before he paid it.
His stomach went down and stayed down,
and his heart went heavy and slow behind it.

Every man on his staff would hear it on a truck radio somewhere this morning,
and Ty Beaumont, who coached his receivers, would hear it
with a truck payment due and his own job riding on Dan's.

Dan kept his face up where the bleachers could see it.
He stood there.

Sonny Dillard came in the side door at eleven in half his gear
and climbed to the end of the Table's row.
He was the feed store's counter by day and the pumper when the pager went,
and all he ever wanted from a morning was to finish it before the pager did.

"Survey crew's back up on the annex ground," Sonny said to the row.
The gym carried it anyway.
"Tripod and all, putting pins in past the whirlpool room.
Fellow in the vest told me they're working to the eleventh."

The eleventh was the annex deadline.
On Monday the trustees had moved the pledge back to a later sitting
with no date on it,
and the crew on her ground was still working toward the eleventh
as if nobody had bothered to tell it.
That ground was her clinic.
The money behind that crew had not stopped for a transcript,
and it was not going to stop for a gym.
Dan did not turn around.

At twenty to twelve Tick laid his hand flat on top of the fax machine.

"Two more letters were due to this table by the close today," Tick said.
"Two families told Ashford yes in the fall.
This machine hasn't rung for either one of them, and neither has my phone.
The day's not over.
But I've sat at this table enough Februaries
to know what a quiet machine means."

Dan had known it since January.
Knowing it had not made it any lighter to stand there and hear it
with the bleachers looking at the back of the new haircut
while Tick said it out loud to the county.
The flipped quarterback and the two quiet families were the class,
and the class was what a coach was paid to bring in.
The extension had come off the trustees' page on Monday afternoon.
Now the county had heard what else Monday cost,
one letter at a time, off Tick's own table.
His breath went in slow and came out slower.
The ache of it sat across his shoulders
like a set of pads worn a quarter too long.
He stood there with the folder and took it.

At five to twelve Tick's phone lit again.
This time he put it to his ear with the headphone pushed back,
and his other hand came off the fader and lay flat on the table.
He listened.
Then he leaned into the mic with the phone still at his ear.

"Friends, Marisol Pruett's on the line from across town, at the review,"
Tick said.
"She promised me she'd call the findings in the minute they were read,
before the board could get them wrong.
She's going to tell it to me, and I'm going to tell it to you."

Marisol moderated the Ashford parents' board
and had a son on Dan's roster, a walk-on named Wes,
and she wanted this heard right the first time by a county she had to live in.

"The reviewer heard one more person before the findings," Tick said.
"Missy Gault asked to be heard. That's 7's mother."

Missy Gault had sat in the hall outside Aisha's clinic every day of the hold
with a notebook in her lap
and her purse on the floor beside the chair and her pen going.
She wanted her boy safe, and she wanted him playing,
and all winter Dan had watched her not be able to have both.

Tick listened.
Then he gave it to the county a sentence at a time,
in the same plain voice he used for the school lunch menu.

"She said, and these are her words," Tick said.
"'We drove Trey to Birmingham to find a doctor who'd say ours was wrong.
The man there wrote us that she was right.
I've had his letter in my purse since the twenty-third of December,
and I never showed it to a soul.'"

The gym had gone so quiet Dan could hear the fax machine hum,
and his own heart under it.
Tick listened again.

"'I wanted him on that field more than anybody in this county.
I still do.
And every day she held him, she was building him a record for the scouts,
so they'd see a boy somebody let heal.
Nobody asked her to do that. I watched her do it.'"

Missy Gault had stood up because of what Dan said on Monday.
Marisol said so down the phone, and Tick told the county.

Something let go in Dan's chest that had been shut tight since Thursday.
The folder slid in his hands, and he caught it against his leg.
Last week the county had heard half of his answer
in a stranger's flat voice on the radio.
Today it heard the whole of Missy's in Tick's.

"Denny Gault stood up next," Tick said.

Denny was the boy's father,
and all fall he had held court about his boy
in the northeast corner of Delmar's porch
to anybody who would stand there with a plate in his hand and listen.
This winter he had gone quiet in that corner with his coat buttoned to the top.
He wanted the town's good opinion, and he wanted his boy's head safe,
and until today nobody had known which one he would pick.

"Marisol says he didn't know about Birmingham," Tick said.
"He heard it when the rest of the room did.
She says he looked at that purse like he'd never seen it before.
Then he stood up anyway, and here's what he said.
'Some of those families brought me their letter in January
and asked me to sign it.
I told them no. I'd done my talking all fall.
I wasn't putting my name against the woman
who had my boy's head in her hands.'"

In January Dan had stood in that corner beside Denny
after the Table went off the air and asked after the boy,
and Dan had let the missing name lie where it was.
Now he knew why it had been missing.

Denny had been asked, and he had said no, and he had told nobody.
He had not even told the corner.

Dan's eyes stung, and he looked at the fax machine until they quit.
He let his breath out through his nose, slowly, so it would not shake.

Tick listened a long time.
He wrote on the back of the sheet of December names
with a pen out of his shirt pocket.
Then he put the phone face down and took the mic in both hands.

"Here's the finding, friends," Tick said.
"The review affirms Dr. Aisha Cole's call.
Affirmed means they looked at all of it and said she was right.
Her suspension's lifted, as of today.
And the review calls her documentation exemplary.
That's the review's word, read to me off the page. Exemplary."

Her whole name went out over the county on 1340.
Dan's breath went out of him all at once and did not come back right.
His knees went loose under him,
and he put one palm flat on the table beside the fax machine to keep his feet.
His heart was going so hard he could feel it in that palm, against the wood.
Heat came up into his face, and he did not care who saw it.
His skin had gone hot all over under the coat.
He wanted to see her face.
It was all he wanted, and it came up through him so hard his eyes burned.

Her record was clean.
A doctor with a clean record could take any job she liked,
and Dan guessed the offer out west had been waiting on this morning's finding
as hard as anybody sitting in the bleachers of this gym had.
If it had, nothing stood in its way now.
He made himself be glad of that as well.

Up in the third row, Sonny Dillard took his cap off first,
without a word, and held it on one knee.
Peanut saw and pulled off the ball cap,
and then Tunk took his own off and held it in both hands.
Earlene pulled her knit hat off,
and her hair came up with it, and she did not fix it.

At the table beside Dan, Tick reached up and took off his own cap
and set it on top of the fax machine.
It was the whole Table, five hats, in the time it took to breathe twice.
Nobody told the rest of the bleachers to do anything.
Down the row a big man in a work coat looked at Sonny,
and then at his own hat, and he left it on.

The money behind the new building had spent the whole winter
trying to move her out of this county,
and the county had just taken its hats off to her in a high school gym.
Dan's throat shut.
He kept his eyes on the hats until he could trust them.

Tick left his cap where it was and leaned into the mic, off his sheet.

"I've had this machine plugged in since nine, waiting on it to ring,"
Tick said.
"It didn't, and it isn't going to.
The name that counted this year got written down before Christmas,
on Thursday the seventeenth of December, at the bottom of a no-go.
It was Dr. Cole's."

"Forty-six!" Tunk called from the aisle.
"Odell said you'd better say it, Tick."

"Odell at Fountain's has been counting the days since 7 sat down,"
Tick said.
"Forty-six this morning, and the boy's fine.
He asked me to put that number on the radio, so there it is."

The gym came apart after that, loud,
the way a county does when it has sat quiet for too long.
People stood up in the bleachers.
A woman behind the Table was crying and laughing at the same time
and not bothering to pick one.
Somebody started clapping, and the whole gym took it up
for a woman who was not in the building to hear it.

Dan stood in the noise and let it come up through the soles of his shoes.
He was glad for her all the way down,
in his hands and his stomach and the backs of his knees.
His hands had a shake in them that he could not stop, and he did not try.
His heart would not come down.
All winter this county had doubted her out loud.
Now it was on its feet clapping for her,
and she was across town at a small table in her good jacket
with her hands on the record and did not know it yet.
He would never have said it to anybody,
but he wanted to be the one who told her.
He wanted to watch it land on her face.

At noon the tone came down the line, and Tick signed off,
and the red light on his mixer went dark.
Dan opened the blue folder to put the December letters back.
His signing-day notes were on the index card on top, in his own marker.
At the bottom of the walk-ons' block he had written *Pruett, W. Spot holds.*
He read it twice.
Marisol would hear it from her boy when the roster went up,
and that was soon enough.

Earlene came down the bleacher steps with her hat still in her hand
and stopped in front of the table.

"Go on and be glad, Coach," Earlene said. "You paid for it."

Peanut came down behind her, settling his cap back on.
"Mule's open at four," Peanut said, to nobody, and went on out.

On Dan's way out, the athletic director's coffee
still sat on top of the trophy case, full and gone cold.


By one Dan was back up the hill in his office at the Fieldhouse,
with the blue folder on the desk and the door open on the corridor.
He had driven up from the high school at half past twelve
with the truck's radio off, because he had heard all of it once
and wanted to keep it the way he had heard it.
Somebody down in the weight room had 1340 on loud.
His phone had nineteen texts on it.
The one from his receivers coach said *HATS* and nothing else.
The photographer had already emailed the morning's pictures,
and the first one up on his monitor was Dan
holding up a letter from December with his eyebrows up
like a man who had just won the raffle at a fish fry.
He laughed out loud at it, alone in the office,
and the laugh loosened something low in his chest.

He did not call her.
Nothing between them had been settled on Monday.
There had been two hands on a back stair
and then a door at the bottom shutting on its closer,
and until the class closed today the terms still held on both of them.
He wanted to be standing in Verna's gravel so badly his knees ached with it.
He stayed at the desk.
If she wanted him today she knew where the Fieldhouse was,
and he could wait out one more afternoon for a day that ended at five.

He ate a sandwich out of the machine down the hall and did not taste it.
He ran the spring roster on the whiteboard with his marker
and hummed while he did it, which he had not done since the fall.
He caught himself humming and did not stop.
For two hours he believed it was over.


At a little before three the athletic director came up the Fieldhouse corridor
and stopped in the open office door.
He had his coat on and his car keys in one hand.
He did not come in.
His other hand was on the door frame, and it was holding on.
His face was careful, and he was working to keep it that way.

On the monitor behind Dan,
the surprised coach was still holding up his December letter.
Dan set the marker down on the desk.
His palms had gone flat on the wood before the athletic director said a word,
because he had seen that face ten years ago from the other side of a desk,
on the day a school sat him down.

"The chair out west called me at two," the athletic director said.
"It wasn't a reference call this time. It was about her release date.
They want her to start early, and the university has to let her go."

He stopped there.
Down the corridor the weight room's radio had gone to a car commercial.
Dan's pulse had gone hard and slow in his ears.
The office was so still he could hear the heater under the window.

"She said yes, Dan."

It went into him low, under the ribs, and stayed there.
His breath went out and did not come back in.
His hands stayed flat on the desk because he could not have lifted them.
The skin on his arms went cold under his sleeves, all at once.
His stomach dropped and kept on dropping.
His right palm had gone damp against the wood.

"The chair said one more thing," the athletic director said.
"A donor here called him in January to speak well of her.
The man behind the new building's pledge."
He looked down at the keys in his hand.
"I thought you ought to hear it from me before you heard it on 1340.
You'll need a physician for spring workouts. I'll start looking Monday."

He stood in the door a second longer,
as if there were one more sentence and he could not find it.
Then he let go of the frame and went down the corridor,
and his keys knocked once against his leg.

Dan did not need the name said.
Rex Boyd had called out west in January and said kind things about her.
A doctor who took a better job somewhere else
was a problem he would never have to touch again,
and he had fixed it with a phone call and a kind word
that nobody could ever call anything but help.

He had not asked her.
He had not asked on Thursday night with her fist in the front of his coat,
and he had not asked on the back stairs with her hand in his.
He had told himself it was not his to ask until the terms were done.
In two hours the class closed, and the terms lifted with it.
She had said yes before they did.
He had stood up in front of the county
and bought her a clean record with his own name,
and a clean record was the one thing a woman needed to take a better job.
He was afraid he had paid for her to go.
He was afraid she had loaded that car
believing nobody in this county would ever ask her to stay.
The fear sat in his stomach, and he could not get under it.

He could see the RAV4 in Verna's gravel with the boxes on the back seat
and the navy parka folded flat on the top one,
the way he had seen it under Verna's office light on Thursday night.
He could see her curls against the collar of the good jacket,
and her hand in his on a concrete landing,
the warm of her palm going up the inside of his wrist
while two walls away the county drank its coffee.

His hand had found the marker on the desk again.
It was the one that had put his number on her last box.
His fingers shut on it until the clip bit into his palm,
and he let it bite.
The yes had gone all the way down him by then.
It sat under his heart like a swallowed stone, cold,
and it did not move when he breathed.
He sat at the desk with the marker shut in his fist
and did not open his hand.
