The plate came down at Aisha Cole's elbow
before she had the wool coat unbuttoned,
and the cook took his hand off it and went back to the griddle.
It was the Coach's Slice,
a quarter of a pie cut as one piece and standing up off the plate.
She had not ordered it.
This was Tuesday the twenty-sixth of January,
the sixth morning of a leave she had not asked for,
and nobody in the room had said a word to her about it.
In eight days an independent review would say
whether she had been right to hold Trey Gault out of a playoff game,
and she would hear the answer the same morning as everybody at this counter.
Earlene Tatum stood at the front window with the coffeepot.
She looked down the counter at the plate, and then at Aisha, once.

It was half past seven at the Checkerboard.
The last of the night's rain was coming down fine on the square,
slant through the streetlights that had not gone off yet,
and it ticked on the awning over the door when the griddle let her hear it.
Her stool was the second one from the register,
and the one nearest it was Dan's, empty on a weekday.
The slice was his order.
The cook wanted his griddle and a quiet room and not much else,
and he had set Dan's order at her elbow anyway,
with the counter watching him do it.

She looked at his empty stool and wanted him on it,
eating his own pie with the register between them
and somebody leaning on it,
as it had been all fall, before any of this.
The coffee here was self-serve.
Earlene walked the pot for the Table and for Dan,
but this morning she came down the counter with it,
filled Aisha's cup without being asked, and went back to the window.

The radio over the pass-through had 1340 on, low, under the griddle.
The news came on while she had the fork up for her second bite.

[TK the Checkerboard's radio and who reads 1340's morning news — on no page]

"Sources close to the university's independent review
say head coach Dan Merritt was called before the review on Monday.
The review is examining the team physician's decision
to hold the quarterback out of the December playoff.
Asked whether, knowing what he knows now, he would have played him,
Coach Merritt said, 'Every coach in the country plays him.
I'd have played him.'
In a statement this morning, the athletic department said,
'Coach Merritt has full confidence in the independent review process.'
Findings are due Wednesday, the third of February."

Then the voice went on to the weather.

Aisha set the fork down on the edge of the plate, slow.

*I'd have played him.*

The voice on the radio had read it flat, the same as the weather.
She heard it in Dan's voice anyway.
It was his voice from the rail, the one that never hedged,
and it was saying he would have put Trey on the field on the nineteenth,
the night she held him off it.

The heat came up her neck first.
It went into her face and stayed.
She kept her face down over the cup.
Her right hand had closed on the counter without her.
She opened it and laid it flat on the wood, palm down, and made it stay.
Her throat had shut on nothing.
Her breath came short over the top of it,
and she let it out through her nose
so the man two stools down would not hear.
Under the counter her knees were pressed together hard enough to hurt.

*I knew it.*

She thought it in so many words. It was almost a relief.
Nothing was ever going to happen for her here.
She had let one man close, one,
in a county that could read an empty parking space,
and he had gone in front of the review
and handed it the one sentence it needed to make her the problem.
Of course he had.
It was every building that had never kept her, all over again,
and this time the man doing it was the one she had let into room nine.

Twelve days ago, on the Thursday of the ice,
Dan had said *her protocol* on this same station
to every radio in the county.
She had carried those two words around since then
like something warm in a pocket.
This morning the same station had him saying he would have played Trey.

She was not sad. She was angry, all the way down to her hands.
Under the angry was a hurt,
and the hurt was about this morning and nothing older.
It sat in the middle of her chest like a hand pressed flat there,
and it did not ease when she breathed.

The department's sentence had his name in it and not hers.
It was the second statement in a week from a department that had promised one.
It did not say he had been misquoted.
Nothing corrected it.

Nobody at this counter was going to see any of it,
so she picked the cup up in both hands and drank,
and the coffee went down past her throat like a pill she had to take on purpose.

A counter regular in a work vest, two stools down,
said it to the man beside him and did not lower his voice for it.

"Most any other doctor, that boy plays in that playoff game,
and nobody's sitting here in January talking about it.
Now his own coach is on the radio saying so."

Aisha did not answer him.
The man was not wrong to think it, with what he had.
The radio had given him the coach.

The cook did not come for the plate.
The slice stayed at her elbow where he had set it, Dan's order,
one bite gone, and she did not eat the rest.
When she held out her card, the cook glanced at it and shook his head.
She went out into the last of the rain with the wool coat buttoned wrong,
one button off all the way down, and did not stop to fix it.


She walked the three streets over to the Magnolia Court
with the rain quitting on her hair,
and sat on the end of the bed in room nine with the wool coat still on.

At nine the offer came in on the tablet.
It was a letter on letterhead from a department chair out west.
Rex Boyd had passed her name along three weeks ago, so that she would go.

[TK the university, the chair, how the chair first reached her — on no page]

*We are pleased to offer you the directorship.*
*The department's physicians and staff will report to you.*
*We ask for your answer by Saturday, February 6.*

The word *directorship* went into her chest and stayed there.
Her breath went short, as it had at the counter,
and this time it was not anger.
Somebody wanted her.
Somebody had put it on letterhead with a date on it.
Saturday the sixth was eleven days off.
She felt it at the back of her neck, like a hand that had settled there.

The building she had been asked to leave by noon on the twentieth
was the one that man wanted,
and the directorship was the door out of it that he had found for her.
It was a good job anyway.
In February the trustees would give his glass building
the ground her clinic stood on,
and in the same sitting they would give her job
to the group that came in with it.

The phone was in her hand before she had decided anything.
Dan's name was under Kat's in her messages. Her thumb stopped over it.
Her pulse was going in the thumb against the glass.
She wanted to hear him say he had not meant it.
She wanted that more than the directorship, and she hated that she did.

She did not call him.
On the twentieth he had chosen the job at her car window,
fourteen hours a day and eleven months a year of it,
and she had let him choose it.
This morning he had told the county he would have played Trey.
A doctor on leave who called the coach the same morning
would hand the county the caption for its picture.
A coach and his team physician caught together in season
were both gone that same day, and the terms still had eight days in them.
She was not going to be the reason for a second time.
She put the phone down.


At one in the afternoon she drove up Millrow to the annex in the wool coat.
The parka had her name over the team's staff patch,
and this fortnight she was not the team's anything.
She had been asked to be out of the annex by noon on the twentieth,
but nobody had asked for her key,
and she let herself in with it at the warm end.

The clinic was open under a man the department had sent up to cover it,
and he wanted a quiet fortnight and no trouble.

"Doctor," he said. "I was told you might come by for your things.
Take your time."

"Two boxes," Aisha said. "Twenty minutes. I know where everything is."

The trainer was in the door of the rehab room.
He wanted to help her, and he wanted his job,
and he stood in the door where both of those could see him.

"I'd carry those out for you, Doc, if it was up to me," he said.
"It isn't."

Her office had the desk, the chair, and the lamp on its clean square.

[TK the office lamp — hers or the clinic's; on no page]

Along the wall the clinic's boxes stood numbered in her black marker,
and the last one had its number in Dan's.
She looked at his handwriting longer than she looked at her own,
and her chest went tight around it.
Those were the clinic's. They stayed.

What was hers went into two boxes,
and one of them was the diploma box, taped since June.
Into the other went the second boots and the charger.
The ring her mug had left by the lamp stayed on the desk.
She could not pack a ring.

The file drawer was shut.
She opened it and put her hand on Trey's chart, the paper one,
with the form in it that had her whole name at the bottom.
Her palm stayed flat on the folder a while.
She could not take Trey's chart, and she had written half the rule that said so.
She shut the drawer.
The cover man was in the office door. He had watched her do it.

Verna's towels were on the shelf over the tank in the whirlpool room,
the twelve she owed back.
She counted them out on the near table and folded each one in thirds,
because Verna's came back in thirds,
and stacked the twelve clean and square on the lid of the second box.

The survey flags still stood in the wet along the fence line, orange.
Across the lot Dan's truck sat nose to the fence at the Fieldhouse.
The sight of it went through her low and stopped her on the step,
the box against her hip and her breath caught behind her teeth.
She wanted to set the box down and walk across that lot,
and she wanted never to see the truck again, both at once.
She did neither.
She put the box in the back of the RAV4 and shut the hatch.


At three she drove to Mt. Nebo with both boxes in the back.
Delores Fountain-Mabry had given her the only copy of the count in December.
Forty-one boxes of the annex's old records sat behind Delores's one key
in the church's locked room,
and the review would want to know they were all still there and all still shut.
Aisha wanted her copy read against the pad before the findings came out.
Delores had said on the phone she would be at the hall by three,
setting out chairs for a business meeting.

The hall had folding chairs out in rows
and the agenda taped to the pass-through.

*February business meeting.*
*1. Canaan Bottom, 1968.*
*2. Men's choir: two tenors short.*
*3. Fifth-grade essays.*

[TK Canaan Bottom, 1968 — what the church lost there; on no page]

Delores had the key on its ribbon and the legal pad.
She unlocked the door at the end,
and the two of them went down the wall with the pad and the copy.
At forty-one the numbers matched.

The last time she had stood along this wall,
Dan had carried box one down the hall against his chest
and said each number as he went past Delores.
She could see him in that door now, as plain as the boxes,
and it caught under her ribs and held.
Delores looked at the hall door once,
as if a man with a box might still come through it,
and then at Aisha, and said nothing about either one.

"I heard the radio this morning, Doctor," Delores said.
"A coach can sit at a table and say what he'd have done.
You're the one who had to do it,
and that child is walking around well today because you did it."

It was the first voice all day that had said out loud that she was right,
and she had to look at the boxes for a while until her eyes were steady again.

The side door went,
and Odell Fountain came in late from his shop
with a stack of essays under a rubber band.
He cut hair on Fountain Row and was a deacon here,
and he wanted the fifth grade judged and himself back in his chair by four.

"Every one of these starts with Webster's Dictionary defines," Odell said.
"Every one.
I'm going to find the child who didn't and give that child the ribbon myself."

Aisha laughed before she knew it was coming, once, out loud in the long hall.
It hurt a little in the chest, going out.


At half past four she was back in Verna's gravel
with the twelve towels in her arms.
Sonny Dillard was on his knees behind Verna's second washer with a wrench.
He did Verna's small repairs,
and he wanted this one done before his pager went off.
He looked at the towels, and at the thirds, and went back to the hose.

"Hose is older than the washer," Sonny said. "Older than me, maybe."

Nobody answered him.
Verna came in from the office and counted the towels with one finger.
She lifted a corner of the top one to see the fold.

"Mm."

Rent at the Magnolia Court was by the month,
and February's was due on Monday.
It was in an envelope in her coat. She held it out.

"February," she said.

Verna Poteat looked at the envelope a while.
Then she put it back in Aisha's hand and closed Aisha's fingers on it.

"The heater rattled," Verna said.

"It runs behind," Aisha said. "It doesn't rattle."

Verna went back out to the office and did not argue it.
Aisha stood by the washer with the envelope
and thought Verna meant the furnace in nine.
Sonny looked at the envelope in her hand and then at the office door,
and went back to the hose without a word.

Outside, the gravel in front of nine had no tracks in it.
On the night of the ice Dan's truck had sat right there with the engine running,
and he had not got out,
because he had said February and he always kept a date.
She wanted him in that gravel again.
After this morning she did not know if she should.
She wanted him anyway, and it ached low in her chest, out in the cold.


At nine at night Aisha sat on the bed in room nine with the letter in her hand.
The wind had come round north after dark, and the furnace was running behind.
A can of sparkling water stood on the sill.

The letter had come out of the wool coat's inside breast pocket,
where it had been buttoned in since December.
The envelope was the color of tea,
with *For the night nurse* on the front in brown ink,
and on the flap Cordelia Hartwell, Hartwell like the House.
She read it for the second time.

The woman had written it to a nurse who worked nights in that building
and kept asking her why she stayed.
Most of it told the nurse she had been needed there.
The last lines were the answer.

*You keep asking what I stayed for, as if a town were a reason.
It was never the town.
It was being needed by it on purpose.*

Out west a department had asked for her by name, on letterhead, with a date,
and that was the nearest thing to on purpose
anybody in any building had ever done for her.
Staying was the brave thing,
the stool every morning and the county getting used to having been wrong.
Going was the thing she wanted.
They were not the same thing.

Going also cost Dan nothing. That settled it.
With her gone before spring there would be no doctor
for the county to put in his truck
and no team physician for anybody to catch him with,
and whatever was left of the terms would matter to nobody but her.
She had always meant to be the one who paid.

She was going to take the directorship.
She typed the answer on the tablet, three lines and a yes,
and left it in her drafts with the chair's address at the top.
She had chosen tonight, and Saturday was only the day it went.

Kat's text came at half past nine.

*Honey. The farmhouse with the bad roof came down in price. The roof did
not. I have decided it's charming. Call me.*

Kat was her friend from up the road.
She wanted Aisha to stay,
and she did not know there was anywhere else to go.
Aisha laughed once at *charming* and did not answer.
Her thumb went down to Dan's name and stopped there, as it had this morning.
She put the phone on the sill by the can.

Then she packed the room into the two suitcases,
everything she would not need in the next eleven days,
and carried them out to the RAV4,
where the two boxes from the annex were already in the back.
Anybody driving past would read a loaded car.

The parka was the last thing, over her arm.
It was the team's coat. It would go back to the team before she went.
She folded it flat on the lid of the top box,
the sleeves in and the hood under, and put the name up.
A. COLE, in block letters over the staff patch.

The nylon was cold from the wind. Her hand stayed on the left sleeve.
On the night of the ice Dan's arm had been pressed along that sleeve
the whole way down Millrow, on the bench of his truck,
with the trainer a foot away on her other side.
Her arm went warm under the wool now, from the shoulder to the wrist,
as if the bench had tipped her into him again.
Her heart came up into her throat and stayed there.
Her breath went short and white in front of her.
She kept her hand flat on the sleeve so it would not do anything else.
Her face was hot out in that wind, with nobody in the gravel to see it.
She was angry at him down to her hands, and she wanted him,
and the two sat in her chest together and neither one moved over.

Across the gravel Verna's storm door opened.
Verna stood in it in the fur collar with the office light behind her.

"Doctor. Come here a minute."

Aisha shut the hatch and went across the gravel to the step.

"I've watched a lot of cars get loaded out of this gravel, my winter doctor,"
Verna said.
"They go in quick when there's nobody standing out here
asking you to take them back out.
That one took you nine minutes. I counted."

"I'll have your key back to you before I go, Verna," Aisha said.
"The towels are in your machines. Twelve, in thirds."

Verna held the storm door open against the wind and did not go in,
and she did not say anything else.
Aisha was afraid Dan would never say one word to her about this morning.
She was more afraid he would, after Saturday,
when it was too late to matter.
From Verna's step she could see through the back glass to the top box,
and the parka folded flat on it in the light from the storm door,
with her name up in block letters and nobody in it.
