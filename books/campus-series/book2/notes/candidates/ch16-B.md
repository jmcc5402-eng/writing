> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**

> **PieBeforeKickoff:** Landed, drove, parked. Lot opens at eight and

The gray nylon went up on Aisha Cole's north edge of the bench in pieces,
two bench-crew kids walking the tent frame out square on the grass,
and Dan Merritt watched it go from the stadium's north lots
at eleven in the morning, through the open mouth of the northeast tunnel,
with a paper plate in one hand
that a father from three states over had put there without asking.
The lots had been full since eight,
coolers and cans and folding chairs, coats on everybody
and scarves on half of them,
and the RV city out on the fairgrounds had been up since before light.
Today was the nineteenth.
The first-round playoff game was here, at six o'clock under the lights,
the first time this county had ever bought a football Saturday in December,
and it had bought it outright,
every room in town sold, the hotel block full.
Trey Gault would watch it in street clothes.
The head coach could have been in the Fieldhouse;
he was out here because he had decided in June
to be the coach who was there for it,
and the county had paid for today in the only currency it had,
nights and rooms and a December.
His own price was quieter.
His extension sat on the trustees' agenda in February,
and by Monday somebody would have picked the word to go beside it.

The father with the plate had a kid, eight or nine, with a poster,
and the poster said COACH MERRITT MY DAD SAYS THROW IT DEEP,
and the kid held it up at belt height because that was as high as he went.

Aisha came through the tailgates from the Millrow side
on the walk she made before every home game,
the cart at the tunnel, the ambulance under the north stands,
the spine board, the airway kit,
with the tablet under her arm and the parka's cuffs pinned flat,
and the lots were between her and all of it.
Dan heard her before he saw her.
The father turned with a second plate
and said, "Doc, you want this one?
Coach has had his ten minutes and he's holding it like a clipboard,"
and her laugh came from behind Dan's shoulder, one burst of it,
too loud for a parking lot,
and he was already bending to the kid's poster to read it,
with the sun coming off the lid of the ice chest beside the kid
and the father's phone up for his kid and the coach,
because the parents' board had opened a thread at eight that morning,
*Playoff Pics — post yours*, and every phone in the lots was feeding it.

"Tell your dad I read it," Dan said to the kid.
"That's all I'm promising him."

He did not look around at her.
She went by him a second later on her way to the tunnel,
close enough that the cold off the parka reached the back of his hand,
her nose red above the collar, her hands out of the pinned sleeves and bare.

"Coach."

"Doc." He kept his eyes on the plate. "Coolers are at the far end."

"I saw where they were from the lot."

She went on through the tunnel toward the cart
and the gray nylon going up on her end of the bench,
and he gave the plate to the kid with the poster
and went back across the lots to the Fieldhouse.
He had been fine not looking at her.
He had not been fine hearing the laugh and not looking,
and there was nobody in this county he would have said that to,
and she was first on the list.

***

The kickoff went up under the lights
and Dan watched it come down from the fifty
with the script cards in his left hand and the headset on,
and forty yards up the sideline to his right
Aisha stood at her north edge of the bench with the trainer beside her
and the tent zipped shut at her back.
That was where she was going to stand until it was over,
and he had already walked the sideline to the coolers twice in warmups
and stopped at the coolers both times.

The county had brought its coats.
Ninety thousand of them, to the last row of the upper deck,
the student section on its feet from the anthem and staying there,
the upper deck the RV city in scarves,
and the phones came up at the kick in one motion,
a whole bowl lit from the inside, feeding the album.
The suite over the fifty had its glass lit gold
and was full to the door with the people who had paid for it,
and Rex Boyd was a shape in the middle of the glass, standing,
and from the field that was all a shape was.
The athletic director stood by the suite's door.
The cold came up through the bench,
and by the end of the first quarter every huddle was a white cloud.

Behind the team, a kid stood in a gray hoodie
with a staff coat over it and the hood up under the coat's collar,
and did not move all night,
and nobody on the sideline needed to be told not to talk to him.
Dan had looked once, at the anthem, and not again.

The backup took the first snap of the game with the first team,
three days of real reps behind him,
and got them a first down on the second play,
and the bowl gave him a sound it had been saving all fall for somebody else.
The visitors were sound and grabby and had a kicker.
Ten and ten at the half.
The press box voice read the halftime sponsors
and did not mention a quarterback.
Coming back out of the tunnel Dan stopped at the coolers again,
forty yards short,
and looked at the trainer beside her, and the trainer looked back,
and that was the one time he did not look.
The trainer had gone out twice for cramps.
She had not gone out; nobody had needed her to,
and she stood there anyway,
because the tape on her wrist was for the night somebody did.

The third quarter belonged to the kid for eight plays.
He did not have Trey's arm and did not try to,
and he kept the ball on the ground and the sticks moving
and stood in on a third down long enough to take a hit
and get the ball out first,
and Ashford was in the end zone with the kid on his back on the turf
and up again before the trainer got two steps.
Seventeen to ten.
The county came up off its aluminum and stayed up,
and the video board ran the kid's face forty feet high,
a face the county had not learned yet.
Dan tapped the kid's helmet once on the sideline
and said nothing, because there was nothing to add.
Up the sideline the trainer said something to Aisha
and she did not take her eyes off the field to answer it.

By the fourth quarter the chain-link behind the bench had her name.
A man's voice, not loud, three feet behind Dan's left shoulder,
to the man next to him.
"That's the doctor. North end, in the big coat.
She's the one that's got 7 in a sweatshirt tonight."
Dan did not turn around.
He squared the cards against his hand until they were one edge
and called the next play,
and the man had said it like a score already posted,
and that was the part that went through him.

The visitors scored on a long drive with nine minutes left and tied it,
Ashford went three plays and punted,
and the visitors went sixty yards in four minutes
and kicked a field goal with three forty on the clock.
Twenty to seventeen.
The bowl did not boo it. It stood up and stayed up.

The kid brought them out from their own twenty-five
with three forty and two timeouts,
and he was brave about it in a plain way,
a run for six and a throw for eleven and a scramble for nine,
sliding late, getting up fast,
and Dan spent the timeouts one at a time and watched his feet.
The visitors' thirty-two, with a minute and ten,
was the edge of what his kicker had in December air,
and one more first down was the whole difference.
Third and four.
The kid kept it himself,
got the four and the fifth yard after it,
and went down under two of them with the ball under his arm,
and the ball came out under them.
He had it under his arm the right way, and it came out anyway,
because that is what a ball does under two men on a cold night
one time in ten, and this was the time.
The visitors fell on it, and it was theirs.

"Shit," Dan said, to nobody, once, with the headset mic off.

His hand was on the whistle at his chest and he had not sent it there.
The visitors knelt on it twice and let the clock run to nothing,
and the bowl stood and watched it run.

That was when he looked, once, forty yards up the sideline.
Aisha was standing where she had stood at the kick.
The tent behind her had never been unzipped.
The trainer was beside her.
There was a white band of tape on her left wrist
and her curls had come down sometime in the second half,
and he could tell that much from forty yards and not one thing more,
and did not walk it.

The whistle went.
He shook every hand in the handshake line.
At the mouth of the tunnel the team had come out of,
two of the university's people picked up a microphone stand
they had set there in the fourth quarter
and carried it back up the tunnel with the cord trailing.
There had been a building to announce on this field at this whistle,
the drawing of it on the video board behind that microphone,
and Dan watched the slot go up the tunnel in two men's hands.
The suite's glass went from full to empty
while the team was still walking off.
Trey went in with the team, in the hoodie, up the tunnel
the county had spent a whole fall picturing him in with a number on,
and the cameras got their two minutes from Dan at the tunnel's mouth,
and the room got the rest.

***

He came out through the staff gate at a quarter past ten
with the headset bag on his shoulder and his cap in his hand,
past Sonny Dillard still working the clipboard in two coats.
"Wore the shirt again, Coach," Sonny said.
"It don't do a thing for the weather."
Dan went by him into the north lots
and turned toward the Fieldhouse along Millrow's low end,
because a bowl that size took an hour to empty.

The county was going up Millrow at a conversational volume,
and that was worse than shouting.
A county that shouts has not decided yet;
this one was walking past the annex toward the square, talking,
not loud, about a thing it had made up its mind on,
and the out-of-state parents went the other direction,
to the lots and the RVs and the hotel block.
A man ahead of him said,
"Every room in this town sold twice for that.
Eight of them this year, and the eighth is the one we drop."
A woman beside him said,
"She had that boy in a sweatshirt for four quarters. Y'all saw it."
Nobody said it to Dan's face, and nobody needed to.
Missy Gault stood at the open door of a car at the lot's edge
with her purse on the roof, and Denny beside her,
and Denny did not say a word to anybody going by,
and people went by him carefully.

Dan could have told any one of them the paper was right.
He had told it to his captains on Thursday in a room with the door open.
Out here it would have been a coach standing up for the doctor
on a lost night in front of the county,
and by Monday it would have been about the two of them,
so he kept walking and kept it.
*Go out in the first round with Trey sitting and it's a review.*
His own sentence, from a corridor on Tuesday.
The athletic director would not even have to say it now.

A pickup idled at the exit of the lot with the window down
and WDSS coming out of it, and Tick Moran had the polish off his voice,
the one he kept for a final.
"Visitors twenty, Ashford seventeen, and that's the year, friends.
Win or lose, we walk home together in this county. Always have."
The pickup pulled out and took him with it.
The phone in Dan's jacket had been going since the whistle,
and at the corner by the annex he looked at it once.
The album thread was filling faster than he could scroll it,
and one from the upper deck, the north end with the gray tent in it,
was captioned by a name he did not know,
*Dr. Cole stood there all four quarters. Just noting it.*
He put it away.

***

The film-room light came on under Dan's hand at a quarter to eleven
and he stood in the window with it on,
and the lot between him and Aisha's building was lit up bright as noon.
The poles over the practice field had never come on today;
nobody had practiced.
What lit the lot was the stadium,
its north banks throwing light clean across to the annex's roof,
and while they were on there was no dark to cross.
So he stood in it where she could see it lit,
and when the banks went off, one, then the other,
he killed the light and went down and across
and in by the warm-end door beside her office.

Aisha was at her desk at the warm end with the heater running
and the parka on the chair behind her,
a can of seltzer open by the tablet,
and she got up and shut the door behind him.
Her curls were down.
The tape was still on her left wrist,
and there was nothing written on it.
She looked him in the face,
and then at the cap in his hand,
and her eyes came back up.

"Nobody went down tonight," she said. "Not one.
I stood there four quarters and never took the pen out."

"I saw you standing there."

"From forty yards." Her eyebrows went up on it.

"It's a long sideline, Doc. I walked most of it."
He turned the cap over. "I stopped at the coolers."

Her eyes stayed on him at that, steady, brown,
and nothing in them was sorry about the coolers.

"The kid kept the ball," she said, and looked past him at the dark window
as if the field were still in it. "I saw that from forty yards too.
He held it right."

"He held it right. It came out anyway. That's a football."
"If I'd had—"

He stopped.
She let it go by, looked down at the tape on her wrist and then at him,
and did not pick it up, and the heater ran.

"You've lost Fridays to me all fall," she said,
with her chin up a little,
"and liked it, and I've watched you like it.
I've never watched you lose one of these.
Where does a night like this go, for you?"

He stood there with the cap.

"I left coaching and did relief work overseas."

She did not move for a second,
and then he watched her take it as she took a number off a strap,
whole, and go looking for where it went,
and her face did not ask him for another one.
Her eyes went to the dark window behind her
and came back to him with something in them he had not seen on a Friday,
and it was not pity; he would have walked out on pity.

"Then you know what tonight weighs," she said, "and what it doesn't."

"I know what it weighs. I'm going to hate it till Tuesday anyway."

"Good."
She came around the desk,
and there was light in her eyes.
"The tent never opened.
The trainer and I stood there and watched a football game
like two people who'd bought tickets,
and I have never had a night on that sideline I'd rather have had,
and I couldn't tell one person in this county that without a chart."

"You're telling me."

"You walked across a lot to be told."

Her hand came up to his jaw, the one he had shaved at five for the cameras,
and stayed there, and she kissed him once at the warm end of the annex
with the door she had shut behind them,
and it was the one thing in the night that had gone right.

"I'd have come across that lot tonight if we'd won it by thirty, Aisha,"
he said. "I need you to have that from me and not guess it."

"I'd have had the door shut before you got to it," she said,
and her eyes went over his face once more and let him go.
"Go on. You've got somewhere to be that isn't here, I can see it on you,
and whatever it is, do it before you talk yourself out of it."

He went out by the warm-end door he had come in by.

***

Dan turned the game ball over in both hands in the equipment cage
in the Fieldhouse at midnight, off a manager's cart by the counter,
Aisha's Friday sheet still in the jacket on him with the week's others,
*Step four. Holds overnight.* under one name and cleared under the other,
and the counter and the dryers and nobody else, the manager gone home.
Nobody keeps a loss ball. It goes back in the rotation on Monday.
The dryers were running, and the detergent sat in the warm air over them,
and the radio on the shelf under the dryers was off,
WDSS gone for the night.
The home whites hung on the rack in numerical order,
and there was a number in the row that had not been worn tonight,
and Dan did not look at it.

He took the marker from the counter beside the laundry loop sheet
and uncapped it with his teeth,
and wrote the kid's name on the leather across the seam,
the name he had typed on the first-team line on Tuesday in the dark,
first name and last,
and it went on the ball the same as it had gone on the sheet,
with nobody in the building to read it.
The kid would never see this ball,
and Dan had not written it for the kid to see.
He had written it because somebody in the building ought to have,
and nobody else was going to.
He capped the marker and put it back by the sheet.
He set the ball on the cart with the name turned to the wall,
and the dryers went on turning behind him.
