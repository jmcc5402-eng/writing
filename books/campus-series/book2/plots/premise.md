## 3. The leads (house format)

**Dr. Aisha Cole, 41 — never married, head team physician since
June.** *Wound:* a career spent being the most-documented person
in every room — she learned early that what isn't charted gets
taken, and that asking for anything smaller than a salary is how
a woman like her gets reclassified as a problem. *Identity:* the
chart — perform certainty, document everything, need no one; she
has lived in Ashford six months out of a motel room and two
suitcases, because unpacking is a request. *Want (named plainly,
early):* him — the wanting is on the page long before it is
permitted; and under it, one town where her name means what she
says it means. *Essence:* a woman who stays somewhere on purpose
and lets a defense she didn't ask for count as love instead of
debt. *Clock (SC3, visible early):* month-to-month at the
Magnolia Court, displaced every home Saturday; Kat's listings
unanswered in her texts; later, the outside offer's Friday
deadline. *The ick-line guarantee:* her medical judgment never
bends one inch for the relationship, and the book never proves
her wrong — what changes is not her call but her address.

**Coach Dan Merritt, 38 — divorced, the young up-and-comer.**
*Wound:* the decade-old fraternization scandal from his
young-assistant years — a season lost, a marriage lost, and (per

## 4. The conflict spine (four layered conflicts, none
dissolvable by honesty — SC4)

1. **Clock vs. clock (structural).** His calendar is the sport's
   — rivalry, bracket, portal window, signing day, each with a
   date printed a year in advance. Hers is biology's — a brain
   heals on an undated schedule that answers to nothing. The
   whole plot is the collision of a schedule that cannot move
   with a recovery that cannot be scheduled. Honesty doesn't move
   either clock.
2. **The rule with reasons, inverted.** The no-entanglement rule
   is real, and HE is its believer — he paid its bill once. But
   the power runs the wrong way for the county's story: she can
   bench his star; he cannot overrule her; neither reports to the
   other (peer conflict wearing authority's clothes — the
   consent-trap dodge, comp-shelf §3). The board adores the young
   winner and dogpiles HER: the inversion at full voltage.
3. **The internet with stakes.** Grapevine on stage (dial), by
   the rulebook: the parents' board second-guesses her medicine
   in the performative-concern register; the vicious sentences
   live in side chats and travel only by screenshot; the pile-on,
   when it comes, is eleven families and a drafted letter — and
   it works, because in 2020s institutional life a polite letter
   campaign IS power. Dan reads the board's verdicts on himself
   nightly; his recovery from that habit is an arc beat.
4. **The values / annex split.** Boyd's moved-up survey (1.1
   hand-forward) needs the annex — the old WPA infirmary the
   athletic department long ago swallowed for overflow sports
   medicine: her rehab rooms, her records, the town's memory of
   its fevers. Dan starts on Boyd's facilities side (the
   performance center recruits); Aisha is the one credible
   "safety" voice slow-walking the vacate. Sidelining her clears
   the survey's path — which is exactly what happens, the same
   week (the ruled low-point shape). The letter in the annex wall
   (S01) is hers to find.

## 8. The A4 finding — the comp that says this book fails

**The comp: Abby Jimenez, *Say You'll Remember Me* (Apr 2025,
#1 NYT)** — already on the 1.2 shelf as the cautionary entry,
which is what makes it the strongest published argument against
this exact book. **The failure mechanism:** a beloved
romance-first author loaded a contemporary with real adult
weight — caretaking, trauma, institutional grind — and her own
readership pushed back in review language the shelf note quotes:
"less rom-com and more women's fiction/family drama," the
emotional toll outweighing "the cute or sexy." The genre reader
accepts adult stakes and revolts the moment the stakes displace
the courtship as the spine. Book 1.2 is *engineered* toward that
cliff: a concussion, a public dogpile, a suspension-pending-
review, a hearing — an institutional-trial plot that could eat
its own romance.

**The defusing beats, named:**
1. **The couple-beat ration is enforced THROUGH the trial arc**
   (RETRO NEW 19: after the first kiss, no stretch longer than
   two chapters without a spendable couple beat). The outline's
   HEAT map shows a couple beat in every chapter from 15 to 30 —
   including the suspension chapters, where the beats are strain
   beats (the coat folded, the standoff, the defense) that keep
   the couple, not the case, at the center of the page.
2. **The low point is a romance beat, not a legal one.** The
   ruled bottom — the never-married woman reverts to alone,
   packing for the clean offer — is staged as want (him vs. the
   life that asks nothing of her), not procedure. Ch 25–26 are
   two-person chapters; the review happens around them.
3. **The climax is courtship architecture.** Ch 27 is built on
   the public-gift shape (estate entry 8): his testimony is the
   grand gesture, priced in his own currency (the pledge, the
   flip, the extension) — the hearing exists so that HE can
   spend something, which is what this readership came for.
4. **The board stays funny.** Every institutional scene runs the
   rulebook's recognition-comedy register and resolves into a
   two-person gesture (NEW 19); the inert February signing-day
   broadcast carries the verdict chapter so the biggest plot
   beat lands inside the book's biggest joke.

Secondary contrarian angle, noted and dodged by design: the
post-2020s rejection of supervisor-downward romance (comp-shelf
§3's Forbes/HR discourse). 1.2's answer is structural, not
tonal — she holds the domain power, nobody reports to anybody,
and the resolution never asks her to resign an inch of
authority. The card is applied; it conflicts with nothing in
canon, remit, or format.
