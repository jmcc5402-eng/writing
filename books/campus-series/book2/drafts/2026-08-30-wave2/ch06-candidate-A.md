# Chapter 6 — Selection Sunday

POV: Dan, third person limited, past tense.
(FIRST DRAFT — wave-2 blind candidate A, card D1 sound-forward.
Brief: plots/wave2-directive.md, CH 6 BRIEF.)

---

The selection show went on the air at noon,
and by twenty past eleven Millrow had quit pretending
to be a street with any room left on it.

Dan Merritt heard Delmar's a block before he saw it.
Truck doors. The pit fan. The screen-door spring
firing every few seconds like something being tuned.
A hundred conversations trying to stay low and losing.
Under all of it, a television playing warm-up brass,
the music the sport uses now to tell a whole town
to sit down and hope.
He had left his truck at the Fieldhouse on purpose
and walked up,
because a man ought to know what a day sounds like
while it can still go either way.

Ray had the porch rigged like a man who had been thinking
about it since Thursday.
The television sat up in the serving window on the pie shelf,
sound as high as it would go,
not near high enough against a hundred and some coats,
so the front two rows had appointed themselves the relay,
calling each matchup back over their shoulders,
where it reached the rail a half second late
and slightly improved.
Propane heaters hissed down both walls.
Butcher paper ran the counter in one long sheet.
The cold kept everybody's words visible,
every sentence going up in its own small fog,
and somebody's baby wore earmuffs the size of biscuits
and narrated the whole morning in vowels.

"We're a lock," Sonny Dillard said. "I ran the math.
A comes first in the alphabet."

Nobody laughed. Sonny nodded like the point stood
and faced the television.

At the serving window Ray set a coffee by Dan's elbow,
unasked.
"Big crowd for you, Coach."

"Crowd's for the screen. I just eat here."

"Mm. You'll be eating here on the nineteenth, then."

From the second row somebody hollered,
"Ray! Flag going up if we're in?"

"Flag flies for wins," Ray said, without turning around.
"Not for television."

Tick Moran had a remote rig set up in the far corner,
a card table, a mixer, one microphone in a wool sock,
and he kept touching his headphone with two fingers
like a man listening to a safe.
WDSS was carrying the porch live to everybody
who could not get on it,
which by the sound of the parked trucks
was most of the county.

Delores Fountain-Mabry was seating the porch
with two fingers and no argument,
elders down front where the sound was,
the loud ones out at the rail
where they could holler without casualties.
"You," she said, and pointed Dan at the front bench.
"Front row, Coach."
Somebody's grandmother was already patting the wood.
"I don't bite," the grandmother said.

"I'll stand back here, Miss Delores. I hear better standing."

She studied him about the length of a spelling word.
"Keep your wall, then," she said, and went back to her rows.

Denny Gault had the northeast corner and most of its air,
coat open, taking congratulations in advance.
"Best defense this county's built in my lifetime,"
he was telling the corner, "and I have been alive a while."
His boy was watching from the house,
lights low and sound low,
his mother's rules and the doctor's, in that order,
and Denny told the corner that part twice too,
proud and scraped raw in the same sentence.

He got to Dan's stretch of wall eventually.

"Program question, Coach. Not a family question."
Denny held one hand up like he was swearing himself in.
"Everybody knows what the new roster numbers
do to walk-ons after Christmas. Whole league's trimming.
Now, that scout-team kid gave your defense every look
they whipped on Saturday. Ran the visitors' whole offense
at us for a week, and I hear he ran it mean.
A boy like that ought to winter inside the building.
Roster spots for contributors. That's all I'm saying.
It's a program thing. I'd say it if I'd never had a son."

"Rosters get built in the building, Denny."

"I know it. I know they do." Denny patted the wall itself,
as if the wall had been the one holding out on him.
"You'll hear it from more than me, is all.
Some of the dads have been talking. Good talk. Proud talk."

"I heard you," Dan said, and he had,
and he had heard the part underneath it too:
a man whose own boy could not be handed a date
had gone shopping for a problem with a lever on it.
He couldn't fault the instinct.
He ran a whole program on it.

***

The music changed, and the porch changed with it.

Dan kept his shoulder blades on Ray's wall
and watched the county instead of the television.
The early pairings went by as sound alone —
the announcer's cadence, the relay working,
names of schools this town would never play
handed back row to row and let go of.
The porch got quieter by regions.
By the third pairing the heaters were the loudest thing on it,
then the pit fan,
then the baby.

Dan could have run the seeding math for them cold.
He had run it all week, privately, against his own advice.
But a county this old does not want arithmetic on a Sunday.
It wants its name said out loud off a national desk.
He drank Ray's coffee and kept the numbers in his coat.

He did not hear the television say the name.
He heard the front row hear it.
One breath, all together, the gasp a congregation gives up
when the name read out is family,
and then the porch came down on the moment
with everything it had.
The roar went off down Millrow and came back.
Out in the lot, forty truck radios had WDSS going,
and the station's feed handed the porch its own noise back,
a breath behind and twice as wide,
the county cheering the sound of itself cheering.

The relay quit passing matchups and started passing facts,
each one handed to the rail like a plate.
"Home game!"
"On campus — they come to us!"
"December nineteenth!"
The date went down the porch hand to hand,
everybody taking some.
"Somebody write it where the street can read it!"
Ray wrote it on the window glass in shoe polish,
backwards, so it read right from Millrow.

"Alphabet," Sonny said, to anybody. "I told you. Alphabet."

Earlene Tatum filled her thermos cup all the way to the brim
and handed it to a stranger.
"It's a day," she said, by way of explanation.

Tick Moran was in the corner with his remote rig,
one hand cupped over his ear, grinning at his own meters.
"Folks, whatever you can hear underneath me right now,"
he said into the microphone,
"it is exactly what it sounds like."

Fifteen winters Dan had coached in towns
where December belonged to somebody else.
He stood against the wall in this one and held still,
and nobody watching would have known
what the holding still was costing him.

Somewhere in the middle of all that sound was a laugh
he knew at one hearing.
She was down the porch with a plate, a whole crowd away.
He kept his eyes on the county
and let the knowing sit where it landed.
Good day. He wasn't going to ask it questions.

Tick worked his way over and held out the microphone.
"One word for the county, Coach."

Dan took it for the length of one sentence.
"December nineteenth," he said. "Bring your coats."
He handed the microphone back
before anybody could decide he was eloquent,
and the porch treated the sentence like Scripture anyway,
repeating it outward in rings.

In the corner Denny had his phone up over his head,
showing the porch a photograph Missy had sent,
taken from the den doorway at the moment of the name.
The den dark, the television bright, the bracket on it.
He showed it around like a christening picture.
Nobody in the frame at all,
and every soul on that porch knew who it was of.

It took the better part of an hour
for the noise to come down to a hum,
and the hum was going to hold till dark.
Delores came to stand at the rail near Dan,
hands folded on the wood,
counting the crowd the way she must have counted recess,
lips not moving.

"First playoff in this town's whole life," she said,
at reading-circle volume, to the porch more than to him.
"Look how happy."
She patted the rail twice,
the pat that dismisses a class she was fond of.
"Now pray for whoever it falls on
to say no to any piece of it."

She never looked at him while she said it.
She didn't have to.

Dan stood with it a while.
Then he leaned into the serving window
and got Ray's attention with two fingers.

"Fix me a plate. Both meats, extra bread. Wrap it good."

"Who's it riding to?"

"Gault's house."

Ray built it without another word
and taped the butcher paper down himself.

Dan carried it through the crowd
and set it in Denny's hands,
keeping his own hands under it until Denny had the weight.

"For your house," he said.
"Tell Missy the porch asked after her people."

Denny looked down at the plate,
and for the first time all afternoon
the volume went all the way out of him.
"I'll tell her," he said. "She'll want to know who asked."

"Tell her all of them."

***

His house was dark when he got home, and the county was not.
Porch lights burning the whole way out,
and horns still going off on the square
at nothing in particular.

At eleven he sat on the edge of the bed
and opened the parents' board,
same as every working night of his life.
It was the one night in ten years
the county's verdict on him owed him nothing,
and the page was a bonfire of love anyway.
Somebody was organizing a caravan
for a game that did not require driving.
Somebody wanted the gates opened early on the nineteenth
so nobody's grandmother stood in the cold.
Somebody had posted the bracket graphic by itself,
no caption, ASHFORD in block letters,
and four hundred people had found something to say to it.
Denny's post was up too, an hour old.
Proud of the boys. Grateful to the town.
One sentence at the bottom about roster spots
for kids who contribute,
dressed as program concern,
already wearing its first few dozen amens.

He read the board to the bottom, the whole happy mile of it.
Then he went back to the top and read it through again,
slower, a film-grader's habit,
looking in all that love for the play
that gets somebody hurt later.
He did not find one tonight.
That did not mean it wasn't in there.

What he would never have said on that porch,
or into Tick's microphone, or to a living soul,
was that when the county's name came up on the screen,
the first thing through him had not been the roar.
It had been Tuesday — her whiteboard in the annex,
a date circled in red,
and beside it a ladder of careful steps
without one date on it anywhere.
The county had spent all afternoon saying the nineteenth
like the word itself gave off heat.
He had stood under a hundred coats' worth of it
doing subtraction.

He plugged the phone into the charger and left it there,
and turned off the lamp.
The game was on the nineteenth.
