# Chapter 8 — Coach's Table

POV: Dan, third person limited, past tense.
(FIRST DRAFT — wave-2 blind candidate A, 2026-08-30.
Card D6: one scene runs past comfortable. Raw material for the
author's pass; not accepted canon.)

---

Delmar's screen porch was built for June and got used hardest
in December.
Two propane heaters stood in the corners with their coils gone
orange,
the barrel smoker out by the steps kept the porch wrapped in
its own hickory weather,
and at the rail end Tick Moran had the whole radio station set
up on a card table — mixer, headphones, a telephone that lit
up instead of ringing, and a microphone on a boom arm swung
out over the rail where the county could lean into it.
Butcher paper taped to the screen said COACH'S TABLE,
THURSDAYS, LIVE ON 1340, in Delmar's own hand,
and under that, smaller, EAT FIRST.

Dan Merritt came at half past six,
because the show ran better if he had shaken every hand on the
porch once before the light came on.
He worked the rail through brisket smoke and bracket talk,
men saying *the nineteenth* to each other in the tone other
counties kept for weddings,
and at the smoker line he made up two paper plates,
white bread under the meat, no forks, the Delmar's way.

Dr. Cole stood at the far rail in a staff parka sized for a
lineman, sleeves turned back,
a cup of coffee she wasn't drinking on the rail in front of
her.
He set one plate at her elbow and kept his eyes on the smoker,
like the plate had gotten there on its own.

"They feeding you out at the college?"

"There's a salad bar."

"That's what I was afraid of."

She picked the plate up in both hands and took one bite,
standing,
and Delmar, forty feet off, straightened like a man hearing
his name called.
Dan got one bite of his own plate.
Then the phone at the card table lit,
Tick waved him into the folding chair,
and that was supper.

"Seven o'clock in Doss County," Tick said,
"twelve degrees and dropping,
and this is Coach's Table, live from Delmar's porch,
where the smoke is free and the coach is cornered.
Say hey to the people, Coach."

"Hey to the people."

The porch liked that more than it deserved.

The first caller was Gary from out past the fairgrounds,
who was proud of him, proud of the boys, proud of the county,
and wanted to know about parking on the nineteenth.
Dan gave him lots and gate times off the top of his head,
told him campus police would open the far lot at ten,
and to dress like he planned on living through it.

The second was a woman who had not missed a home game since
her boys were small,
and her boys were forty now,
and she guessed what she really wanted to know was whether it
was safe to get her hopes up.

"Hope's free, ma'am," Dan said.
"Only thing on this porch Delmar doesn't charge for."

There it was, though. The word.

Caller three opened with
"Long-time listener, first-time caller.
Course, there's the one station, so that's everybody."
No laugh came back off the porch.
Tick asked him for his question,
and the question was the boy.
"Just tell us he's going to be all right, Coach.
That's all anybody on this porch wants to hear."

"Nobody knows yet," Dan said.
"I'd rather hand you that straight than sell you a date.
He's got the best care in three counties and his family's
with him,
and the answer stays the same till it changes."

"Well. We're praying."

"I'll take that over a forecast any night."

And the calls kept coming.
Tick took them like a man dealing off a warm deck,
and the porch stayed pleased,
and every call, wherever it started,
came around the same curve before it ended.
Was it safe to book the church bus for the nineteenth.
Was it safe to let the print shop run the shirts they had,
frankly, already run.
Was it safe to tell a boy's grandmother in Tennessee to start
driving.
Safe, safe, safe.
He had lent that word out once, in October, off a supper lawn,
with a microphone in front of him and a doctor in earshot,
and the county had been spending it ever since,
and tonight it wanted the loan renewed on the air.
He answered every caller without touching it,
gave them schedules, tickets, flu shots, long sleeves,
and the light on the telephone kept coming back on,
past the break Tick usually took at half past,
past the place any radio man would have cut to the feed-store
spot,
because Tick Moran knew a full porch when he had one.
Down the rail, Dr. Cole had not touched her plate again.

The phone lit.

"You're on the porch," Tick said.

"Yes — hi. This is Roberta, we're out on the Millrow road.
Genuine question for you, Coach, no agenda.
Is a 'precautionary hold' a real medical category,
or is that a coaching decision?
My husband looked it up and he couldn't find it anywhere."

The porch got quiet enough to hear the smoker settle.

He knew the answer.
He knew it cold, depth-chart cold.
*It's real, it's medicine, it's hers, and I'm glad.*

"Well, Roberta," he said,
"I'm going to disappoint your husband,
because I don't keep the rulebook on the medical side of the
building. I coach the ones they hand me.
What I'll tell you is nobody's pulling harder for the
nineteenth than the people inside this program,
and I trust it all to come out the way it ought to."

Radio-sized.
Nothing to grab, nothing to quote,
a shrug you could hear in a kitchen out on the Millrow road.
The porch eased.
The porch eased, and the six feet of rail around the doctor
got emptier without one person moving.

Tick reached for the phone bank.
She left her cup on the rail and came down the porch,
and the crowd gave her the lane it gives a doctor,
and Dan had time to think she was going home,
and then her hand was on the boom arm
and the microphone came around to her mouth
like she was adjusting an exam light.

"Roberta, this is Aisha Cole. I'm the hold."

Tick's hand stopped over the board, then opened, palm up.
She said it flat, at chart pitch.
"Your husband couldn't find it because the full name is a
graduated return-to-play protocol.
Stepwise. Symptom-driven.
Charted against a baseline we took in August.
'Precautionary hold' is the short way to say it on the radio.
So it's a real category, and it isn't the coach's,
and it isn't the bracket's. It's mine.
The word safety gets borrowed a lot in this county.
For buildings. For brackets. For radio.
I don't lend it out,
and when it comes out of my mouth, it's medicine.
That boy's mother gets everything I know the same day I know
it, in writing, and she has not asked me to hurry.
Good night, Roberta,
and tell your husband he can call my office.
First question's free on Thursdays."

She set the microphone back on its arm, square in front of
him,
and went down the steps into the cold.

Nobody moved a chair,
and the porch went courtroom anyway,
sixty people in coats waiting to see what a coach does with a
live microphone and a ruling already read.

In October, at Delores's supper, by the sheet cakes,
he had said it to her in front of the whole county —
"You have fought me on every call since June" —
and believed himself saying it.
Sitting on it now, mic hot, smoke drifting through the screen,
he finally heard the sentence right.
She had not been fighting him.
She had been holding a line,
and he had been living behind that line the whole season,
the clean program, the printed half hour,
the sheet that came back right every Friday.
A man could spend his whole working life behind a line and
still shrug at it on the radio, if the porch was warm enough.
He had heard himself doing it, and done it anyway.

Tick let the quiet run one breath longer than radio likes,
then leaned in.
"And that's the doctor, friends.
This station don't cut away from the truth,
but it does sell propane. Back in two."
The feed light died.

The porch let out its breath and turned to itself in a low
rumble.
Odell, who fed the barrel smoker on show nights and said maybe
six words an hour,
put two splits of hickory to the fire as if the show had never
happened.
Out in the lot a set of headlights swung around and took the
county road.

And in the two dead minutes before the feed came back,
with the county studying its plates and not studying him,
Dan quit not-knowing the thing he had been walking around all
fall.
He wanted her.
Not the sheet. Not the printed half hour on his schedule.
Not even the best no in three counties.
Her — the woman who had just walked his county's favorite
word back out of its mouth and left him sitting in the warm
with the microphone.
Hell of a time to find it out, he thought.
A week and change from the biggest game of his life,
ninety seconds from a live feed,
and the one call he'd gotten wrong all night still sitting on
the air where he'd left it.

The light came back on.
Tick asked him about the kicking game,
and he talked about the kicking game —
operation time at one-three,
living with a wind out of the north.
Tick asked what worries a coach most in a long layoff,
and he said timing, and hands, and the flu.
The last calls were gates and jackets and where the pep bus
would stage,
and by the top of the hour the porch was nearly easy again.
Nearly.

***

At nine Tick gave the forecast, hard freeze and a Friday wind,
thanked Delmar, thanked the county,
and told everybody the Booster Club had all the propane it
could use and to please start bringing folding tables instead.
"Good night from the Table. Go home. It's twelve degrees."

The porch emptied in coats and taillights.
Delmar stacked chairs.
Tick coiled cable with the headphones around his neck.

Odell banked the smoker and shut the barrel down for the
night,
then came to stand at the rail beside Dan,
gloves in one hand, looking out at the dark lot.

"That station's been on in my kitchen fifty years," he said.
"Ball games and funerals, both kinds of weather.
I'll tell you the one thing I know about it, Coach.
A microphone never healed anybody."

He pulled his gloves on and went to see about his truck.

Dan stayed at the rail while the porch went dark behind him,
Tick's van crunching out of the lot,
the heaters ticking as they cooled.
He buttoned his coat and did not go in just yet.
Off air, two paper plates were left on the rail,
one bite gone from each.
