# Chapter 8 — Coach's Table

POV: Dan, third person limited, past tense.
(Wave-2 blind draft — candidate C, jewel-forge slot; cards D6/D1/D5,
D5 weather-as-texture briefed lyric-dense; 2026-08-30.)

In season, Thursday night in Doss County had a second supper,
and this one was broadcast.
Coach's Table ran an hour on 1340,
live from the screen porch at Delmar's,
and the county turned out in coats
to stand around and watch a man talk on the radio.

Tick Moran kept the card table at the rail end of the porch,
mixer, phone line, two microphones on little stands,
the cables taped down with the pit's own silver tape.
Dan sat the guest stool with one headset ear on and one off,
the way he wore it in a press box,
half for the show and half for the room.

The cold stood up against the mesh with everybody else, looking in.
Breath frosted the screens in patches where the crowd bunched,
bloomed, faded, bloomed again.
Delmar's pit had been going since noon,
and the smoke came around the corner of the building
low and sweet and hung in the lot between the trucks.
Two heaters did what two heaters do for forty people.
Nobody went home.

"You know the drill," Tick told the county at the top of the hour.
"The man is here, the line is open,
and the number hasn't changed since the tower went up."

Delores ran the plate line off a folding table by the door,
brisket and two sides on paper plates that bowed in the middle.
She set one at Dan's elbow during the first ad break
and asked him nothing about it.
He got one bite down at the break.
That turned out to be his supper.

"You're at the Table," Tick said. "Go ahead."

"Ruby Sessions, out the Doss road.
First off, Coach, we love you,
and we are so proud of these boys."

"Yes, ma'am."

"My question. When that boy comes back —
and I know nobody can say when, I know that —
just tell us it'll be done safe.
Lend me the one word, Coach, and I'll hang up happy."

There it was, the whole hour, first call out of the chute.
He had lent that word out once already this fall,
at a supper in October, free of charge,
loud enough to carry a lawn,
and the county had been trying to collect on the loan ever since.
Every line lit on Tick's board tonight
would be the same call in different Sunday clothes.

"Ruby, I'll lend you two better ones," he said.
"Looked after. That young man is looked after,
by the best hands in the state,
and I'm not being modest, I'm being accurate.
Sleep good tonight."

"Bless you, Coach. See you on the nineteenth."

The show went out over the porch's own heads
and came back at it from the lot,
truck radios running the same hour with the doors open,
his voice everywhere twice and a half second apart.
Steam came up off the plate line and quit within the yard.

He didn't see her come in.
He knew anyway; the air on his right side changed its mind.
When he let himself look, between calls,
she was at the rail with a plate,
coat buttoned to the collar,
standing the way she stood at a tent flap,
weight even, hands accounted for.
She ate one bite.
After that she just held it.
There was a version of this hour
where knowing exactly where she stood
made a man broadcast better.
He was not in that version, and he put his eyes back on the mic.

The second caller wanted bracket talk,
and bracket talk was a thing Dan could hand a county whole.
"First round comes to campus. That's seeding, not charity.
You host what you earn — home gate, home locker room, our grass.
The allotment's the allotment, and they'll use every seat of it,
and the rest of that building is ours."

"Harlan Pruett here. Long as they leave us the good end zone,"
Harlan said.

Nobody laughed.
Tick read out the ticket-office hours.

The third was a grandmother who gave her name as Vessie
and did not bother with a question mark.
"Coach, honey, that boy back under center on the nineteenth
is all any of us—"

"Miss Vessie, I keep a card in this jacket pocket
with three words on it.
NO CALENDAR WORDS.
I wrote them myself, in ink, in a hospital lounge,
so they're not mine to break."

"Then whose are they, honey?"

"A doctor's," Dan said.

Tick took it to the feed-store ad.

Delores came by during the ad and topped off his mug,
and he toed Tick's loose cable square while he drank.
Three calls in, and every one of them had come to collect.
He had signed the word safe out in October in his own voice,
and the county had kept the receipt.

The fourth caller had a voice he half knew and couldn't place.
"Charlene, out by the fairgrounds.
Genuine question for the Table, no agenda,
and Coach, we love you.
Is a precautionary hold a real medical category,
or is that a coaching decision?
My husband looked it up and couldn't find it anywhere."

The porch got the particular quiet
of forty people deciding they were only listening.

His mouth had the chuckle out before he'd chosen it,
the fish-fry chuckle, the one that handled things.
"Well, now. I'm not going to grade anybody's homework
on a Thursday night."
Laughter, a little of it, grateful.
"I'll tell you the truth, Charlene.
There's more vocabulary flying around this county right now
than footballs.
Call it a hold, call it whatever the search engine calls it.
I quit refereeing words back in two-a-days.
The young man's being seen to,
I coach the ones in helmets on Saturday,
and that's the part I can speak to. Fair enough?"

"Fair enough, Coach. That's all anybody wanted to hear."

The porch warmed back up around him, loosened, relieved.
Fifteen years of living rooms; he knew a saved room by ear.
He reached for the mug and got it halfway.

A paper plate came down on the rail.
Paper on wood is nothing.
It isn't even a sound.
He heard it over the whole porch.

She came up the rail line with her coat still buttoned,
not fast, not stopping,
and Tick, who had kept live things live
since the transistor was news,
had the guest mic potted up
before her hand finished reaching for it.

"This is Dr. Cole," she said,
and she did not raise her voice for radio.
"Since it's my paperwork being looked up,
I'll save your husband the search.
There is no such thing as a precautionary hold.
Nobody is holding that boy.
He's in a return-to-play protocol.
It's stepwise. It's symptom-driven.
It's written down in his chart,
and my signature sits at the bottom of it every day it runs.
His brain sets the schedule.
Not the bracket. Not this porch. Not the man at this table.
And one more, since the county's on the phone anyway.
You're going to hear the word safe a lot
between now and the nineteenth.
Safe off a flatbed is a pep rally.
Safe on a schedule is a sales pitch.
When it comes out of my mouth, it's medicine.
He gets that one. Good night, Ashford."

She set the mic back on its stand, dead level,
and went down the porch steps into the smoke,
and the screen door didn't even bang.

The porch went courtroom.
Nobody moved a plate.
Forty verdicts got written in the quiet where he could watch.
He had stood inside a silence exactly this shape once before,
in October, at a supper, with a fiddle stopped mid-tune,
when he'd said to her, loud enough for the whole lawn,
"You have fought me on every call since June."
He had believed it when he said it.
He had believed it right up until about a minute ago.

Because that, tonight, was not fighting him.
Fighting him would have been aimed at him.
That was a woman holding a line.
The same line she'd held at the supper,
at the tent flap, on every Friday sheet since the summer,
and the line ran in front of his quarterback,
his season, his program, his name.
She held it for everybody it protected, him first in line,
and got courtroom quiet for wages.
And half a minute ago, with the county on the phone,
he'd been handed one clean turn to hold it with her,
and he had handed back vocabulary.

The dead air ran.
Silence on live radio is billed by the second,
and Tick let it run anyway,
a second past expensive, then two.
A pro's mercy or a pro's verdict; no telling.
Inside that bought quiet,
the thing he had been calling professional respect all fall
turned out to have a shorter name.
He wanted her.
Not the sheet coming back right on Fridays.
Not his quarterback cleared, not the argument finally won.
Aisha Cole. He wanted Aisha Cole, goddamn it,
named at last and plainly,
live, on an open phone line,
at the one hour of his week
when he could do exactly nothing about it
except lean into a microphone and talk about tickets.

"Coach's Table, friends. 1340, from Delmar's porch,"
Tick said, to the county at large.
"We'll pay some bills."

Dan said nothing until the ad was rolling.
Then he went on saying it.

He came off the stool at the break
and carried his plate to the rail,
meaning to eat, and set it down next to hers instead.
Hers had quit being warm a while ago.
Nobody had touched it and nobody had thrown it out.

Odell had the corner post, hat on, cup steaming,
an old man the porch had come with, same as the pit.
He looked at the two plates on the rail
and not at Dan at all.
"Fifty years I've kept that station on," he said.
"Fine company. Finest there is."
He drank.
"But a microphone never healed anybody."

Out past the screens her headlights swung onto the road
and were gone in the smoke.
Dan stood at the rail next to the old man and didn't answer.
There wasn't an answer that improved on it.

The back half of the hour was band seating and travel routes
and one man wanting the allotment explained twice,
and Dan ran it clean on craft alone.
Nobody asked about holds again.
Nobody said the word safe again either,
all the way to the sign-off.

Tick sent the county home to its own radios and cut the feed.
The applause after was porch applause, mittened, brief.
Dan stood for the handshakes and took all of them.
Delores sent the last plates out the door with the stragglers.
Delmar cut the heaters,
and the cold came back through the mesh
and took the porch for itself again.
The frost let go of the screens where the crowd had stood
and ran in beads when the heat died,
little clean lines down through everybody's breath.

By nine tomorrow the board would have her forty seconds
word for word,
and he would read all of it to the bottom, like always,
and that was tomorrow's weather.

Tick coiled his cables. Delmar got the lights.
Off the air, on the emptied porch,
two paper plates were left on the rail,
one bite gone from each.
