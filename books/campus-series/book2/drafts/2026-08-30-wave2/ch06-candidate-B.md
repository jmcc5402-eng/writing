# Chapter 6 — Selection Sunday

POV: Dan Merritt, third person limited, past tense.
FIRST DRAFT — wave-2 blind candidate B, card D6
(let one scene run a beat past comfortable before cutting).
Raw material for the author's own pass;
line and continuity edits follow. Not canon until accepted.

---

Delmar's had cooked all day Sunday for a game nobody was playing.

By dark the hickory smoke lay over the whole low end of Millrow,
and the screen porch stood shoulder to shoulder in coats,
breath-fog inside the screens, kids loose in the yard,
and the two televisions over the pit window
still working through other people's regions.
Dan Merritt came through the yard gate at six
and lost his right hand to the county for a quarter hour.

The team had its own watch running up at the Fieldhouse,
position rooms and pizza boxes,
coordinators posted in the doorways.
He had started the evening there and left it there,
because the players should get their name
in a room full of each other,
and because a town got one December like this, maybe,
and the town's room needed a coach in it too.

Sonny Dillard had claimed the rail nearest the screens.
"Wore the lucky shirt again," he said.
"Three big Sundays running. It's about earned a locker."

Nobody laughed.
A man by the door asked whether the church lot
was open for parking,
and Sonny turned back to the television with his coffee.

Delores Fountain-Mabry ran the food line off two card tables
inside the porch door, foil pans in ranks,
a cake nobody had been allowed to cut yet,
and she fed the crowd in the order of how cold they looked.
"You," she said, to anybody she caught with empty hands. "Plate."

Tick Moran had the remote rig set up on the pit window's sill,
headphones down around his neck between segments,
sending the porch out over 1340 to every kitchen radio
in Doss County.

A coach's Sunday was film and a legal pad
and hating both by ten o'clock.
Dan could not name the last one he'd spent
standing in woodsmoke while the county beat on his shoulders,
and he decided a man could stand more of these
than the schedule ever issued.

The show was in no hurry.
It filled the first region a line at a time
and went to commercial twice doing it.
The porch quieted every time the graphic changed
and let its breath back out every time the names were wrong.
A man read a rumor bracket off his phone
and got told by the whole rail to put it up.
Out in the yard a kid hollered a score from some other game
and was hushed by three grandmothers at once.

The second region filled. Not them.

Dan had turned to hand Delores his empty cup
when the porch went off.

The sound reached him before the screen did,
the whole porch in one voice, tin roof and all,
and under it the yard,
and under that, faint, car horns starting
clean up on the square.
He came around and read it for himself.

ASHFORD.

Then the line under it, in the graphic's block type.
FIRST ROUND. ON CAMPUS. DEC. 19.

Somebody's grandmother kissed his jaw.
The fight song started up in the yard
on no instruments at all.
"Run the flag up the stack tonight!"
somebody hollered from the grass.

"Flag's for wins," Odell Fountain said from the rail,
without turning around. "It'll keep."

He was facing the far corner of the porch
before he'd told himself to look.
Verna Poteat's thermos was down there on the rail,
and the doctor beside it, cup in both gloved hands,
the only still body under the tin.
She was reading the date off the screen
like it was the bottom line of somebody's chart.

He'd have told any microphone in the state
this was for the seniors, and it was.
But standing in the smoke with the county's hands on him,
he admitted the other half where nobody could hear it:
he had wanted a December of his own
longer than anybody under this roof,
and he had never said so in any town that had him.

Tick got the headphones up one-handed
and put the flat voice on.
"It's official, friends. Ashford is in.
First round, on campus, December the nineteenth.
I have read this county's scores on this station
most of my working life,
and that is the first December date
I have ever put on the air."
Then he tipped the microphone over the sill
and let the porch report itself for a while.

"Thirty seconds, Coach," Tick said. "The county's listening."

Dan leaned into the sill.
"Proud of our kids. Proud of our seniors.
This is the town's night — the kids won the games,
and this county built the kind of Saturday
a playoff wants to visit.
We'll see everybody on the nineteenth. Dress warm."
He handed the microphone back
before the porch had quit hollering.

Home game. Campus. December nineteenth.
All week the county had been asking him for one date
and carrying another one it couldn't get,
and the television had just handed twelve thousand people
the first, printed and gold.
The other date still didn't exist.
He'd stood in a hospital lounge and heard why.

Denny Gault held the middle of the porch,
a to-go cup going cold in one hand,
telling it over for each new ring of listeners.
"Missy's home with him. Lights low, sound low, doctor's orders.
She says when the name hit he stood up off the couch,
then sat himself right back down and behaved.
That boy's got more sense tonight than his daddy."
He told it louder each time, and nobody minded.

He got to Dan with both hands,
the cup pinned under one arm.
"Coach." That was as far as the sentence went the first try.
He started over. "I promised Missy
I wouldn't ask anybody a thing tonight. So I'm not asking."

"I know what you're not asking," Dan said.

"Fifteen days," Denny said. "I can count, Coach.
That's all it is. I can count."

"I quit using calendar words in hospitals.
I'm not going to start using them on a porch."

Denny nodded slower than the porch was moving.
"That doctor talks the same way," he said.
"You notice that?"

"I've noticed," Dan said.

The man at Denny's elbow had been waiting his turn
since the graphic changed.
"Great night, Coach. Great night."
He kept Dan's hand a second long.
"One thing, while I've got you.
Playoff travel roster's a hard number — I read that today.
All I'd say is, those spots ought to go to contributors.
Kids who've paid in. Program first. That's all I mean by it."

The circle found its plates.

"Rosters get made in the building," Dan said, easy.
"Same as ever. Now go get you some of that cake
before Delores cuts it down to church-sized pieces."

The man laughed and let go,
and the porch closed back over the question.

The night had two or three natural ends and used none of them.
The fight song went around again, wordless, then with words.
The pans went out a third time.
Men at the rail were already trading shift swaps
for the nineteenth, holding phones up,
calling names across the yard.
A woman read the ticket rules off the athletic site
to a circle of coats, twice, both times into argument.
The cake got cut at nine and was a memory by ten past.
Kids slid on the frosted grass until the frost gave out.
Tick did his sign-off, packed nothing,
and stayed another hour off the air.
When Dan looked for the far corner of the rail again,
on some later pass through the crowd,
the thermos was still there and the doctor wasn't.
The pit cooled by degrees.
The smoke thinned until you could smell the river under it,
same as the night the county had promised itself all of this.
He stayed for the whole long tail of it,
because handing a town its night
meant staying under the tin till the town was done having it.

Delores caught him at the yard gate
with a foil plate already made up,
rubber band around it, fork under the band.
"You shook four hundred hands tonight and ate nothing.
I watched you do both."
She put the plate in his hands
and then didn't let go of it right away,
so that for a moment they were both holding it.

"First playoff in this town's whole life," she said,
looking past him at the porch lights.
"I been cooking for it since five this morning,
and I'll cook for it again come the nineteenth."
She let the plate go.
"Now go on home. And pray for whoever it is
has to say no to any piece of it.
Tonight's the last night that job's easy."

"Yes, ma'am," he said.

She was already headed back to her card tables,
stacking foil pans by feel,
before he had the gate shut behind him.

The plate held its warmth through his coat sleeve
the whole climb up Millrow,
and the porch noise carried behind him
almost to the Fieldhouse lot.

***

He ate what Delores had sent standing over the sink,
coat half off, the kitchen dark except the stove light.
Monday's plan was already built, light on purpose,
jog-through and film and ice tubs,
because the county's night out
would be in everybody's legs by three o'clock.

Last thing, lamp still on, he opened the board.

The bracket thread was six hundred replies deep and gaining.
Somebody had the ticket rules wrong,
and nine people were correcting them,
each a different way.
Somebody wanted the bonfire back for playoff week
and got told the fire department had rules.
Denny had posted a photograph of his own television
with the name on the screen,
no caption, a line of exclamation points,
and the county had left two hundred amens under it.
Every third reply, whatever the thread,
said a prayer for 7.
And under all of it, a week old now,
the clarity thread was still up, still polite.
It had added three replies since supper.
Nobody in it was celebrating.

He read the page to the bottom.
Then he took it back to the top
and came down through it again, slower,
matching porch faces to usernames without trying to.

He turned off the lamp.
Monday's plan sat squared on the dresser for morning,
and the quarterback line on it still read OR.
