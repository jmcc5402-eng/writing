# Chapter 7 — The Coat

POV: Aisha, third person limited, past tense.
(FIRST DRAFT — wave-2 blind candidate, 2026-08-30. Raw material
for panel read and the author's own pass; not final prose.)

---

The freeze had come down from the north over the weekend,
and by Tuesday the radio had quit saying cold snap
and started saying hard freeze, twice an hour,
and nobody in the county accused the radio of drama.
At the practice fields the wind ran the length of the grass
with nothing between Ashford and the pole to slow it,
and the ground had gone hard enough to ring under cleats.

Aisha Cole stood her sideline in her own wool coat,
the one that still carried a little of the bonfire in it,
and learned by the hour what that coat was actually for.
It was built for walking between warm buildings,
and she was on her third hour of standing still.

She kept her hands in her pockets on a schedule,
knuckles working, fingertips against her palms,
because a physician's hands are instruments,
and an instrument gone cold reads false.
If an ankle turned on the far hash before dark,
somebody's December was not going to be graded
by fingers that couldn't tell a tendon from a bootlace.

Cold stacked the deck against her whole trade.
Numb doesn't hurt,
and hurt was half of her early-warning system,
so on afternoons like this she watched gaits instead,
looking for the hitch a boy couldn't feel yet.
The practice reached her mostly as sound:
pads, whistles, the long two-note complaint
of the blocking sled skating over ground gone hard as a road,
and the breath of forty players standing up over the field
in one slow cloud.

At the water break the head trainer brought her
the staff-gear checkout sheet on a clipboard,
held out like an apology.
Program parkas, hooded, down to the knee, school colors.
Every line of the sheet was a name and a size,
large, extra large, double extra,
the column running away from her like a fence.

"Gear order's been men's cut since before I got here,"
the trainer said. "Nobody ever had cause to notice till now.
I can put you in for spring."

"Spring."

"I know how it sounds."

"It sounds like a season I'm expected to survive to.
Is there anything in that cage under a large?"

"There is not. There's a large with nobody's name on it,
if you want to swim to the parking lot."

"I need my hands out and working. I'll finish in wool."

"We've got a case of hand warmers. You want hand warmers?"

"I'll take six."

The sheet wasn't malice; she knew order forms.
Somebody had copied last year's order,
and last year somebody had copied the year before,
back to a year when every name on the sheet was a man's.
That was how a building told you who it had been expecting.

By the last period her back teeth wanted to chatter.
She held them still by main force,
and nobody on that field got to see her do it.

***

The Magnolia Court's office was lit when she pulled in,
the television down low for once,
and Verna Poteat was on the phone at the desk,
half turned from the door,
her voice coming through the storm-door glass with room to spare.

"No, I'll say it to anybody who'll hold still," Verna was saying.
"That woman stands out in that wind all day for those boys.
Doctors get cold same as the rest of us.
Somebody up at that school better see to that woman's coat."

A pause, while Delores said her piece on the other end.

"Well," Verna said. "That's what I told you. Coat."

Aisha let the storm door bang so as not to be eavesdropping.
Verna hung up without ceremony and reached under the desk.

"Soup," she said, by way of hello.
"Delores made a washpot of it
and has opinions about me eating alone."

"You were talking about my coat."

"I was talking to Delores about soup."
The quart jar came across the desk warm through its dish towel,
pressed into Aisha's hands and held there a second,
as if the hands were the point and the jar was the excuse.
"Wind's gone north," Verna said.
"You know what north does to the furnace in nine."

"The dial and I have an understanding."

"The dial's understanding runs about two hours behind.
Use the blanket. That's what it's for."

"Thank you for the soup."

"Thank Delores. I'm just the middleman."

In room nine the heater ticked and caught and ran late,
as advertised.
She ate the soup standing at the dresser,
still in the wool coat,
and her phone lit three times before she finished,
Kat the realtor dealing out listings in a patient row.

*New this morning, two blocks off the square.
Wood floors under that carpet or I'll eat my sign.*

*Brick two-story dropped its price again. Third time.
Getting embarrassing for everybody involved.*

*No rush. Houses only sell every day.*

She read all three and answered none of them.
She told herself it was the hour. It wasn't the hour.
An answer in either direction was a decision,
and she was not making that decision in December,
in a motel room, over soup.

She pulled Verna's blanket up before ten,
cedar still holding out against the woodsmoke,
and the wind kept at the window seam all night
and never got in.

***

The parka was folded over her office chair
when she got to the Annex on Wednesday morning,
team issue, school colors, hooded, down-filled,
the fold lines still in it.
The tag inside the collar said XXL.
Over the left breast, above the staff patch,
block letters said C. COLE.

She hung it on the door and did the morning charting first.
The ladder on the office whiteboard
got one more rung filled in than it had on Friday.
The ladder kept no dates; it never had.

Mid-morning she carried the parka to the equipment cage.
The cage smelled like detergent and dryer heat,
WDSS running low under the tumble,
and the equipment manager had a sideline headset in pieces
on the bench, cord skinned back to bright copper at the flex.

"This was on my chair," she said.

"Cold-snap order," he said, without looking up.
"Half of it came in Monday.
Other half's lost between here and the warehouse,
and the warehouse quit answering the phone about it.
What showed up, showed up."

"It showed up double extra large."

He shrugged, one shoulder, hands staying with the cord.
"Lost orders," he said, the whole explanation.

"It has my name on it."

"Then I'd say it's yours."

He twisted the splice home, slid the shrink tube up the wire,
passed a lighter under it twice, turning the cord as he went,
and ran his thumb down the mend.
It came out cleaner than the factory jacket.
"Cold gets into a cord right at the flex," he said,
to the work more than to her.
"I'll do six of these before New Year's.
This one will outlive the headset."

It was small, sure work, the kind she trusted on sight,
and the county kept turning out to be full of it
once you knew what you were looking at.

She set four facemask bolts on the bench,
bright from riding in her coat pocket since the rivalry game.
"Off seven's mask. I keep finding them."

He rolled them once in his palm
and dropped them into a coffee can on the shelf,
where they landed with the sound of small change.

"I'd wear it," he said, when she was at the door.
"Supposed to stay hard through Friday."

The walk back to the Annex ran straight into the north wind,
so she put it on in the doorway.
The hem reached her knees.
Her hands went in past the cuffs and kept going.
The collar stood up past her ears
and turned the wind into a rumor.
It was, immediately, the warmest she had been outdoors
in this county.
She wore it because it was warm,
and because the alternative was principled shivering,
and she had never seen principled shivering
heal one single ankle.

***

She wore it to the Checkerboard at noon.
She was three steps in the door when Sonny Dillard
set down his fork at the Liars' Table.

"Doc," he said, "did you walk over, or migrate?"

The table enjoyed that more than it deserved.

"There's room in there for a whole second doctor,"
Sonny said, going back for more,
and nobody helped him with that one,
and he ate his eggs.

The woman at the urn poured her coffee unasked
and looked the coat over, up and down, professionally.
"Warm?"

"Very."

She nodded and moved off along the counter.

Earlene Tatum looked up from the county paper.
"Roll the cuffs," she said. "You'll have them in the gravy."

She rolled them, one turn, standing at the register,
and they were down again by the time she reached the truck.

Back at the Annex, the campus mail came after one:
two journals, a supply catalog,
and one envelope with the university's seal printed
in the corner where a person's name would have gone.

> FROM: Facilities Planning and Management
> RE: Revised survey schedule — winter term
>
> Building 12 (Athletics Annex) has been added to the
> January survey window in support of site assessment for
> the proposed Performance Center. All clinical,
> rehabilitation, and records storage functions currently
> housed in Building 12 should complete relocation of
> materials no later than February 1. Interim space
> assignments will follow under separate cover. Questions
> may be directed to your unit administrator.

She read it twice, the second time standing in the doorway
of the file room, where the program's medical records
went back thirty years and change,
paper charts in tight, careful handwriting
belonging to people she had never met,
knees rebuilt in decades when the rebuilds were news,
boys grown into men who sat in the stands now
watching their own sons.
Two rehab rooms, a file room, a taping table
with thirty-odd seasons ghosted onto its vinyl,
all of it due in boxes by the first of February.

Somebody had already drawn the building that wanted the ground.
She had seen it taped to the film-room wall,
three stories of glass holding a permanent sunset.
She had looked for the rehab rooms in that drawing before,
and she had not found them yet.

She taped the notice to the edge of the whiteboard,
squared under the circled date,
so the mornings would start with both of them in view.

The afternoon was charts and phone calls,
the heater in the far rehab room running behind
like everything else she was renting from this county.
What she would not have told one soul
between here and the state line
was that she had put the coat on that morning
for the name as much as for the down.
Everything else she owned in this county
could be in the truck in nine minutes.

The cuffs came down over her hands while she typed,
and she pushed them back,
and they came down again.
She thought of Earlene and rolled them right this time,
two turns apiece, snug above the wrist,
and went back to the afternoon in a coat
sized for somebody else and warm past arguing,
her name over the patch,
the sleeves rolled twice.
