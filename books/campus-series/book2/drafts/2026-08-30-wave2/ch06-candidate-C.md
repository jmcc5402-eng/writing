# Chapter 6 — Selection Sunday

POV: Dan, third person limited, past tense.
(Wave-2 blind draft, candidate C — card D5, weather-as-texture,
briefed lyric-dense per the jewel-forge notice.)

---

Delmar's pit had been going since before church let out,
and the smoke stood off the stack dead straight,
white on a sky scrubbed down to its last hard blue,
a plumb line hung over the square
from higher than this county had ever had business hoping.

Dan Merritt parked on Millrow and walked the two blocks in,
the gravel frozen into its ruts and ringing under his boots,
the puddles cased over in window glass,
his breath keeping a half step ahead of him the whole way.

Nobody had hung a sign, and nobody had needed one.
By half past twelve the screen porch held every coat in the county,
church coats and barn coats and the good hunting orange,
and the television Ray had carried out from the back room
sat up on two feed crates at the end of the rail,
tilted down so the whole length of the porch could see it,
the cord run through the kitchen window
and the window shut on the cord.
Folding chairs had come from the fellowship hall,
and blankets off truck seats,
and nobody sat in the chairs or under the blankets for long.

"Plate first," Ray said at the door, to everyone, all afternoon.
"Screen'll keep. Brisket won't."

Inside the screens the porch made its own weather.
Sixty coats and a pit's worth of borrowed heat,
sixty breaths riding up under the rafters in one slow cloud,
and the cold coming through the wire anyway, fine as sifted flour,
sifting the whole afternoon, and not one word said about it.

Out past the screens the square sat in its Sunday winter light,
frost holding gray in the shade lines, the sun thin and without
weight, the storefronts dark except the hardware store,
whose owner had locked up at noon
and crossed over rather than miss it.

Sonny Dillard had the near rail staked out with a thermos.
"They'll fly that flag off the smokestack on the nineteenth,"
he said. "I'd bet the shirt. I'd bet Ray's shirt."

"Big Sunday, Ray," a man called toward the pit.

"Smoker doesn't know it," Ray said, and turned his coals.

It took Dan the better part of an hour to cross forty feet of porch.
He said "the kids did that" until he quit hearing himself say it,
and every man who took his hand looked past him mid-shake
to be sure the television was still where Ray had set it.

Dr. Cole had the far end of the rail, collar up,
holding a plate she appeared to be guarding rather than eating,
down where the crowd thinned and the pit smoke crossed the screens.
Somebody had put a paper cup of coffee in her free hand anyway.
The county fed you whether you had asked it to or not.
He noted her the once, and left her the quiet end.

A porch this glad was the thing he had been chasing
through every job in five states,
and for one Sunday he let it be Sunday,
and did not go looking for the work in it.

***

The show made them wait.
It dealt out the other half of the country first,
schools with fight songs nobody on the porch could hum,
and it sold a truck between every four names,
and the porch watched the trucks too,
nobody risking the kitchen,
nobody trusting a bracket to hold its place
through a trip to the bathroom.

Denny Gault stood square in front of the screen
with his phone already up in both hands,
filming the television and the porch together,
narrating nothing, saving all of it.

By the time the map came around to their part of the country
the porch had gone quiet enough to hear the pit tick out back
and the screens hum when the wind put its shoulder in.

Dan had one hand on the rail frost
and the other around a cup gone lukewarm an hour ago,
and he kept both hands easy where they were,
because sixty people were watching that screen
and eight or ten were watching him.

A name went up. Not theirs. Another. Not theirs.
Sixty white breaths hung still under the rafters.

Then ASHFORD came up the board in block letters,
a seed number beside it and a little drawn house beside that,
and under it, small, the line the whole porch read twice.
DEC 19. FIRST ROUND. ON CAMPUS.

The sound had no shape to it at first, no words in it anywhere,
just sixty coats' worth of held breath let go at volume.
Then it turned into the fight song,
and the fight song spilled off the porch and into the square,
and car horns took it up on Millrow,
and dogs answered from three streets over,
and the smoke wavered where the porch screens shook.

Ray came out of the kitchen with the serving fork still in his hand
and stood in the doorway and let it all go past him.
Sonny beat the rail flat-palmed till the thermos jumped.
Somebody's grandmother got both arms straight up over her head
like a referee calling the whole day good.

Denny reached Dan through the crowd, took his hand in both of his,
and pumped it like a jack handle.
"Home," he kept saying. "Home, Coach. In December. At our place."

"They earned it in August," Dan said. "Two-a-days. Nobody filming."

"I'm filming now!" Denny had the phone back up over his head,
hollering under his own footage, and the porch hollered with him.

On a whiteboard in the Annex on Tuesday,
Dan had seen the date already, circled in red,
and beside the circle a ladder of steps with no dates on it at all,
the rungs earned, not booked.
Now the circle had a stadium, a seed line, a little drawn house,
and the county's whole voice attached to it.
The county had been keeping two clocks for a week and a day,
the bracket's and the boy's.
As of a Sunday afternoon they pointed at the same date.
Only one of them had hands.

Ray started brisket moving down the rail without being asked,
and Delores Fountain-Mabry, in a chair by the kitchen door
she had not needed to leave all afternoon,
sent a sheet cake out into the noise pan by pan
and kept her own count of the forks.

Denny played the video back for anybody who stood still,
and for plenty who didn't.
"He had it on at the house before I ever got my phone up,"
he said, holding the screen out.
"Missy texted me the second our name went up —
he's on that couch hollering at one television
and I'm on this porch filming another one.
Family's got a system."
He laughed at his own self and played it again.

"First thing I thought of," a man at the rail said, not loud,
"was 7 in that tunnel in December.
First December there's ever been here,
and he's the one gets to run out into it."
Heads went down the rail nodding, easy, sure of it, glad.

Dan heard it the porch's way first, love with a date on it.
He heard it the second way after,
and put the second way in his pocket for the night.

"Now I'm going to say one thing," Denny announced,
"and then I'm done saying things tonight."
The near tables gave him room the size of his voice.
"This program is going where we've all prayed it would go.
And runs like this get built by kids nobody films.
That scout-team bunch gave our boys the whole look
all rivalry week, and you saw Saturday what that was worth.
So while everything's growing, all I'd ask,
and I'm asking it as a friend of this program:
roster spots for contributors. Kids who gave their fall to it.
That's all. That's the speech,
and I'm buying the next round of cake."

There was not a syllable in it with an edge showing.
Forty heads gave it the polite inch,
and the porch went back to its noise.

"That's a real conversation," Dan told him,
"and I'll have it with you Tuesday, long as you want.
Tonight I've got this one."

"Tonight we've got this one," Denny said,
and gripped his shoulder, and went back toward the screen.

Somebody hollered for words.
Dan gave them thirty,
his seniors named one at a time
so the clapping had somewhere else to land,
Ray thanked for the fire and Delores for the cake,
and not one calendar word anywhere in it.

The light went off the square before five,
the fast December dusk with no color in it,
and the television turned lantern,
blue-white on the coats and the breath-cloud,
and the cold came up through the porch boards with the dark
and stood in everybody's boots, and still nobody left.

Delores buttoned her coat one-handed,
the empty cake pans stacked against her hip,
and stopped at the door by Dan and the few nearest chairs.
"First playoff in this town's whole life," she said,
at report-card volume, so that only the six of them got graded.
"I taught half this porch their letters,
and I'm as glad as any fool on it.
Now go home, say your prayers,
and put in a word for whoever it falls on
to say no to any part of it."
She patted the door frame twice like a shoulder
and went down the steps into the dark.

Nobody near the door said anything back to that.
The fight song started up again anyway,
farther down the rail, where it hadn't landed.

Dan looked down the porch without quite deciding to.
The quiet end had been empty a while.
He hadn't seen her go.

He would have denied what came next in every room in the county,
this one included.
Some part of him had been glad all week, low down,
out of the light where he kept it,
that if a no ever had to be said to any of this,
the saying of it would never fall to him.

Denny was the last big voice out.
Dan met him at the steps with a foil plate
Ray had loaded heavy on the brisket end.
"For the couch," Dan said.
"Tell him it's a home game. That part's confirmed."

Denny looked at the plate longer than a plate calls for looking.
"First thing through the door," he said.
"Before my coat's off."
He took Dan's forearm in his free hand and squeezed once,
and carried the plate down the steps
like it was the bracket itself.

Dan stayed while the porch emptied by families into the cold,
shook everything offered,
and was the last coat out but Ray's.
Behind him Ray unplugged the television through the window
and carried it back inside,
and the porch stood empty with the pit banked low
and the smoke still climbing straight into the dark.

***

His house was dark when he got in
and he left most of it that way, one lamp, the bedroom,
Monday's schedule printed and squared on the dresser.

Denny's video had been pinned to the top of the parents' board
since suppertime.
He watched it muted and heard the whole thing anyway.
Under it the county ran long and glad,
photographs of other porches and other living rooms
at the same second of the same afternoon,
parking questions three weeks early,
a report that the visiting county would come up
a hundred motel rooms short and serve it right,
an argument, friendly, about reprinting the season shirts
with one more line on them,
and no hard question anywhere in the column.

He read the board down to the last reply,
the whole column of it, glad top to bottom.
Then he went back up and came down it again, slower,
in case the night had turned somewhere while he was reading.
It hadn't. Not yet.
Twelve thousand people, one date, all of it joy.

December nineteenth had a stadium now, a bracket line,
the county's whole voice attached.
The other date still didn't exist.
He put the phone on the charger, turned off the lamp,
and lay there a while with the wind crossing the eaves.
