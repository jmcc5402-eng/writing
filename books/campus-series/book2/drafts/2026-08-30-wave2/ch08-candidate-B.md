# Chapter 8 — Coach's Table

POV: Dan, third person limited, past tense.
(Wave-2 blind draft, candidate B — card D1 sound-forward.
FIRST DRAFT per plots/wave2-directive.md, 2026-08-30.)

---

Coach's Table went out live from Delmar's screen porch
every Thursday night of the season,
one hour on WDSS, 1340 anywhere in the county you stood,
and in all its years on the air the season had always quit
before December could get at it.
Tonight the show wore coats.

Delmar had the smoker drafting sweet at the yard end,
lid down, and the porch heaters ran their low orange hum.
Tick Moran sat at a card table behind a mixer
older than most of the roster,
headphones half on, one ear for the broadcast
and one ear for the room,
and when the seven o'clock tone came down the line
he opened the way he always opened.

"You're at the Table, friends.
We got a cold porch, a full porch, a coach, and a phone line.
Delmar's got ribs if you're quick and sides if you're not.
Call us. You know the number better than your own."

Somebody put a paper plate in Dan's hands
on his way to the guest chair —
brisket, beans, a roll riding the edge of it.
He got one bite down before the on-air bulb lit,
and set the plate on the rail behind him,
staged where he could find it in the dark later,
like everything he meant to come back to.

He heard her arrive before he saw her.
The crowd noise at the yard steps opened a seam,
a patch of quiet that moved, and settled, and stayed,
and under the smoker's draft came Verna Poteat's
porch-step laugh, and the scrape of a serving spoon
doing up one more plate.
Dr. Cole had come to the Table, then.
He kept his eyes on Tick
and let his hearing do what it wanted about it,
which was everything.

The first caller was a granddad from out past the fairgrounds,
the phone line sanding his voice down to burlap.
"Home playoff. Our own grass. December nineteenth.
Coach, I waited my whole life for this
and I do not have the words."

"Then we're even," Dan said.
"I've been rationing mine since Sunday."

The porch laughed easy,
and the same laugh came back out of a truck radio
in the yard a half breath late,
the county listening to itself listen.

"Now tell an old man it's all going to be safe out there,"
the granddad said. "The cold, the boys, the whole business.
Say that one word and I'll let you go."

"I'll say the kids will be coached
within an inch of their lives,
and the trainers pack for weather," Dan said.
"That's the end of the field I own."

"Close enough," the granddad said,
and was gone in a click and a hum.

The hour wanted one word out of him.
He heard it early and he heard it often,
callers leaving the door open for *safe*,
propping it, sweeping the porch off in front of it.
He had lent that word out once, in October,
at a supper with sheet cakes and a fiddle,
and the county had never handed it back.

The second caller was a mother
who was not asking about anybody's evaluation.
She wanted that understood before she asked
how long a boy generally stays in the concussion protocol,
asking for the group, so families could plan their Decembers.

"I wrote myself a rule on a card two weeks back,"
Dan said. "It says NO CALENDAR WORDS.
Best coaching I've taken all year, and I'm keeping it.
The day I know a thing this county can have,
this county will have it."

Tick rode a station break in over the top.
"Delmar says if the ribs run out
he's got opinions instead, and those stay free."

The porch went on eating.
Tick read the feed store and came back to the phones.

The fourth caller had a kitchen behind her voice,
a dish-towel sound, a television low in another room.
"Genuine question for the Table," she said,
"and no agenda behind it, I want that understood.
Is a 'precautionary hold' a real medical category,
or is that more of a coaching decision?
My husband looked it up and couldn't find it anywhere."

He knew the phrase. It wasn't hers and it wasn't his.
It was a board phrase; he had watched it grow all fall
on his phone, a little taller every night.
And he heard the fork built into the question,
polite as Sunday.
Real category, or somebody's choice a man could argue with.
Medicine, or her.

"Well," Dan said, and heard the shrug go out over the air
in his own voice, easy as anything.
"I'm going to stay in my lane on categories, ma'am.
Paperwork words aren't my end of the building,
and I couldn't tell you where every one of them comes from.
What I can tell you is we're all waiting on the same news,
and nobody on this porch is waiting harder than me."

The porch made its warm sound.
Relief, agreement, forks going again.
Somebody over by the smoker said, "There you go."
It had worked.
His mouth had bought rooms in five states,
and it had just bought this one,
and the purchase went out over 1340 with his name on it.

Behind him, the rail took a paper plate,
light as a card laid down.

***

She was leaving.
He knew it by the seam closing in the crowd,
by the boards taking her weight in even steps,
and her way out ran along the rail,
past the card table, past the mic.

She stopped.

"Tick," she said, off the air, level as a Friday sheet.
"One minute of your Table."

Tick was a showman down to the marrow.
He had the guest mic turned her way
before the porch understood what it was watching.
"Friends, the Table's got company.
You know her from the best sideline in the state.
Doc Cole."

She delivered standing.
The compressor had nothing to do; she arrived level,
and her voice went out through the porch speaker
and came back off a dozen truck radios in the yard
a half breath late, so that she was everywhere at once.

"Good evening. This is Dr. Cole.
To the caller's question — your husband couldn't find
'precautionary hold' because it is not a medical term.
It isn't mine, and I won't be adopting it.
It grew up on a message board, and it can live there.
What I run is a return-to-play protocol.
It is written down. It is stepwise. It is symptom-driven.
It moves when his brain says move,
and it does not keep a calendar.
Mine, yours, or the bracket's.
And while I have the porch,
the word safety has been getting borrowed
all over this county this fall,
so let me tell you where it lives.
On a podium, that word is marketing.
When it comes out of my mouth, it's medicine.
Only one of those is up for negotiation,
and my office hours are posted.
Thank you, Tick. The ribs are worth the cold."

She set the mic back in its cradle herself.
The screen door made its small complaint,
and her steps went down the yard stairs
in the same even time they had come up in,
and nobody on that porch said a word while they went.

The mic sat live in front of him.
Tick's eyebrows asked the question,
and the whole porch waited on the answer.
Anything he put on the air now would be a rescue
she had not asked for, or another dollar of the same
soft money he'd just spent, and he sat there and spent neither.
The dead air ran three full seconds.
On 1340, that is a civic event.
He heard every kitchen in Doss County hear him say nothing.

"That's the Table, friends," Tick said.
"Real porch, real doctor. Let's pay a bill or two."

The feed-store ad rolled.
Out in the dark an engine turned over,
and headlights swung across the screen and away.

Under the ad the porch stayed courtroom-quiet,
that particular quiet with a verdict in it.
He had stood inside this exact quiet once before,
beside a sheet-cake table in October,
the night he told her, with a county listening,
*you have fought me on every call since June.*
He had believed it, saying it.
Tonight, in the cold, he finally heard it right.
She had never been fighting him.
Not then. Not four minutes ago.
She had been holding a line,
and every Friday of his clean, careful program
was lived out on the safe side of it.

The show found its way back, because shows do.
A man wanted to know about parking on the nineteenth.
A woman reported a casserole dish three potlucks overdue,
described it down to the chip in the lid,
named no names, and everybody knew the name.
Tick played a song older than the porch
and signed the county off gentle.
"That's Coach's Table. Drive slow. It's December out there."

***

The on-air bulb went dark,
and the porch came off the air into its own noise —
tailgates, foil going over pans,
engines climbing away up the dark one set at a time.
Tick coiled cable by feel and offered no comment at all,
just set a coffee at Dan's elbow on his way past,
black, in a paper cup,
then carried his rig to the van in two trips
and lifted a hand from the yard.

Odell had the folding chairs going two at a time.
He stopped at the rail beside Dan with the last pair
on his hip, an old man breathing like the work was easy
and the standing still was the hard part.

"I've kept that station on since Eisenhower," Odell said.
"It's good company.
But a microphone never healed anybody."

He carried the chairs in through the kitchen door,
and the door clapped shut behind him.

The heaters ticked as they cooled.
The smoker was down to coals.
The last engine faded off up the road,
and after that there was just the cold
and the creak of the boards under his own weight.

Dan stood at the rail and ran the tape honest,
the same tape he'd run for any kid
who bailed out of a clean pocket.
The protection had held all night. The line had held.
The line was her.
He'd had all the time in the world to step up into it,
and he had climbed out the back
and called it staying in his lane.

And underneath that, late, plain,
in no hurry now that the porch was empty,
the other thing said its name where he could hear it.

He wanted her.

Not the signature. Not the truce.
Not the Friday paper that came back right
and kept his program clean.
Aisha Cole, who would stand up alone
on a cold porch in front of the whole listening county
and hold the line for a boy
who would never even know it got held.
He wanted her, and he had learned it for certain
in the same hour he showed her, on the record, at volume,
exactly what his help was worth.
Hell of a scouting report, he thought.
Right call, a season late, and written on himself.

Inside, Delmar's kitchen light went out,
and the porch bulb after it.
With the heaters off, the cold came straight up
through the boards.
Off air, the porch was boards and hickory and dark,
and on the rail, side by side where the two of them
had left them, sat two paper plates,
one bite gone from each.
