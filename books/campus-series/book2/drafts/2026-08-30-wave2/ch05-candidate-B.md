# Chapter 5 — Two Calendars

POV: Aisha, third person limited, past tense.
(Blind wave-2 candidate B — slot card D2, hands and objects.
Brief: plots/wave2-directive.md, CH 5 — "Two Calendars.")

---

Aisha Cole let herself into the Annex at six forty on Tuesday
and brought the old infirmary up bank by bank,
east hall last,
because the east hall's lights hummed a while before they caught.

The building had been the county's infirmary
back when the WPA poured it,
and the athletic department had swallowed it long ago
for overflow sports medicine.
Rehab rooms down the east hall,
parallel bars and a cold-tub room with its own weather.
Thirty years of records shelved in the basement in county order.
And at the end of the hall, behind a door
with her name on a card taped over somebody else's,
her office.

It was the one room in the county she had unpacked.
The diplomas hung here, not in the truck.
The references stood spine-out over the desk
in the order her hand knew without her eyes.
The kit's twin sat under the desk, latched, handle out,
and the desktop was bare except for a mug of pens
she had tested one by one.
The room at the Magnolia Court could be in the truck
in nine minutes.
This room would take a day and a dolly.
She had built it that way on purpose,
and had told nobody,
and would not have known how to start telling.

The whiteboard held the wall across from the desk,
and the whiteboard held two calendars.

On the left, in red: DEC 19, circled twice,
the circle gone over so many mornings it shone.
The playoff's first round had its date printed a year out,
and half the county could say it in its sleep,
and now so could she.

On the right, in black, the protocol's ladder:
symptom-free at rest, then light aerobic,
then sport-specific, then non-contact,
then full contact, then play.
Six rungs in her block printing,
and not a date anywhere on that side of the board,
because nobody had ever taught a healing brain
to read a schedule,
and she would not be the first to pretend otherwise.

She had written the county's date up there herself,
Monday morning, red marker, two passes.
A thing on her board was a thing she could look at,
and a thing she could look at was not looming.

At six fifty she ordered the imaging.
The Saturday CT was clean,
and clean was good,
and clean was not the same as cleared.
She took the Thursday-morning cancellation up the highway,
wrote the referral herself,
and set the full neurocognitive battery for Friday,
scored line by line against the August baseline.
The protocol required none of it.
The university had asked for none of it.
She blocked the drive time out of the column
with her own name at the top
and signed the order the same size she signed everything.

Then she charted Monday's evaluation in order:
symptom score down two from Sunday and not at zero;
sleep, screens, light;
his answers taken down exactly,
none of her own adjectives anywhere in them.
App copy and paper copy both.
She had learned young what uncharted things cost.
It was not a lesson that needed refreshing.

Tuesdays she ate at the Checkerboard before rounds.
Eggs, coffee, the short end of the counter.
A doctor the county could watch eating
was a doctor the county was less afraid of,
and this week the county needed the watching
more than she needed the eggs.

***

The Checkerboard was down to one cook.
Birdie Pham had the flattop to herself,
five tickets turning on the wheel,
working them in an order only she could see,
and the coffee pot traveled the counter hand to hand
on the honor system.
The front windows had steamed themselves solid
against the cold coming off Millrow.

The Liars' Table had its window.
Earlene Tatum sat with her back to the glass.
Sonny Dillard still carried the engine bay on his coat.
Odell Fountain worked a toothpick like a long project.

Aisha took the short end of the counter and ordered eggs.
She was two bites in when Earlene got her phone out
and held it at arm's length, screen up, like a citation.

"He posted," Earlene said.

"Read it," Sonny said. "You've been circling it since you sat."

"Denny G.," Earlene read.
"'Proud of these boys tonight.
Nobody prouder than this house,
and nobody here is blaming a soul.
Just asking for clarity on the evaluation timeline
so families know what to expect going forward.
That's all this is.'"

"Count the nobodies," Sonny said.

"Two so far," Earlene said. "He isn't done.
DeeAnn Prewitt.
'Praying so hard for #7 and his sweet family tonight.
Does anybody know how long these evaluations usually run?'"

"She means that kindly," Sonny said.

"She does. It still reads like a stopwatch.
B. Hollis.
'Brother-in-law does sports medicine two counties over.
Says days not weeks, most cases.
Not saying that's this case.
Just perspective for everybody.'"

"Whose brother-in-law is that?" Odell said.

"Everybody's," Sonny said.
"He lives two counties over on purpose."

Odell moved the toothpick to the other side.
"My cousin got his bell rung in '78. He's been fine."

Nobody took it up. Earlene read on.

"PomPoms and Prayers.
'She is being SO careful and we all appreciate that!!
Careful is right.
Just hoping careful comes with a calendar,
for the family's sake.'"
She gave the SO both barrels, as written.
"And Denny again.
'Nobody here is questioning anybody.
We love this program.
Clarity is not criticism.'"

She leaned the phone against the sugar shaker.

"It's criticism," she said.

Birdie's spatula went on scraping the flattop.
Sonny turned his mug a half turn on the wood.

"I have ordered every part on that pumper two times over,"
he said.
"Gauge glass. Foot valve. The whole relief train.
County writes the check, the part ships, I bolt it in,
and nobody stands over my shoulder wanting a calendar."
He looked into the mug and not at anybody.
"Brains aren't a part you can order twice."

Nobody argued with that either.
The pot came down the counter;
Aisha filled her cup and sent it on.

Birdie lifted the dome off the pie by the register,
cut a quarter of it loose in one long stroke,
and came down the counter with the plate.
A quarter of a whole pie, cut as one piece,
the point of it aimed up the counter like a prow.
She set it in front of Aisha, unasked,
squared the plate to the counter's edge,
and went back to her tickets without a word said.

The Table watched the plate land.

"You know whose slice that is," Sonny said.

"I've seen the sign," Aisha said.

She turned the plate a quarter turn,
took up her fork, and cut the point off clean.

"I'm eating it where he can see me," she said.

Earlene lifted her coffee an inch off the wood,
and no more was made of it,
and the pie was as advertised.

They had waited till she was inside to read it aloud.
That was the ruling underneath the other rulings —
the board would talk where she couldn't see it,
and this table would talk where she could,
and pass her the coffee while it did.

She paid her ticket at the register
and left four ones under the pie plate,
because the slice hadn't been on the ticket
and she meant to stay square with this county.

***

Rounds, clinic, two ankles and a wrist,
the light gone off Millrow by five.
She was back at the Annex charting under the desk lamp
when the knock came at six,
two knuckles on the propped-open door.

Dan Merritt stood in the hall with his cap in his hand,
whistle lanyard still around his neck,
practice still all over his jacket.

"Doc. You got ten minutes?"

"For what?"

"The ladder." He nodded past her at the board.
"Sunday they put the bracket up,
and Sunday this county is going to ask me for a date.
Teach me the rungs so I say them right.
I'd rather carry your sentence than build my own."

She looked at him one second longer
than the request strictly needed.
Then she stood and took the black marker from the tray.

"Rung one." She touched the bottom line.
"Symptom-free at rest.
No headache, sleeping normal, screens managed,
and none of it by report only. I check.
There's nothing to do on rung one but tell the truth and wait.
Rung two, light aerobic.
Stationary bike, twenty minutes, heart rate capped,
somebody watching the whole ride.
Rung three, sport-specific.
Running, cuts, his footwork, no ball, nobody near him.
Rung four, non-contact team drills.
Helmet, no pads, nobody so much as brushes him.
Rung five, full contact, a full practice of it.
Rung six is football."

"How long is a rung?"

"Twenty-four hours at the shortest,
and only if the rung under it stayed clean.
Any symptom, any rung, he steps down one
and the clock starts over.
That's not caution. That's the floor.
Caution is what I build on top of it."

She watched him run the arithmetic
and keep the sum to himself.

"People are going to do that math," he said.

"Let them. Math isn't the problem.
A best case is a number,
and a number gets loose in this county
and comes back wearing a jersey."

"Where is he on it now?"

"Not on it. You get on at zero symptoms.
He's close to zero and he isn't there,
and close doesn't start clocks."

"And Thursday?"

So the trainers had talked. Trainers always talked.
"Thursday is imaging," she said.

"The CT was clean Saturday night.
An MRI's not standard for this."

"It's not. It's extra.
Extra imaging, my own order, my own dime of time.
Come February, when somebody official asks
whether the doctor was thorough,
I'm going to hand them a stack and go back to work.
I don't chart to remember, Coach.
I chart to be believed."

He looked at the ladder a while after that.
Then he took the script cards out of his jacket,
found a clean one,
and copied the ladder onto it in block letters,
standing at the board to do it,
and that put them shoulder to shoulder at the rungs,
her marker still uncapped in her hand,
his cap hanging from the hand that braced the card.
Neither of them stepped back to make room.
There was room.

He copied all six rungs and no dates,
because there were no dates to copy,
block letters a manager could read at a jog.

"Sunday they get six rungs and nothing else," he said.
"Word for word."

"Word for word I'd pay to hear."

He stopped in the doorway with the cap back on
and looked at the wall once more,
the red circle and the black ladder sharing it.

"You've fought me all season," he said.

"And I'm undefeated."

He knocked once on the frame
with the same two knuckles that had asked to come in,
and went down the hall.
The outside door thumped.
The truck caught, and faded up toward campus,
and then it was the radiators and her.

She stood in front of the board
with the marker still uncapped in her hand.

Plainly, then, since plain was the house specialty.

She wanted him.

Not the fight,
though the fight was the best hour of most of her weeks.
Not his respect — that came back to her signed
every Friday at four fifteen.
Him. The man who had just copied six rungs and no dates
onto a card in his own block letters
and hadn't asked her to shorten a single one.

She was forty-one years old,
she kept the cleanest chart in the conference,
and she wanted the coach.
And the word for that — she made herself say the whole word,
at chart-reading pitch, nobody in the building but her —
was inadvisable.

Inadvisable stood.
So did the want.
It had her own stubbornness in it,
and no symptom she knew a treatment for,
and no dates on it anywhere.

She capped the marker and set it in the tray.
She took her coat off the hook,
stopped in the doorway with her hand on the switch,
and gave the wall a last look.
The county's calendar burned red inside its circle,
sure of its one date.
Beside it the ladder climbed in black,
six rungs, no dates, her printing steady all the way up
into the clean white where the answer wasn't written yet.
