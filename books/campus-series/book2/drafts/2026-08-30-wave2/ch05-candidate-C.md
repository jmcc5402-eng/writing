# Chapter 5 — Two Calendars

POV: Aisha, third person limited, past tense.
(FIRST DRAFT — wave-2 blind candidate C, per

---

Frost had come up the annex windows overnight in ferns,
and the eight o'clock sun sat too low over the quad
to do anything about it except light it.
Aisha kept her coat on through the first hour of charting.
The radiators in the old infirmary spent that hour knocking,
somewhere down under the terrazzo,
and turned to heat only when they were good and done.

The Annex had been the campus infirmary
back when the WPA poured its floors,
and it still behaved like one —
transom windows, deep sills,
terrazzo gone cloudy at every doorway,
brick that banked the cold all night
and let it back out all morning.
The athletic department had swallowed the building years ago
for overflow sports medicine,
but the building had never heard about it.
Her office was the old admitting room.
DR. A. COLE sat typed on a card in a steel holder on the door,
and of every room she had keys to in Doss County,
this was the one she had unpacked.

On the whiteboard, the length of the admitting wall,
the county's winter hung in two columns.
On the right, alone, DEC 19, circled in red.
She had written the date up there herself, in November,
big enough to read from the doorway.
On the left, in her chart hand, the ladder.
Step one, symptom-limited rest.
Step two, light aerobic, her bike, her heart-rate cap.
Step three, sport-specific, nothing that spins the head.
Step four, non-contact with the team.
Step five, full contact, her signature on a page first.
Step six, a game.
No dates on it anywhere. Not one.
Every rung cost twenty-four clean hours at the rung below,
symptoms anywhere sent the climb back down a step,
and no bracket, no booster, and no red circle
could buy the next rung an hour early.
A date that could not move,
and a ladder that could not be hurried,
six feet of whiteboard apart.
She looked at the pair of them together every morning,
on purpose, before the coffee.

Her phone had buzzed at six forty-one,
a photograph of a notebook page,
ruled columns in a school-supply spiral,
Missy Gault's night set down in times along the left margin.
Woke at 5:15 on his own. No lights, no screens, his choosing.
Headache a three on waking, down from Sunday's seven.
Water, toast, back asleep by eight.
Aisha charted every line of it into the record,
collateral report, parent,
and typed back: *Perfect. Keep going. Same tomorrow.*

Then she opened the ordering portal
and did the thing the protocol did not ask of her.
Baseline-plus imaging on the clean CT's heels,
scheduled Thursday morning —
extra imaging, her own order, her own dime of time —
with a neuroradiologist's second read
she would sit in on by video after clinic hours.
Whoever came asking later could ask the record.

She wrote it all down, in order, in her own hand,
time-stamped, signed while it was fresh.
She had learned young what an uncharted thing can cost.
Young enough that the lesson had no words left on it,
only the habit it had made,
and the habit did not care whether she was tired.

***

By ten the light on the square was bright and useless,
and the flags on the courthouse lawn
had stiffened into their folds.
The Checkerboard sat at one cook between rushes,
the griddle down to a murmur,
the front window fogged in its corners
where the Liars' Table was in session over eggs.
Earlene Tatum had her phone out at arm's length
and her reading glasses far down her nose.
Tunk Ferrell was buttering toast he had no plans for.
Sonny Dillard sat with his fire-department coat
over the chair back.
Aisha took the counter two stools down from the register
and ordered coffee and eggs from the cook,
and nobody at the window turned around,
and everybody at the window knew she was there.

"Here it is," Earlene said, to the Table at large.
"Top of the board since midnight Saturday, still growing.
'Proud of these boys tonight.
Nobody prouder than this house,
and nobody here is blaming a soul.
Just asking for clarity on the evaluation timeline
so families know what to expect going forward.
That's all this is.'"

"Signed?" Tunk said.

"Denny G."

"Man puts his name on his worry," Sonny said.
"Give him that much."

"Replies," Earlene said, and scrolled.
"'Brother-in-law does sports medicine two counties over.
Says days not weeks, most cases.
Not saying that's this case.
Just perspective for everybody.'"

"B. Hollis," Tunk said.
"B. Hollis's brother-in-law diagnosed my knee at a fish fry.
Wrong knee."

Nobody laughed. Earlene kept scrolling.

"'She is being SO careful and we all appreciate that!!
Careful is right.
Just hoping careful comes with a calendar,
for the family's sake.'
Two exclamation points," Earlene said. "After 'that.'"

"Ruling," Tunk said.

Earlene took the glasses off and folded them on the table.
"Ruled that it's polite," she said. "Every word.
That's the trouble with it.
It don't ask anybody in particular,
so nobody in particular can ever be done answering it."

Sonny had been quiet through the reading,
squaring his toast crusts into a little levee
around the last of his eggs.
"I fit those boys for helmets every August at the fire hall,"
he said, to his plate as much as to the Table.
"Helmet cracks, you fill out the form,
new one's on the truck Thursday.
Brains aren't a part you can order twice."

Nobody ruled on that.
The griddle ticked. The urn finished a cycle.
Earlene put her glasses back on.

The cook came off the griddle with one plate,
walked it the length of the counter,
and set it down in front of Aisha, unasked.
A quarter of a whole pie, cut as one piece,
standing up off the plate like public works.

"I didn't order this," Aisha said.

"No," the cook said, and went back to the griddle.

At the window, Earlene looked over the top of her glasses.
"That'll be on the board by supper, Doctor.
Somebody in this room will see to it."

"Good," Aisha said. "I'm eating it where he can see me."

Tunk raised his coffee an inch.
The Table moved on down its docket
to the dog still missing out past the fairgrounds,
brown, sixty pounds, still at large,
still answering to nothing.

She ate the eggs and then the whole quarter,
down to the crumbs.
Nobody at that window had asked her one question,
with the county starving for the answers she had.
Fed instead of deposed.
She would take that trade any Tuesday of the season,
and nobody in the room would ever hear it from her.
She put four ones under the sugar jar on her way out,
because the sign said four dollars,
and she intended to be able to say she'd paid it.

***

Practice noise came across the road all afternoon,
whistle and air horn and the far-off clatter of pads,
while the light went out of the transoms early
and the rooms came warm at last.
At a quarter to six the whistle quit for the day.
At six there were footsteps in her hallway,
rubber soles on terrazzo.

He came in still dressed for the field,
cap in one hand, whistle riding his chest,
a legal pad rolled in the other like a kid's diploma.

"Doc. You got ten minutes for a football coach?"

"Eight," she said, and capped her pen.

He went straight to the whiteboard. Everyone did.
He read the ladder rung by rung, cap in hand,
then the red circle, then the ladder again.

"Walk me through it," he said. "Coach terms."

"Step one ends after a full day without symptoms at rest.
Step two is light aerobic — my bike, my heart-rate cap,
this building, my table's schedule.
Step three he touches a football again.
Nothing that spins the head.
Step four he's back around your team, non-contact.
Step five is full contact,
and he does not reach step five
without my signature going onto a page first.
Step six is a game."

"Time between rungs."

"Twenty-four clean hours each, minimum.
Symptoms anywhere on the climb send him down a rung,
and we start that rung over.
The ladder doesn't know what week it is.
That's not a flaw in the ladder. It's the design."

He stood there running arithmetic against the circle,
and she watched him swallow every number he came to.

"Sunday night they'll put our name on a bracket," he said.
"With a date under it."

"There's a date on my board already. I wrote it up myself."

"Why would you do that to yourself?"

"Because I'd rather stare at a thing
than find out it got behind me."

He nodded at that, slow, and kept his eyes on the ladder.
She stepped up beside him
to add the heart-rate cap onto step two.
"One thirty-five," she said, writing,
and the red marker dragged, thinned,
and quit in the middle of the five.
He was still talking, something about his Tuesday presser,
every question the same question wearing a different hat,
and his hand had already gone to the windbreaker pocket
and come out with a fresh marker,
uncapped, right side up, set in her palm
without his losing the thread of his own complaint.
She finished the number in his red.

"Give me the sentence for the staff," he said,
"and I'll carry it whole."

"He's on the protocol.
The protocol has steps, not dates.
Anybody who needs more than that gets sent to me."

He said it back to her word for word, eyes shut,
a man learning a two-minute call,
then wrote it on the legal pad anyway.

At the door he put the cap back on and stopped,
one hand on the frame.

"You've fought me all season," he said.
It didn't come out like a complaint.
It came out like a scouting report.

"And I'm undefeated," she said.

He laughed once, mostly breath,
knocked a knuckle twice on the doorframe, and went.
His footsteps ran back down the terrazzo,
and the outside door opened,
let in one gulp of December, and shut on it.

She had been listening for that door without deciding to.

She signed the last chart of the day.
She squared the pens in the tray.
The radiators ticked on in the walls.

She wanted him.

There it was, at ten past six on a Tuesday in December,
in a room that smelled of radiator paint and alcohol swabs.
She wanted Dan Merritt —
not the flatbed version the county owned,
the one who had stood in her office counting rungs
and swallowing the numbers,
who carried spare markers and other people's sentences whole.

She was forty-one years old and precise for a living,
and she gave the finding its right name. Inadvisable.
Cortisone logic. You could have the relief today,
and the joint itself would pay for it by spring.
She did not argue with the finding,
and she did not chart it anywhere,
and it changed nothing on the board behind her.

Outside, the frost was already starting again
at the transom corners, first ferns of the night shift.
The security light across the walk came on
and put a small false moon in the window glass.
On the whiteboard the red circle kept its color
after everything else in the room had gone gray,
and the ladder stood beside it in the half dark
with every rung in place and none of them numbered,
and his marker lay in the pen tray under both calendars,
capped, nearly new,
the one thing in the room
that had come in out of the cold and stayed.
