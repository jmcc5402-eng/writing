# Chapter 7 — The Coat

POV: Aisha, third person limited, past tense.
(FIRST DRAFT — wave-2 blind draft, candidate C.
Card D5: weather as texture, never as topic; briefed lyric-dense.)

---

The practice field had frozen from the bottom up,
and by Tuesday afternoon cleats rang on it,
and every dropped ball bounced wrong and far,
like something ceramic let go on a tile floor.
The painted numbers wore frost down in their grooves.
Ice stood in the hose bib like a cork.
The extension cords had gone stiff as rebar
and lay where they had been flung.
The field did not even smell like a field;
the freeze had shut the grass smell off at the main.

Aisha Cole stood her end of the sideline
in the wool coat she had bought years ago for a city
where weather was a thing between two doors,
and the wind came through its seams
like the coat had never been there.

The staff gear had come up from the cage at noon,
a rolling bin of navy parkas with the tags still on,
and the packing slip taped to the lid had one column.
L. XL. XL. XXL. XXL.
There was no line on the program's order form
where her size had ever been,
and nobody had decided that.
It had been ordered by habit, the same habit,
going back longer than most of the buildings.
She charted the afternoon in her head
under exposure, staff, unmitigated,
and stood the rest of it in city wool.

"Tape's not biting today," the head trainer said,
peeling off a strip that had given up whole.
"We're double-wrapping anything that flexes."

"Under-wrap first, then the white," she said.
"And pass the word again.
Anybody who says the word numb
comes to me before he goes back in a drill.
Toes count."

"Toes count," he said.

"And move the heaters in behind the bench, both of them.
Rotate your linemen through. Two-minute turns."

He went on up the line with the tape.

Her radio clicked at the half-period.
"Booth to Cole. Glass is icing up here.
If you want eyes on anything, ask early."

"Copy," she said. "Watch get-ups, not the ball."

Breath hung over the huddles and moved off in one piece.
Managers went up the sideline
cracking water bottles loose from their crates.
At the far end Merritt ran his periods
off the printed schedule, hat down, collar up,
voice carrying flat and level across the frozen grass,
and the periods changed on the horn, on time,
cold or no cold.

By the last one her feet had gone from cold to theoretical,
and she printed her final note in block letters
because her cursive had quit for the day.
When the horn ended it,
she was the first one off the grass,
and she was not ashamed of it.

***

The wind swung all the way north after supper.
At the Magnolia Court the VACANCY tube buzzed against it,
and the parked rigs took the gusts broadside and boomed.
The office glowed at the end of the row.
Verna had the phone at her ear
and the television murmuring under it,
and she crooked one finger at the glass,
and Aisha came in out of the wind
to heat as thick as a greenhouse.

"I know it," Verna was saying into the phone.
"I watched her cross my lot just now
like a woman coming in off the ice floes."
The phone talked a while.
"Because doctors get cold same as the rest of us, Delores.
Somebody had better see to that woman's coat."

"I can hear you," Aisha said.

"Good." Verna tipped the receiver against her shoulder.
"Furnace in nine runs behind when the wind's north.
You know that by now. Take the little heater."
She hauled a space heater up from under the desk,
the cord wound in a figure eight.
"Bring it back when the wind swings.
And don't set it near the curtains.
I know where you live."

"You'd know before I did," Aisha said,
and took the heater.

Verna put the phone back to her ear on the way out.
"Not you, Delores. You'd be last."

In room nine the furnace ran behind, as billed,
and she lay under cedar-smelling wool
while the heater's coil got its color up.

Kat had sent three listings since supper.
A brick two-bedroom with gas heat
and a roof two years old.
A bungalow four blocks off the square,
walk to everything, seller motivated.
The third was only a link and one line — *this one won't last.*

She read all three, twice.
Looking was free. Answering was an address.
She set the phone to charge on the dresser
and got under the wool,
and the last light in the room
was the heater's little coil,
one orange line in the dark.

***

The annex had been the county's infirmary
back when the county caught its fevers together,
and it heated like it remembered.
The radiators knocked twice at six and then steamed,
room by room down the hall,
and the windows ran wet at their corners,
and by seven the old brick held a warmth
the glass buildings across campus couldn't make.

The parka was on her chair when she unlocked her office.
Team navy, the staff patch on the chest,
and over the patch, stitched in block letters, C. COLE.
The tag inside the collar said XXL.

She put it on.
The cuffs went past her hands.
The shoulder seams landed halfway down her arms.
The hem went past her knees.
There was a doctor in it somewhere.

She wore it across to the Fieldhouse anyway,
and none of the wind reached her, the whole way,
for the first time since the freeze came down.
It was, at minimum, an excellent coat.

The equipment cage had six helmets on the counter
and the equipment manager going valve to valve
down the row with a pressure gauge.
"Cold pulls the air out of a bladder overnight,"
he said, without looking up.
"Kid puts his head in a loose helmet Saturday,
that's my name on it. One minute, Doc."
He finished the row, wrote five numbers on a card,
went back and rechecked the one he didn't like,
and only then looked at the coat.

"This was on my chair," she said.

"Mm. Order got lost somewhere back in the fall.
Something turned up on this week's restock,
tag had your name on it,
so it went to your chair."

"It's a double extra large."

"It's what turned up."
He shrugged, the shrug of a man with sixty helmets
and one morning.
"I can put you on the spring order if you want your size."

"The spring order. For a winter coat."

"That's the order that has coats in it."

"Then this one will do," she said.

She was at the door when he said, "Doc."
He tapped two fingers on his own wrist.
"Roll the cuff under, not over. Twice around.
The liner grips itself. It'll hold all day."

"Noted," she said, in the voice she kept for good charting.

Back at her desk she logged Missy Gault's morning pages
against the August baseline.
Times, sleep, screens,
the headaches spacing out slower than anybody wanted,
but spacing.
The notebook was telling her more than any scan had,
page by pressed-hard page,
and she typed it in with the coat
over the back of her chair.

***

At noon she wore it up to the Checkerboard,
and the Liars' Table saw the coat before it saw her.

"Coat won," Sonny Dillard said. "Doc, you in there?"

"Present," she said.

"They spelled it right, anyhow," Earlene said.
"Bank took three tries on mine, and I was born here."

"Delores hems, you know," said the woman at the urn.
"She'd have that down to your size by Sunday."

"It's warm," Aisha said. "I'm keeping every inch."

"That's a two-occupancy coat by the fire code," Sonny said.
"I'd have to inspect it."
The Table let it lie.
Sonny went back to his eggs and inspected those.

"Coffee's poured if you can get a hand out to take it,"
the woman at the urn said.
She got a hand out.

She ate at the counter with the sleeves pushed back,
and the Table went on to the bracket without her.
Being teased by the Table was, near as she could tell,
how this county told you it had noticed you were there.
She stayed for a second cup she did not need
and let them have the whole show.

***

The campus mail was in her box when she got back,
one page, on letterhead,
cc'd to nobody a person could argue with.

> FROM: Office of the Athletic Director
> RE: Facilities condition survey — revised winter schedule
>
> Please be advised that the athletics annex has been added
> to the winter facilities survey cycle in advance of the
> proposed performance center site evaluation. Departments
> housed in the annex should plan for all records,
> equipment, and program materials to be packed for
> relocation no later than February 1.
> Interim space assignments to follow.
> Facilities will provide boxes.

She read it twice,
the second time for the phrase that mattered.
Site evaluation.
Boyd's survey, moved up a season,
this building on its list,
and February 1 the day the annex was supposed to hold
nothing but its own heat.

She walked it with the memo in her hand.
Two rehab rooms, whirlpool tubs older than her license,
taping tables re-taped at their corners.
The ramp worn to a shine by sixty years of wheels.
The ward ceiling twelve feet up,
built high in a year when the treatment
for half of everything was air.
The records room, steel cabinets in rows,
thirty years of the county's knees and shoulders
and baselines in the drawers,
and on the bottom row, older paper,
folders gone soft as cloth,
from whoever was carried in here
before anybody called this building overflow.

Facilities would provide boxes.
She stood in the records room a while with that sentence.

One thing she would have denied to any face in the county:
she liked the coat better than anything she owned that fit,
and she liked this warm old building
better than any glass box in anybody's rendering,
and neither preference was reasonable,
and she was keeping both as long as she was allowed.

At four she pulled the coat back on
for the walk down to the field.
At the annex door she did what the cage had told her.
Right cuff turned under, twice around, then the left,
until her hands were out where the work was.
The wind was still north. The coat didn't care.
She picked up her kit and went out into the hard bright cold
with the memo folded into one pocket,
her gloves in the other,
her name on her chest and the sleeves rolled twice.
