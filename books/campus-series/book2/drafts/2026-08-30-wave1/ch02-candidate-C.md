# Chapter 2 — Everything to Prove

The cold came down in the night
like a decision the sky had been putting off since August,
and by five, when Dan let himself into the Fieldhouse,
the flag on the practice pole had gone from hanging to pointing.

He was first in by better than an hour. He usually was.
The building at that hour ran one lane of light
down the middle of the indoor turf,
and the heaters up in the trusses ticked on, quit,
and ticked on again, arguing with the front
in the only language they had.

He re-taped the walkthrough hashes first,
forty yards of painter's tape laid off a chalk line,
down on his knees in the half dark to press every edge flat.
A walkthrough was a rehearsal, a rehearsal was a promise,
and he did not make promises on crooked lines.
His line coach came in at six, found him at it,
and offered to take the tape gun.

"Floor settled funny over the summer," Dan said,
and kept the tape gun.

By seven the managers had the period cards racked,
the graduate assistant had the mezzanine camera running
with the clock burned into the frame,
and the doors at both ends of the building
were propped the width of a cinder block.
Those were Merritt's rules — every room two adults deep,
every door open, every minute of every practice on film.
Nobody on his staff asked him to justify any of it anymore.
The rules had come with him, like the whistle.

The injury report went to the league office at noon,
signed, a day before anybody would have asked for it.
The visitors' sheet hung on a clipboard by the ramp door,
and everybody signed it, scouts, preachers, the propane man.
There was a way of running a program
where nothing you did needed explaining later.
It cost him twenty extra minutes a day,
and he paid it the way other men paid insurance,
early and without discussion.

The team came through the propped door blowing on their hands,
helmets carried, jerseys over hoodies, no pads on a Friday,
and at seven thirty sharp Dan blew the whistle once
and the building went from sixty conversations to one.

A walkthrough ran at half speed and full attention,
no pads, no contact, no music,
the only voices coaches' voices,
and between periods the claps came in volleys,
players breaking huddles they didn't need
because breaking huddles sharp was a habit
and habits were what showed up at two thirty.

The griddle in the equipment bay had been going since six,
his own call, eggs and white bread for anybody in a jersey,
because it was the first hard-cold morning of the year
and a walkthrough taken on an empty stomach
was a walkthrough you paid for twice.
He told the managers the griddle was for the film crew's
morale. The managers knew better and ate too.

Ninety plays in ninety minutes, every look scripted.
The scout team wore pinnies with the rival's numbers
and gave honest looks, which he thanked them for by name.
He called the structures as they showed —
Tite, Mint, Okie, a two-high shell rolling down late —
and watched the front seven reset without a word said.
Punt safe twice. Field-goal block once.
The swinging gate they would never call but had to carry,
because the rival carried theirs.
The last period was the two-minute drill,
no clock in the building except the one in his mouth,
ball on the minus-forty, one timeout, down three,
and the whole thing ran clean with time to spare, twice.

A freshman aligned a yard wide of his landmark in period nine.
Dan walked over, moved him with two fingers on the shoulder,
and told the kid's position coach,
loud enough for the kid to bank it,
that he had the best feet in his signing class.
The freshman studied the turf and stood half an inch taller
the rest of the morning.

"Third and medium, they're a pressure team, forty percent,"
his coordinator said, walking the next card over.
"You want the scat menu or the slide?"

"Both. Scat to the field, slide to the boundary.
If that backer's feet get hot, kill to the flat and live."

"That's the Tuesday answer."

"It's the Friday answer too.
Good teams are boring by Friday."

He would not have said it in any room he ran,
but he liked Friday better than Saturday.
Saturday belonged to everybody —
the county, the board, the roofs around the square.
Friday the game was still perfect,
still ninety clean plays on white cards,
and nobody had lost anything yet.

***

At ten the staff broke to the film room for the last install.
Nothing new went in on a Friday.
Friday you took plays out,
until what was left was only what they owned.
Boyd's rendering was taped to the wall beside the screen,
where Dan had taped it himself two weeks ago:
the performance center in watercolor and steel,
a hundred yards of indoor turf under a glass spine,
cold tubs racked like a car wash,
a building with its collar turned up
against exactly the kind of morning this one was.
In the drawing there was lawn and a row of young maples
where the annex stood now,
brick and window units and a roof he'd been told about twice.
He liked the picture better every week.
A program ought to say plainly what it thinks it's worth,
and there was no plainer sentence than glass
and lit turf you could see from the interstate.

"Rivalry thread's been running since Tuesday," the graduate
assistant said, sorting cut-ups.
"Twelve thousand of them, Coach,
and every last one's a believer this week.
They've got your story pinned up top again.
They tell it nicer every year."

The version was ten years old now and traveled light:
a young assistant at a program that had everything,
a woman he had no business falling for,
a marriage and a season gone in the same spring,
seven quiet years overseas working it off,
and Ashford at the end of it,
the second chance he'd since made good twice over.
The board told it the way the county told all its stories,
fond, word for word, secondhand.

Dan squared the period cards against the tabletop once.

"Period six," he said. "Run it back."

Tick Moran came up the ramp at noon
with the recorder already running,
which was Tick's version of knocking.
He signed the visitors' sheet without being asked.
Everybody signed Merritt's sheet.

"Two minutes for WDSS, Coach. County's hungry."

"County's fed. Go ahead."

"How are we feeling about tomorrow?"

"I feel good about our preparation.
The players earned this week. The staff built the plan.
I mostly carry the cards."

"Now — the county hears you've got a picture
of Mr. Boyd's building hanging in your film room.
That true, or is that board talk?"

"It's true, and you can play this part on the air. I'm for it.
Every kid we recruit tours a building like that
somewhere else first.
The portal doesn't take Decembers off,
and neither do facilities.
I owe the kids in that locker room
the best version of this place I can get built."

"That'll rattle some china."

"It's a drawing, Tick.
Nobody's china was ever broken by a drawing."

The line coach leaned in over Tick's shoulder.
"Put it on the record we're commissioning a statue
of my two-deep if we win it."

Nobody bit. Tick checked his levels.

At the ramp door Tick turned the recorder on himself
and taped his sign-off with the wind for a backing track.
"Tick Moran, WDSS, at the Fieldhouse.
Win the one tomorrow, friends,
and for the first time since they drew this county on a map,
December around here means something
besides short days and long sermons."

Then, with the recorder off, at the door,
"My sister's boy started two years ago
scared of his own cleats.
His mama says whatever you did, thank you."

"His mama did that," Dan said. "We just gave him reps."

Tick went down the ramp holding his hat on.
Dan stood in the doorway a minute,
cold turf and propane,
the county out there getting louder by the hour,
and went back inside to the cards.

***

The Friday sideline check sat on his practice plan
in the only handwriting on it that wasn't his.
It said one o'clock. It never meant one o'clock.
It meant whenever the training room turned her loose,
which on rivalry week could be any hour with a one in it.
He built his practice plan in five-minute blocks,
laminated, initialed,
and had learned by July to write her line in pencil.

She came through at one forty
with the annex's cold still on her coat,
crossed the back of period fourteen
on a line that bent for nobody's script,
and stopped at his elbow.

"Friday list," she said. "Nineteen's cleared for contact.
Fifty-six warms up in the red jersey;
my call at one tomorrow, not before."

"He'll warm up clean."

"Then at one tomorrow I'll say so."

"You'll say so from where?
We're tight on the visitors' side."

"From where I always say it, Merritt."

She was gone before the next horn,
and the far door let in thirty yards of November behind her
and shut.

He let period fifteen run eight seconds long,
which nobody in the building but him would have counted,
and then blew it dead.
Six months of Fridays,
and she was still the one item on his schedule
that scheduled him.
He went back to the cards.

***

He walked the building out at six
the way he walked it out every Friday of the season,
end zone to end zone, lights dying in banks behind him,
the film room dark, the training-room door locked
and then checked locked,
the compliance binder square on its shelf
where any auditor in America could find it without asking.
The last car out of the lot was his. It usually was.

The square on the way home was already tomorrow.
Sawhorses stacked outside the hardware store,
flags zip-tied to the light poles,
the Checkerboard's window soaped with the score
everybody wanted,
and out at the fairgrounds the first motorhomes
had taken the good corners,
generator exhaust standing straight up in the cold
like the smoke of a small, patient army.

At home he ate supper standing over the sink,
found the whistle still on his neck at nine,
and left it where it was.
Out the kitchen window the yard went stiff by degrees,
and by ten the truck glass had furred over white,
first frost, the night before the one Saturday
the whole county had circled.

He got into bed at eleven and opened the parents' board.
The rivalry thread had run to nine hundred posts.
He started at the pin and read down.
They were for him tonight, all twelve thousand.
Somebody had counted the wins out loud and liked the total.
The old story sat above it all where it always sat.
After that came gate times, parking,
a mother asking whether the stadium allowed blankets,
and three kind answers inside the hour.
He read every post to the bottom.

He plugged the phone in face-down
and set the alarm for four forty-five.
Kickoff was at two thirty.
