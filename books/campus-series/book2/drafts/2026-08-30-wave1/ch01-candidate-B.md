# Chapter 1 — Bonfire Night

POV: Aisha, third person limited, past tense.
(FIRST DRAFT — Book 1.2 wave 1, candidate B, card D7. For the
author to revise; not accepted prose.)

---

"Thirty more seconds," Aisha said. "You're doing fine.
Don't look at the thumb. Look at me."

The Boy Scout kept his hand under the spout of the water
cooler she'd rigged at the end of the first-aid table,
and looked at her, and did not cry,
which with a blister already rising was a kind of valor.
The marshmallow had come off the stick wrong side first
and landed in the web of his thumb,
and marshmallow holds its heat like tar,
which was why she'd scraped it clear with a tongue depressor
before anything else and got him under cold water second.

"My mother would have said butter," the den mother said.

"Butter's for biscuits. It seals the heat in.
Water pulls it out." Aisha turned the small hand over
under the light of her headlamp and liked what she saw.
Superficial, palm-side, smaller than a dime.
"He did this exactly right. Came straight over,
didn't pop anything, held still."

She dried the hand, laid a non-stick pad over the blister,
wrapped it in two turns of gauze,
and folded a tab into the tape so it would come off
without a second injury.
Then she took the den mother's number and sent her a link.

"Picture of it in the morning when you change the pad.
I'll look at it before school and tell you
if I need to see him.
Blister stays closed, everybody goes to the game."

"You are an answer to a prayer, is what you are."

"He did the hard part," Aisha said.

The Scout gave her a three-finger salute, solemn as church,
and went back toward the fire at a dead run,
bandaged thumb held high like a lantern.

She had packed the table herself that afternoon,
and packed it for the night she expected,
not the one anybody advertised.
Burn dressings on top, saline, elastic wrap, a foil blanket,
the AED borrowed from the fieldhouse
with its battery checked twice,
and a thermos of hot water for the simple reason
that cold hands can't tape anything.
Nobody had asked her to set up at the rope line.
The rope line was where the burns would be.

The fire department had built the fire itself,
pallets and scrap stacked two men high
inside a ring of sawhorses and rope,
the engine at the curb with a hose charged and waiting,
firefighters standing around their own blaze
with the proprietary air of men grilling.
Her table sat at the rope line, red bag open, blankets,
the water cooler, a folding chair she hadn't sat in yet.
Across the square the hardware store had grown lawn chairs
along its roofline, blanketed adults passing a thermos,
and somewhere down Millrow the band was getting louder,
the drumline arguing with itself around every corner.
The cold came up through the bricks.
Breath stood in the air over the whole square
like the town was running one engine.

Her whole job tonight, if the county behaved,
was small burns, rolled ankles,
and one grown man who would eventually stand
too close to the fire in a nylon jacket.
She had worked sidelines with a helicopter on standby.
She could work this.

Sonny Dillard brought Odell Fountain to the table
a little after seven.
"Doc Cole. New team doctor. Fixes the boys."
An hour later Odell brought Sonny over.
"Sonny. You met the new doc?"

"Pleasure," Aisha said, both times,
and shook the same two hands twice.

In between, the roof crew called down "Hey, Doc"
from the hardware store like they'd known her for years,
and a woman she was fairly sure ran the flower truck
raised a cup to her from across the rope.
Six months in this county
and she was somehow both a stranger and furniture.

Nobody in Ashford remembered meeting her yet.
She let it ride, both times, and did not correct anybody,
because being brand-new twice in one evening
was the most company she'd had since June,
and she would have denied that under oath.

***

The cocoa was in her hand before she'd finished waving
at the roof crew, Earlene Tatum already looking elsewhere,
the question of whether Aisha wanted cocoa
having never been on the table.

The Checkerboard had a stand two tables down,
pies under cake domes, a cigar box for a register,
a hand-lettered sign taped to the front.
PIE BY THE SLICE. And under that, larger:
THE COACH'S SLICE.

"What's the coach's slice?" Aisha said.

"Same pie," Earlene said. "Cut a third again as wide.
Went up on the menu his first November
and it has not come off. You order it by name."

"And people do."

"People stand in line to."

Down at the far end of the square — the whole length
of the county between them, near enough — the coach stood
on a flatbed dressed with hay bales, being adored.
She could not hear most of it. She could see the mechanics.
A question came up out of the crowd; he leaned to take it;
his hands came out of his jacket pockets
and drew the answer in the air;
the crowd got its laugh or its nod,
and he was already looking for the next raised hand.
Once the wind carried her a whole sentence,
"depends what they show us — count the safeties,
you'll know before I do,"
and kept the rest for the band.
Somebody hollered a name off the transfer portal at him,
some rumor with a hometown attached,
and he put both palms up and gave back an answer
she lost under the drums,
and the crowd laughed and let him keep it.
He never checked a note. He never looked for a way down.

Read, answer, next.
It was chart work, was what it was.
She ran her table on the same three verbs,
and she knew what it cost to make that look easy,
because she knew what it cost her.

She watched for a song and a half.
Then a fireman walked a woman over from the rope line
with an ember hole burned through her coat sleeve,
and Aisha checked the forearm under it,
found nothing but a proud story,
and taped the sleeve shut anyway, because the woman asked.

"Know why they call it rivalry week?" Sonny said,
to the group at large.
"Because you can't have it but the once a year."

Odell looked at the fire.
The band turned up Millrow.

"One Saturday plays for the whole year in this county,"
Earlene said, in the tone she kept for frost warnings.
"Win it."

Then she drifted off toward the pie stand,
ministry concluded, and left Aisha holding the cocoa
she had never asked for and had nearly finished.

***

She was eleven replies deep in the rivalry thread
before the cocoa went fully cold.
She had joined the parents' board her second week
on the job, and not for company.
Injuries get reported there before they get reported to her;
a mother posting about a limp at ten p.m.
beats a Monday phone call every time.
Tonight it was all hype, and she read it
with her back to the fire's heat.

**TonyaBrock (2 at ASU):** ROOF CREW — hardware store, 6:00,
bring your own chair. Dwayne is not furnishing chairs again.
Saturday we send them home quiet.

**CarlaJean Pruett:** For the new families, the bonfire square
is the same square where Marisol and Cal had their first
kiss. High school sweethearts. It was raining and neither
one of them moved an inch.

**RaeLynn T.:** My cousin was there!! Says the band kept
right on playing.

**Hollis Webb:** Different subject but has anybody else
looked at Coach Merritt's Wikipedia lately? Seven seasons
just gone. Page skips like a scratched record.
Theories below, winner gets nothing.

**Dale Gentry (3 boys):** Crab boats. I have said it before
and I will keep saying it.

**TonyaBrock (2 at ASU):** He can keep his seven years,
long as he gives us Saturday night.

The kiss story came out buffed smooth with handling,
every detail worn friendly,
and nobody in forty replies corrected a word of it.
She had been introduced to Marisol once. Possibly twice.
The story didn't match anything she knew,
and the county plainly loved it too much to check,
and she found she liked the town better for that, not worse.

Seven missing seasons.
A gap like that in a chart would make her ask questions.
It wasn't her chart.
She put the phone in her coat pocket
and kept her hand in there with it, for the warmth.

The band broke onto the square at full volume
and the fire department fed the fire up to meet it,
sparks climbing, the roofline of the hardware store
on its feet, lawn chairs abandoned,
and for a while there was nothing to treat
and nothing to chart and nowhere else in the county
anybody was.

***

The coat went onto the shower rod in room nine
to air the smoke out, and the smoke went everywhere anyway,
woodsmoke settling into the carpet and the bedspread
like it had paid for the room.

The grocery sack she'd found hanging on the doorknob
sat on the bed. Inside it, a wool blanket, heavy,
smelling of cedar, brushed clean,
and a note in pencil on the back of a receipt.

*Furnace in 9 runs behind when the wind comes north.
Use the blanket. Don't fight the dial. — V.P.*

Verna Poteat had yet to say a warm word to her.
The blanket was the warmest thing in the room.

The suitcase stood open on the luggage rack
where it had stood since the second week of June.
Two moving boxes under the window still wore their tape.
Her diplomas were in one of them. She knew which one.
Her toothbrush lived in a motel glass
she rinsed out every morning,
and tomorrow's clinic bag sat zipped by the door,
which was the one packed thing in the room.

She sent the den mother the follow-up link
before she could forget,
and got back three exclamation points
and a photograph of the Scout asleep in full uniform,
bandaged thumb held to his chest like a medal.

She spread the wool blanket over the bedspread
and turned off the lamp.
Through the gap in the curtain the sky over the square
still held a low orange,
the fire department walking its fire down to coals
on a schedule of its own.
Her coat would smell like woodsmoke till Sunday.
