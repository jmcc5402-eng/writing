# Chapter 4 — The Quiet Tent

POV: Dan Merritt, third person limited, past tense.
(Book 1.2 wave 1 blind draft — candidate C, card D5: weather as
texture, briefed lyric-dense. FIRST DRAFT for panel judging.)

---

The hospital kept every light it owned turned on,
a building that did not believe in night.
Dan crossed the visitor lot at half past ten
with his breath standing up white ahead of him
and the year's first hard frost gone to glitter
in the seams of the asphalt.
The square's smoke was still in his collar.
Across the river the town was singing —
he could feel it more than hear it,
a bass note riding under the cold,
the winningest night of his life going on
somewhere he wasn't.

He rode the elevator up with a nurse
who recognized him
and was kind enough to pretend she didn't.
Fourth floor. Family lounge.
Down the hall a door stood shut
with a chart in a plastic sleeve beside it,
and every chair in the lounge faced that door
whether the person sitting in it meant to or not.

Denny Gault came up off a couch like a man
who had been waiting all night
for something to do with his hands.
"Coach."
The handshake went long.
There was gratitude in it and grievance in it
and no line between them yet.

Missy Gault stayed seated,
her boy's duffel at her feet, coat still on,
the game program rolled tight in one fist.
She watched Dan come into the room
the way a line judge watches a foot
come down close to chalk.

Dan killed the TV first.
The replay was running with the sound off,
and it was going to reach the fourth quarter
whether anybody in the room was ready or not.
Then he pulled a chair around so the three of them
made a huddle instead of a waiting room,
because a room like this needs an agenda
more than it needs a speech.

"Here's what I know," he said.
"Here's what I don't. And here's Monday."

He gave it to them in that order, slow,
and Denny let him get all the way through it,
which cost Denny something.

"You were twenty yards away," Denny said.
"Tell me what you saw."

"I saw the hit. I saw him down.
I saw the fastest tent I've ever stood next to,
and I've stood next to a few."

"The bracket's in three weeks."

"It is."

"So give me a date. Best case.
I won't hold you to it.
I just need something to set my watch by."

Dan had built a career out of finding
the one sentence a room could carry out the door.
He went looking for it and it wasn't there.
"I don't have one," he said.
"And I'm not going to invent one in a hospital."

Denny sat back down beside his wife.
"I'm not mad at you," he said, loud,
because loud was the only speed he came in.
"I'm not mad at anybody. That's the problem.
I want somebody to be mad at,
and there's nobody in the whole building,
and that's worse."

Dan believed him.
That was the trouble with Denny Gault —
you could hear the love in the volume.

"Here's your job," Dan said.
"You don't answer one phone call tomorrow.
Not the radio, not the paper, not the group.
The noise is mine. Yours is down that hall."

Missy said nothing.
The window behind her gave back the parking lot,
frost feathering up the corner of the glass,
and whatever she was doing behind her face,
she was doing it alone.

Aisha came in a little after eleven,
still in sideline gear, badge clipped crooked,
a folder riding her forearm like a call sheet.
She had been somewhere behind that shut door
since before the stadium finished emptying.

Denny was up again. "Doc. Straight. Please."

She didn't answer him standing.
She pulled the fourth chair around to face the couch,
sat, set the folder on her knees,
and did not open it.
She let the question sit for five full seconds,
which in a hospital lounge is a month.

"Straight is the only speed I've got," she said.
"Here's tonight. His scans are clean.
That is genuinely good news,
and a concussion does not show on a scan,
and he has one.
He's sleeping.
Sleep is the best thing in this building.
Every hour of it, he's doing the work."

"When does he play?" Denny said.

"Nobody knows yet. That's the answer."

"That's not—" Denny caught himself.
"Doc. Respectfully. That can't be the answer."

"It's the true one," she said,
"and I'll give it to you again tomorrow,
and the day after,
and it will be true every time
until the day it isn't.
When it changes, you'll hear it from me
before you hear it from anyone,
and it will come with a date on it,
because by then the date will be his and not ours.
I could hand you a number tonight.
It would be a good number. You'd sleep on it.
It would be for you, not for him."

Dan stood by the dark TV
with his hands doing nothing,
which his hands were bad at.
He knew the play here.
He had run it a hundred times himself —
you give the room a number,
you coach the room toward the number,
and the room loves you for it.
The cheap points were sitting right there,
tonight of all nights,
with the county singing across the river
and this family leaning toward her from the couch.
She left them on the table.

Missy spoke for the first time.
"Does his head hurt?"

"Yes," Aisha said. "Less than it did at nine.
We're staying ahead of it.
He asked for the score
before he went down for the night."

Missy nodded once
and looked back down the hall.
She did not say thank you.
Aisha did not appear to need it.

A nurse leaned in and said the Gaults
could sit with him a while.
Denny shook Dan's hand a second time going out,
gratitude and grievance, still no line.
Missy picked up the duffel herself
and did not hand it to anybody.

The lounge went quiet behind them,
the same quiet the sideline had held
while the tent was up.
A quiet with a job to do.

The vending machine at the end of the hall
sold coffee the color of river water.
Dan bought two without asking
and held one out, and she took it,
and for a while they stood drinking it
because it was hot and it was there.

"Monday," he said.
"What do you need from my side."

"A locker room that doesn't text him
asking when he's back.
He'll answer every one of them
even if it costs him. They always answer."

"Done. What else."

"When you talk tomorrow — no dates.
Not 'soon.' Not 'week-to-week.'
Nothing with a calendar hiding inside it.
This county can hear a date
through a cinderblock wall."

He took the card out of his jacket,
the one he scripted openers on,
and wrote NO CALENDAR WORDS across the back,
and she watched him do it
over the rim of her cup
and didn't say anything about it.

"In there," he said.
"That was the best no I ever heard called."

"It wasn't a no. It was the answer.
There's a difference,
and I need you of all people
to have it by Monday."

"I've got it tonight."

She looked at him
for longer than the hallway strictly required.
Six months of arguments between them,
and neither one reached for one.

"Go get some sleep," she said.
"One of us should."
She went back down the hall
toward the door with the chart beside it,
cup in hand, reading as she walked.

Some coach's corner of him had sat in that lounge
listening for a number anyway.
A number was a thing
you could build three weeks around;
he'd have had it installed by Sunday film.
That was his to carry out of the building,
and it wasn't going any further.

***

The frost had thickened on the windshield
while he was inside.
He scraped it with the edge of the play card,
NO CALENDAR WORDS going soft at one corner,
and sat and let the heater run.

Across the river the square had gone
from singing to horns.
Winningest night of his life.
It had cost a kid a dark room on the fourth floor
and a family anything to set a watch by,
and no scoreboard in the county
was going to show the goddamn price.

He didn't want his own kitchen
and he couldn't stomach the square,
so he took the third way,
out the river road to the Blind Mule.

***

The Mule at midnight was down to its regulars,
neon in the window, the TV muted,
the replay running anyway.
Peanut had a rag moving on the rail,
and a club soda arrived in front of Dan
before he got his coat off his shoulders.

"First hour they drank to the win," Peanut said.
"Second hour they drank to the kid.
Third hour they commenced asking me
medical questions,
like thirty years behind this rail
comes with a board certification."

"What are they asking?"

"When. Only question in the building. When."

Sonny Dillard was down the bar with a bottle
he'd been nursing since the third toast.
"Kid's tough as a two-dollar steak," Sonny said.
"He'll be fine."
It lay there. Nobody picked it up.
Sonny turned the bottle a half turn
and read the label awhile.

Dan's phone lit against the rail.
It had been lighting up all night,
and his thumb had the app open
out of pure nightly habit, quick as a tell.
The thread had gone up at 11:40.

> **Prayers for the Gault family (and one question)**
>
> **Shelly Ramey** Home from the square and I can't quit
> thinking about that family. Prayers all night at our house.
>
> **KellyAnn Prewitt** Meal train is up as of ten minutes ago,
> link below. Do not argue with me, just pick a day.
>
> **Todd Sizemore** Heck of a win. Hate every way we got it.
>
> **FirstDownMom** Genuine question, asked with love. Is there
> a standard evaluation timeline the medical staff follows?
> We're three weeks out, and families are just asking for
> clarity so the boys know what they're preparing for. All of
> us are only worried about the kids.
>
> **Denny Gault** From the family. He is awake, he is himself,
> he asked who won. That's my boy. To the timeline question,
> we have been asking it since nine o'clock tonight to
> everybody in a badge, and nobody in that building would give
> us so much as a day of the week. Not blaming a soul. We just
> believe in being straight with people.

He read it twice.
Polite as a church bulletin,
and it sat on him the way the frost had,
thin, even, total,
nothing in it you could point at.
Denny's post had the lounge still in it —
the volume, the love,
and under the two of them,
a man out looking for somewhere to put the mad.

He set the phone face down on the rail.
"What's this county going to do
with 'nobody knows'?"

Peanut pulled the rag through a glass
and took the question seriously,
which was the service the Mule actually sold.
"This county will forgive you a loss
by Sunday dinner.
Being told to wait,
they'll nurse till Easter."

Dan finished the club soda.
Peanut set down another one, unasked,
slid Dan's twenty back across the rail
with two fingers,
and went on down the bar.

The phone lit once more against the wood.
He turned it over long enough
to see the thread's name
and the number beside it climbing,
and that was his last look at the board
for the night.
He left it unread
and put the phone in his pocket.
