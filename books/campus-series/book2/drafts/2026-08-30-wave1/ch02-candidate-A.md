# Chapter 2 — Everything to Prove

POV: Dan Merritt, third person limited, past tense.

Raw material for the author's own pass;

---

On rivalry Friday the Fieldhouse belonged to Dan Merritt
from six to seven, and he spent the hour the way he'd built it,
alone, the lights coming on one bank at a time,
the building still holding Thursday night's cold.

The whistle went on with his shirt.
There was nobody in the building to blow it at.
It went on anyway.

He walked the halls with a roll of tape in one hand,
squaring what the week had knocked loose —
the depth chart by the meeting rooms,
the sign over the tunnel door
that a season of jumping men had slapped off level,
the hydration chart on the cooler that nobody read.
He was not a superstitious man.
He was a man who liked a straight sign,
and on a Friday the difference didn't matter.

In the staff room he signed the week's hour log
before anyone was in to watch him do it.
The league allowed twenty hours of a player's week.
Ashford had used seventeen, and he could prove every one,
and the proof was already in its folder,
because the folder was most of the idea.

Ty Beaumont found him in the film room at seven
with two coffees, one of them already half gone, both his.

Boyd's people had come through the building Thursday,
and the rendering was up on the film-room wall,
foam-backed, laminated, hung level as a church window:
the performance center, four stories of glass and steel
standing where the annex stood now,
sunrise coming off it in colors
Ashford had not been issued yet.

"Looks like a boat show married a parking garage,"
Beaumont said.

Nobody laughed. The projector hummed on.

Dan looked at the rendering a while longer.

"I like it," he said. "I'll say that in daylight.
Put a kid from two states away in that lobby in January
and you can skip the rest of the visit.
And it sits on the annex block,
so the training room triples and gets windows.
Hard to be against a building that's good to everybody."

"You want to be the one to tell Doc Cole
it's good to everybody, or do we sell tickets to that."

"She reads square footage the same as anybody," Dan said.

He'd have signed anywhere that showed him that lobby
at eighteen. That wasn't cynicism. That was recruiting,
and recruiting was mostly knowing
what a kid can't help wanting.

They took the call sheet down a column at a time.

"They'll show two-high on the opener and play man behind it,"
Beaumont said. "They did it to us last year.
They did it to everybody all November."

"Then the fade's on, and seven knows it.
The cut-ups went to the tablets last night, sorted by down.
If a receiver of yours is surprised tomorrow,
he was surprised on purpose."

Beaumont peeled the lid off the second coffee.
"Board dug out the history again last night,
if you didn't see it. Rivalry week. You're the sermon."

"Spell the name right and I'm easy."

He hadn't seen it yet. He would.
He knew the shape of it without looking,
because the board told his story every big week,
and it always told the county's version.
In the county's version, the young assistant
at the big program broke the one rule everybody knows,
stood up in his head coach's office and said so
before anyone had thought to ask,
and served his year in the wilderness with his chin level.
A season lost. A marriage lost. A man improved.
The county liked that version fine.
It scanned. It cost nothing to believe.

Dan uncapped a marker and wrote tomorrow's first twelve plays
on the whiteboard from memory, in order,
and the morning went on the way he preferred mornings to go,
forward.

***

Walkthrough went at ten, moved up an hour
so the band could have the stadium by noon.
He'd posted the change to the team app
at nine the night before,
and slept the sleep of a man whose app was up to date.

Helmets, no pads, sneakers on turf,
a game played at the speed of church.
The openers, all twelve, in order.
Motions walked at half speed, then run at game speed,
the freshmen mouthing their rules as they went.
The third-and-medium menu.
Red zone from the eleven, both hashes.
The managers reset the ball between plays like croupiers.
The two-minute, twice.
The second time through he took the voices away —
wristbands and hand signals only,
ninety thousand people implied —
and the offense ran it silent, and clean,
which was the point of running it at all.

#7 took the first snap of every period,
because tomorrow he would.

A rep that walked wrong got walked again.
Nobody yelled.
Yelling was for programs that saved their thinking
for Saturday afternoon.

They rehearsed sudden change,
turnover to kick team inside twelve seconds,
because chaos keeps a schedule too if you write one.
They rehearsed the two-deep at every spot,
each backup taking his rep while the starter watched
and the starter's rep while the backup watched.

The equipment manager flagged him at the water station.
"Tackle landed at nine last night.
Made his grandmother's service and made curfew, both.
His mama said to tell you what you did."

"Compliance did it. They found the rule; I dialed a phone.
Tell his mama the tackle looked fresh in period two."

Between periods the tackle came and stood near him,
wordless in the way of big men everywhere,
and Dan straightened the kid's collar with two fingers
and called up the punt team,
which handled it for both of them.

At eleven the far doors opened.
The sideline carts came through with the training staff,
and Dr. Aisha Cole came in behind them
with a tablet and a headset,
on time from a direction he didn't control.

"Walkthrough was posted for eleven," she said,
in place of good morning.

"Moved to ten. It went out on the app last night."

"The shared calendar says eleven.
I don't schedule medicine off a football app."

"Field's clear in forty minutes."

"The check is at eleven. It's eleven."

He moved the kicking game to the far forty
and gave her the near sideline,
and the walkthrough bent around the check
like water around a bridge piling.

The crash bag staged at the thirty.
The cart route walked gate to tunnel,
the gate key shown to two people
and taped inside the gate box besides.
The spotter upstairs answered the headset on the first click.
She timed the route to the hospital door on her own watch,
escort included, and wrote the number down
without telling anybody what it was.

"That Gator's parked in my cart lane," she said.

It was Beaumont's Gator.
Dan backed it off the lane himself, forty feet up the ramp,
talking the whole way about band trailers
and where a Gator ought to live.

"You'd have lane to spare if that crash bag
didn't stage like a yard sale," he said, hopping down.

"The bag stages where I can reach it blind.
That's what staging is."

By the time he'd parked it she had moved on
to the sideline boxes,
which was the correct amount of thank-you
for a lane that should have been empty to start with.

She ended the check where she always ended it, on him.

"Tomorrow, if I walk onto that field, the game stops.
My timeout doesn't run on your play clock.
It runs until I'm done,
and the first person who waves me off a player
will be famous by Sunday.
Brief your sideline so nobody's surprised on television."

"They were briefed in August."

"August was August. Refresh them."

He blew the whistle for the first time all day,
called them up at midfield,
and gave them her sentence word for word,
*if the doctor walks on, the game stops until she's done,*
with nothing added,
which from him was a co-signature.

She was gone before the huddle broke,
already up the stairs talking to the spotter.
The check she had scripted for ten minutes had taken sixteen.
He ran the kicking game tight, got all of it in anyway,
and said not one word about the six.

She had been right about the calendar,
and they had both known it by her second sentence.
He had finished the argument anyway,
the way you carry out the fake after the handoff.
Somebody is always watching,
and the play is not over just because the ball is gone.

Then he ran the openers again. All twelve, from the top,
against air, in a building the band wanted.
Beaumont checked his watch and put the watch away.
A walkthrough is over when it's clean twice,
and every man in the program could recite that,
and no man in the program had ever loved it.
The twelfth play of the second run
looked exactly like the twelfth play of the first,
which was the only thing he had asked of the day.

Walkthrough closed at ten minutes to noon.
He sent them out light.
"Feet up tonight. Phones down by ten.
Everything we need is already in the building."

He stood in the empty Fieldhouse with the script
rolled in his fist.
He would never have said this out loud in a county
where Saturday was the point of the week
and the week was the point of the year,
but Friday was the day he would have kept.
On Friday the sheet was still perfect.
Nobody was hurt yet. Nothing was lost yet.
Every call he had made was still right.

***

The team took its hotel floor at seven, rivalry-eve rule,
players two to a room, coaches at the ends of the halls,
the itinerary taped to every door
down to the eight o'clock optional chapel.
Dan drove over with WDSS on,
because from August to Thanksgiving the truck radio
had one station and no argument about it.

The square had rivalry eve all over it.
Trucks sat three deep at the Checkerboard.
The flatbed from Thursday was still parked by the courthouse,
its bunting gone soft in the damp,
and when the wind came around
there was still smoke off the bonfire scar.
Up Millrow the House stood under its tarps
with one work light burning,
somebody's crew getting their Friday hours in.

Tick Moran ran the evening show long on rivalry eve,
the one night a year WDSS called itself coverage.
He read the forecast. He read the parking.
He read the list of businesses closing early tomorrow
in the tone of a man reading a church bulletin,
and the hardware store's ad ran twice back to back
without apology.
Then, coming out of the last commercial,
in the same flat voice he read lost dogs in,
he said, "Win tomorrow, and for the first time
in the history of this county, December will mean something."

Dan turned the radio off in the hotel lot
and let that ride up in the elevator with him.

That was the arithmetic in a county like this,
and he had never minded it.
You were what Saturday said you were, a week at a time.
He had been on both ends of that math.

He walked his hallway at ten.
The doors were quiet.
Whoever still had a television on
had it low enough to deserve it.

His room had two beds and he would use half of one.
Tomorrow's polo went on the closet hook.
The whistle came off last and lay on the nightstand.

Then, the last thing, lamp still on,
he opened the parents' board
and read the county's verdict on himself.

It was a good night on the board.
The flatbed pictures from Thursday were still collecting
hearts.
The board had tomorrow won by three scores.
A mother was worried the student section
would stand too long in the cold,
and wanted the gates opened early for the kids,
and by ten o'clock eleven other mothers
were worried right along with her.
The morning's history thread had gone down the page
under all of it, told and liked and done.

He read to the bottom. He read to the bottom every night.

He set the phone face down on the nightstand
next to the whistle, and turned off the lamp.

The alarm was set for four forty-five.
Kickoff was at two.
