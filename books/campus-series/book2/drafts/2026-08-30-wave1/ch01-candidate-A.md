# Chapter 1 — Bonfire Night

POV: Aisha, third person limited, past tense.
FIRST DRAFT — Book 1.2 wave-1 blind candidate, for author revision.

---

On the Thursday night before the rivalry game,
Ashford set itself on fire, on purpose, with a permit.

The fire department had been stacking pallets on the square
since Tuesday, a tower two stories tall with its corners squared,
built by men who knew they would be judged on it.
At six the band came down Millrow at walking tempo,
past the House under its tarps,
and the crowd closed in behind the horns
like water closing behind a boat.
At a quarter to seven the chief walked the rope line one last time,
nodded to a man holding a torch, and stepped back.

The stack took the flame like it had been rehearsing.

Light went up over the whole square.
On the hardware-store roof a row of lawn chairs
had appeared sometime during the afternoon,
and the adults up there raised their cups to the crowd below,
and the crowd below raised theirs back.
Somebody's dog went home on principle.
It was twenty-nine degrees out and nobody anywhere was cold.

***

Dr. Aisha Cole had the first-aid table at the south end
of the burn line, upwind, between the rope
and the Checkerboard's pie stand.
She had not volunteered.
Verna Poteat had volunteered her on Monday,
sliding the fire department's sign-up sheet
across the motel office counter with the pen already uncapped.

She had packed the kit her own way regardless.
Burn gel, saline, cling film, non-adherent dressings,
gloves in three sizes, and a pulse oximeter
she did not expect to need
and would have been embarrassed to want and not have.
She taped down the table's corners herself,
set the sharps box where no child could reach it,
and by six thirty she was open for business
in a folding chair with her breath showing.

Odell Fountain brought people over to meet her
the way some men show off a truck.
"New team doctor," he announced, twice, both times
to Earlene Tatum. "The one that went toe to toe
with Coach at Delores's supper."

"Head team physician," Aisha said, both times.

"We've met," Earlene said, both times, and stayed anyway.

Six months in this county and she was still new enough
to be introduced twice to the same people.
She minded it less than she would have guessed.
Nobody in Ashford had known her longer than June.
So far, her name out here meant exactly what she had said it meant,
and not one thing more.

Verna ran the cocoa urn beside her, mostly so she could sit down,
and scrolled the parents' board with one glove off.
The rivalry hype thread had been going since lunch.
She tilted the phone so Aisha could see it.

> **RivalryWeekRita:** Square's going to glow tonight.
> Bring the babies, bring a chair for Grandad,
> bring something for the pie table besides your appetite.
>
> **BleacherDad88:** Seven seasons missing off Coach Merritt's
> Wikipedia and the man wins anyway. The Wikipedia years.
> I heard shrimp boat.
>
> **TailgateTina:** My sister-in-law heard witness protection.
> Either way he's ours now.
>
> **PieBeforeKickoff:** He was a monk. You can tell by the
> posture.
>
> **AshfordBornAndRaised:** All I know is this same bonfire
> is where Cal Sutter kissed our Marisol the first time,
> right out front of the hardware store, whole town watching.
> That's how Ashford starts things.
>
> **RivalryWeekRita:** And Saturday is how we finish them.
> Go get em.

"Is the kiss true?" Aisha asked.

"It's how we tell it," Verna said, and put her glove back on.

Seven seasons, just gone.
In her trade, a seven-year hole in the record
was the first thing you asked about,
and the last thing you asked about out loud at a bonfire.
She handed the phone back and did not ask.

***

The evening's first real customer arrived at a run,
towed by a mother wearing a stadium blanket as a cape.
A Scout, ten or eleven, sash on over his coat,
holding his right hand out in front of him
like a plate he had been told not to drop.

"Marshmallow," the mother said. "It slid off the stick
and he grabbed for it. I turned my back thirty seconds."

"They time it," Aisha said. "Sit him here."

The boy sat. He laid the hand on her table palm up
and held still, which almost nobody did.
Two fingertips gone shiny at the pads,
a blister rising on the middle one about the size of a dime.
Skin closed. Edges pink, not white.
She gloved up and worked the exam out loud,
because narrating it was half the treatment at this age.
"Superficial. Partial thickness right here at the worst.
Blister stays on. It's doing its job."

"He ran straight for the water table," the mother said.
"Stood there with that hand under the jug spigot
ten straight minutes, like a man holding a position.
Wouldn't let his grandmother near it with the butter dish."

"Then he already did the medicine," Aisha said.
"Cool water is the whole treatment for a burn like this —
everything I add is ribbon." She looked at the boy.
"Who taught you that. The sash?"

He rotated the sash a quarter turn and pointed once,
at a round badge, the way a man indicates a diploma.

"First aid," the mother read out, unnecessarily, bursting.

Aisha dressed the fingers, a non-adherent pad
and two neat turns of tape, nothing bulky,
and talked while she worked.
Keep it clean, keep it dry, leave the blister alone.
Children's ibuprofen per the label tonight.
Any red streaking, any fever, she wanted a call, not a wait.
She texted the aftercare sheet from her phone to the mother's
while they stood there, and photographed the dressed hand
for her own chart, with permission,
because a thing not charted was a thing that never happened.

"They said you were good," the mother said.
"We're glad you're here. Whatever Coach thinks."

"He did the medicine," Aisha said, nodding at the boy.
"I did the paperwork."

The Scout accepted a fresh marshmallow for the road
in his left hand, already retired from the incident,
and his mother thanked Aisha three more times
walking backward into the crowd.

This town set a two-story fire in the middle of itself
once a year and trained its ten-year-olds so well
it barely needed her.
She had worked sidelines where nobody held still
and nobody said thank you.
Ashford, so far, was not one of them.

***

By eight the line at her table was nobody,
which in her business was the best possible result.
Earlene Tatum turned up with two cocoas
and put one in Aisha's hand as a settled matter,
and they stood at the rope and watched the fire eat.

At the pie stand the Checkerboard girls were down to two pies
and a hand-lettered sign that said COACH'S SLICE.

"What in the world is a coach's slice," Aisha said.

"Quarter of a whole pie, cut as one piece," Earlene said.
"Checkerboard started it when the winning started.
You order it, everybody at your table knows
what you think of the man."

Sonny Dillard considered the bonfire from the rope
and delivered his finding. "Now that's a fire."
Nobody took it up.

"One Saturday a year plays for the whole year in this county,"
Earlene said, in the voice she kept for frost warnings
and road closures. "It's this one coming. Win it."

At the far end of the square, a county away by crowd,
a flatbed sat under the night's only floodlight,
and Coach Dan Merritt stood up on it being adored.

She could not hear a word of it from where she stood.
She did not need to.
He let the chant run longer than a modest man would have,
then cut it off with one raised hand,
and the square went quiet for him
the way a room goes quiet for a surgeon.
He brought his seniors up onto the truck bed
and stepped out of the light to let the crowd have them.
He pointed people out at the rope, an old man,
somebody's grandmother up on the hardware-store roof,
and whatever he said about each one landed,
because twice the whole square laughed
and once the old man had to take his hat off and wave it.

He was working that crowd the way she worked a chart.
Top to bottom, nothing skipped, nothing rushed,
every finding named in order,
and signed off before anybody knew the exam was over.
He gave them back to the band early, while they still wanted him,
and stepped down into a knot of handshakes that closed over him.

Her cocoa had gone cold in her hand.
She could not have certified when.

She went back to her table and restocked the gloves.
The gloves did not need restocking.

***

The fire came down to coals on schedule,
and the fire department stood around the glow
looking as satisfied as men are allowed to look in public.
Verna appeared while Aisha was snapping the kit shut.

"Follow me back, honey. The square empties out stupid,
and the deer act worse on bonfire nights."

They ran the two miles to the Magnolia Court in a line,
Verna's taillights swinging wide at the turn
the way they probably had for forty years.

Her room smelled like clean nothing, the way it had since June.
Two suitcases sat open, one on the luggage rack,
one on the second bed, everything she owned
still folded to travel dimensions.
The dresser drawers had never been opened.
The closet held an ironing board and six wire hangers, all empty.
Come Saturday the room would go to game people
who had booked it back in August,
and she would go wherever Verna could put her.
The suitcases made that simple.

Kat the realtor had sent her eleven listings since June.
Aisha had told her once, in July, that the market seemed high,
and after that had stopped answering.
The market was not high.
What the listings were was permanent,
and permanent was a thing you had to ask a town for out loud.
She could have this whole room in the truck in nine minutes.
She had timed it in June, and once more in October,
to be sure the number still held.

She hung her coat over the chair and got into bed,
and the room went on smelling like nothing.
She fell asleep with the bonfire still in her hair.
