# Chapter 3 — Rivalry Saturday

POV: Aisha, third person limited, past tense.
(FIRST DRAFT — wave-1 blind candidate B, card D1 sound-forward.
Raw material for the author's own pass; not final prose.)

---

Rivalry Saturday arrived by ear, the way thunder does,
felt in the bed frame before there was anything to see.
By six, strangers' trucks were crunching across the Magnolia
Court's gravel, and by seven Verna Poteat stood at the office
door taking parking cash with a church fan in her free hand,
the radio going behind her, pregame four hours early.
She had set a traffic cone behind Aisha's bumper at dawn.
"You're coned off," was all she said about it. "Go on."

Aisha rode to the stadium on the jump seat of the equipment
truck, knees against a crate of prewrap,
checking the AED a third time because the checking was free.
She heard the place long before the truck cleared the last
block of houses, a low weather of voices over the rooftops,
the drumline warming somewhere inside the concrete,
brass finding each other note by note down in the tunnel.
On the maintenance gator by the tunnel mouth
somebody had WDSS going.
"Big one today, folks," Tick Moran told the county. "Real big."

Her station was twenty feet of sideline
and a table she'd stocked herself.
Ninety thousand people had come to watch one football game;
she had been hired to watch one body at a time,
which struck her as the fairer half of the deal.
Thursday night the whole county had stood around a fire
and spent this game like money already in hand.
Saturday was when the register rang.

***

She knew the first half mostly as weather.

The roar came over in tides, the way it had all season,
and she had learned to read the tides without looking up
from a taped ankle: the long swell of a drive alive,
the flat drop of a flag, the groan that meant third and long.
Her two trainers worked the far end;
her eyes worked the near one.
A guard limped off in the second quarter and she watched him
walk it down the sideline until the walk told her it was
a cramp and not a strain, and by then the half was gone.

By the fourth quarter you could hear the county believing.
The bracket talk from the bonfire had come down out of the
hardware-store roofs and into the bowl itself,
a sound with teeth in it, happy and hungry at once,
and Ashford up by two scores, then one,
the visitors patient as a toothache.
The bowl overhead did its bargaining with the officials,
the replay board, and God, in that order.

She was watching the boundary receiver's hamstring,
the way he came out of his cuts a half-step careful,
when the sound changed.

Not louder. Wrong.
A swell that climbed with the scramble — she looked up in
time to see seven step out of the grasp and throw it away —
and then a seam opened in the noise,
a groan folded inside the roar like a bone inside a fist,
and the roar came apart around it.

Seven was on the turf past the numbers.

He rolled. He got a hand under himself.
He came up to a knee the slow way,
and slow was fine, slow happened forty times a game.
Then he sat back down on the turf,
and the second whistle was still in the air
when she went over the rope.

The run across a field is longer than it looks from a couch.
She had time to hear her own kit slapping her hip,
time to hear the crowd noise drain out of the bowl
from the top rows down, like a tub pulling,
time to register the officials' arms crossing over their heads
and the visiting sideline taking its helmets off.
By the time her knees hit the paint beside him,
the loudest thing on the field was breathing —
his, inside the helmet, fast and working at it, and hers.

"Be still," she said, at the earhole. "It's Doc.
You're not moving till I say. Squeeze my fingers."

He squeezed. Both sides.

"Good. Toes. Push down like a gas pedal. Good."

"Helmet stays on," she said, over the crown. "You have
the head?"

"Got it," the trainer said, and had it, two hands,
the way they'd drilled it since August.

She went along the neck
through the collar of the pads, one bone at a time,
asking her questions low and getting what she needed back,
none of it hers to repeat.
She asked the questions the laminated card asks,
in the order the card asks them,
and listened as much to the spaces as the words.

Above them, ninety thousand people made the sound
that is not silence.
Silence is empty. This had weight,
a held breath with a county inside it,
and under it small things went suddenly enormous:
a gate chain somewhere, one radio talking to itself,
a single voice high in the north stands calling a name
the PA had not said and was not going to say.
The PA asked for patience with the injury timeout,
and said no number, and no name,
and the crowd said both, quietly, to each other,
forty thousand times.

Neck clear. Story consistent. Everything moving.
"We're going to sit you up in stages," she told him.
"You race me, we start over."
Then, to the trainers, "On my go. Go."
Up to sitting, and the bowl inhaled.
Up to a knee, to his feet, a trainer under each arm,
and the sound that came down was rain crossing a lake,
applause starting nearest the tent and rolling up the rows,
gathering the whole bowl by the time he was walking.
He walked. She kept her hand on the back of his pads anyway,
all the way in, counting his steps without meaning to.

The second try was the part she didn't like.
Getting up slow is football.
Sitting back down is information.

***

The tent didn't keep the sound out.
It just took the top off it, cut the crowd to a rumor,
and left her a small hot canvas room
that smelled of turf pellets and old tape,
quiet enough to hear velcro, a pen, his breathing leveling.

Helmet off now. Pads off. The card again, the long form,
symptoms scored in his own words, balance on a line,
eyes tracking her thumb while the rumor outside
swelled and broke and swelled.
She ran the exam in the order it was printed,
because the order is the point,
and ten minutes later she ran it again.
The second set is the honest one.
What the second set told her, she wrote down
instead of saying out loud.

"Call County," she said, not looking up from the clipboard.
"Precaution, nonemergent, we're coming.
Baseline's on file with them from August — have them pull it.
And keep this radio traffic off the open channel."

She was glad of the protocol
in a way she'd never admit to a living soul —
glad it was laminated, glad it was numbered,
glad it stood there telling her hands what to do
next and next and next,
so no part of her had to hold the sound the bowl had made
when the boy sat back down.

Dan Merritt came through the flap between possessions,
headset around his neck, play sheet still in his hand,
bringing the game's noise in with him like cold air.

"What do you need?"

"Room. And your sideline's backs turned."

He gave her both.
He backed the sideline off the tent with his arms spread,
put himself between the flap and the TV truck's long lens,
a wall of windbreaker with its back to her,
and the whole time his mouth was somewhere else,
calling the next personnel group,
telling somebody the ball was coming out quick now,
his hands squaring the flap shut behind him
like he was tucking in a fence line.

"He's done today," she said, to the back of his windbreaker.
"That's medical. It's already made."

"I know," he said, and was gone.

It was the first call since June he hadn't fought her on,
and there was no time in the tent to wonder
what that was supposed to mean.

The ambulance stood at the tunnel mouth with its motor
running, and they made the walk from tent to stretcher
short and boring, which took planning.
Strapped at the shoulders, sitting up, blanket to the chest,
a jersey and a spine board's worth of caution
and the seven showing the whole way.
One hand came up off the strap as they rolled him,
and the sound that answered it from the bowl above
was the biggest of the day and the gentlest,
ninety thousand people clapping like they were afraid
of waking somebody.

She gave her report at the bumper, flat and short.
"Witnessed. No loss of consciousness anybody will swear to.
Moving everything, story straight,
second symptom screen worse than the first.
This is a precaution, so drive it like one.
No lights through town. He's had enough soundtrack."

"Sitting up and riding easy," the medic said.
"We'll keep it boring."

The medic pulled the doors,
and the game came back up over the wall in pieces
while she repacked her kit:
a swell that broke wrong, a whistle, a flat official voice
on the PA measuring out a penalty,
then a swell that kept climbing past where swells stop,
found nothing at the top, and went off like a shelf of ice
coming down, horns and brass and eighty rows of feet.
The backup's drive. All of it by ear.

At the fence an analyst stood with his headset half off,
watching the field.
"Same package the walk-on ran against the ones all week,"
he said, to nobody in particular.

The defense took the last two minutes in two long swells
that broke into nothing, and then the horn,
and then the sound the town would spend all winter
describing to people who were there.

***

"Hold on, Doc," the trooper said,
and ran her out on a gator through streets
already filling, horns finding each other block to block,
somebody's trombone loose and happy on a porch.
At the Magnolia Court the lot was emptying and refilling
at once, and Verna stood in the office doorway
with the radio turned up hoarse behind her,
the winning call getting its second replay already.

"Radio says it's the tent," Verna said.
"Radio don't know one thing past that, and says so hourly."
She lifted the cone off herself and held it to her chest
like a hymnal while Aisha got the door open.

"Go on, Doc," Verna said. "They can holler all night.
That's somebody's boy under that tent."

There wasn't time to say what that was worth,
so Aisha said thank you,
and Verna waved it off with the church fan
and turned back to her radio.

She caught the ambulance where Millrow met the square,
riding dark like she'd asked, at walking speed,
the crowd so thick the whole street had become a room.
And the room did a thing she would remember longer
than the score: the celebration went quiet
in a moving circle around the ambulance,
hats coming off in a ring that traveled with it,
and closed up loud again behind her car
like water behind a boat.

Then the last block let them go, and the road opened,
and the town shrank in her mirrors to a glow with a voice.
The radio ran the call a third time, the announcer gone
to gravel on it.
"Folks, we will play that drive on this station
till the tower falls over," he said, and his partner said,
"And we'll have more on the young man
when there's more to have."
The first honest sentence the county had been handed
all night, and it held for almost a mile.

She cut the engine under the hospital canopy lights
in the middle of the town's third listen.
The ambulance doors stood open and empty, already inside.
Behind her, thin over the dark fields, Ashford was still
at it, one long held note that didn't know yet
what the night had cost.
She stood in the bay and heard it all the way through.
Then she went inside, where it was quiet.
