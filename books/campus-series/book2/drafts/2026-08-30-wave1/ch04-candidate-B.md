# Chapter 4 — The Quiet Tent

POV: Dan, third person limited, past tense.

---

The winningest night of Dan Merritt's coaching life
smelled like burnt coffee and hand sanitizer.

The third-floor family lounge was the color of oatmeal,
eleven chairs, a coffee machine, and a TV
he'd asked the charge nurse to shut off
before the Gaults came upstairs.
The ten o'clock shows would run the hit on a loop.
Nobody in this building needed the loop.
Everybody in this building had seen it live
from the best seats in the county.

Through the one window, past the parking deck,
the sky over town still held a glow, and horns came and went,
and every phone on the square right now was playing
the backup's drive — six plays, sixty-one yards,
the kid flipping the protection at the line twice
because he'd run that package all week and owned it cold.
Dan had hugged him at the numbers and told him the truth,
that he had just won a rivalry game.
Then he'd given the radio ninety seconds in the tunnel
and driven north with the sideline paint still on his shoes.

Two hours now. He had done what he knew to do.
He'd sent a grad assistant to walk Denny Gault's truck
off the stadium lot so the family wouldn't surface at two a.m.
to a tow notice. He'd gotten the guest wifi code in writing.
He'd told the charge nurse which door a TV truck would try first,
and she'd looked at him like a man announcing water was wet,
and posted an orderly on it anyway.

What he could not do was go down the hall.
The hall was family, and a chart, and a door that stayed shut,
and that was correct, and he wouldn't have moved it an inch,
and it meant the last job left to a coach was to sit
where the family would see him whenever they came out.
So he sat. Standing read as leaving.

Denny Gault came out at twenty to eleven with his phone in his fist.

"They moved him to observation." Denny said it twice.
"That's good. Right? Observation."

"It's good he's where people watch all night," Dan said.

Denny was a big man with a big honest voice
in a quilted feed-store jacket, and he paced the little room
like it owed him a bigger one.
"Doc's coming up. Cole." He stopped. "Coach, I need it straight.
You've stood over a hundred of these. Boys get dinged,
boys come back. What's your gut say? Ten days? Give me ten days."

"My gut doesn't get a vote upstairs, Denny."

"Off the record."

"There's no off the record on a Saturday night," Dan said,
gentle as he could build it. "Let's get through tonight
before we go pricing calendars."

Denny looked at him a long moment.
"You're handling me, Coach."

"Little bit," Dan said. "It's what I've got on me."

Denny laughed once, no humor in it, and sat down hard,
and that was the truest thing either of them had managed yet.

Missy Gault had not said a word.
She sat at the end of the couch with her coat still on,
a spiral notebook from her purse open on her knee,
and every so often she wrote something down
and looked at the hallway door like she could hear through it.

Dr. Cole came up at eleven,
still in her sideline jacket with the credential on the lanyard,
a tablet under one arm, moving like the night was long
and she intended to be accurate all the way through it.

"Mr. Gault. Mrs. Gault." She didn't sit,
and she didn't stand over them either;
she pulled a chair around so the three of them made a triangle.
"Here's everything I know at eleven o'clock,
and I'll tell you which parts are solid."

"Doc—"

"Solid first," she said. "The scan ruled out the emergencies.
No bleed, no fracture. That was the fear on the field,
and he does not have it."
Denny put both hands over his face and breathed once through them.
"What he has is a concussion. A scan can't see one.
We grade it by symptoms, and the symptoms set the pace.
His brain is the only clock in this, and it doesn't take requests.
The hospital watches him tonight. I do the first recheck
in the morning, and after that we go check to check."

"Ballpark it," Denny said. "That's all I'm asking. Two weeks?
I can live with two weeks. Bracket doesn't even open till—"

"I know exactly when the bracket opens," she said.
"I'm not going to sell you a number tonight.
Anybody who does is guessing. You'd keep the number
and lose the part where it was a guess, and in ten days
the guess would be standing in this room arguing with me."
She let that sit. "Nobody knows yet. That's not me
holding something back. That is the answer,
and it's the only one I own that will still be true at breakfast."

Denny turned to Dan like a man appealing a spot.
"Coach. You hearing this?"

"I'm hearing it," Dan said.

Missy clicked her pen.
"You were in with him at 9:12 and 9:40," she said,
reading it off the notebook like a deposition.
"Nurse at 10:25. Vitals every hour on the whiteboard by his door."
She looked up. "What do I watch for tonight
that means I push the button instead of waiting for morning?"

The room went still in a new way.

Dr. Cole didn't blink. "A headache that gets worse instead of better.
Vomiting more than once. One pupil bigger than the other.
If he's hard to wake, or anything that looks like a seizure —
you don't wait for morning, you don't wait for me, you push it."
She nodded at the notebook. "Keep that going. All of it,
times and all. In two weeks that notebook will tell me more
than anything I can order."

"I know it will," Missy said. "That's why I'm keeping it."
She wasn't warm about it, and it wasn't for anybody's benefit.
It was a woman who had decided at some point tonight
that if nobody could give her a date,
she would build the record the date would come from.

Dan sold certainty for a living. Mondays at noon, all season,
a ballroom of boosters and forty minutes of it, medium rare.
He knew to the dollar what it cost to say *nobody knows*
out loud at the exact hour a room would pay anything
for the opposite, and he watched her pay it
without checking the room first, and he did not have a name
for what that was. He didn't go looking for one.

At half past, a nurse put her head in
and the Gaults went down the hall,
Denny with his phone already back out and lit,
Missy with the notebook against her chest like a clipboard.

Dr. Cole stayed to finish her charting, thumb and stylus,
fast, exact. Dan went to the alcove,
where the machine had cooked the pot down to varnish hours ago.
There was one cup on the counter he'd poured
before the Gaults came up and never touched.
He carried it back and held it out.

"It's the last one that isn't varnish," he said.

She looked at the cup. Then she took it.
It was the first time since June the two of them had been
inside ten feet of each other without a roster between them.

"Press availability is Monday noon," he said.
"Anybody calls you about a timeline, send them to me.
I run vague better than anybody in this league."

"I don't do vague," she said.

"I know it. That's why they'll come at you first."

She drank the coffee. The machine hummed.
Down on the street somebody's horn did the first six notes
of the fight song and quit.
Neither of them moved, and he noticed his hands,
which had nothing to carry for the first time
since the tent went up at the fifty that afternoon.
The lounge had the same quiet the tent had —
a working quiet, with a boy on the other side of it.

Her tablet buzzed. She drained the cup like it was a task.

"Go home, Coach," she said, and it wasn't unkind.
"There's no film to fix tonight."

"I know it," he said. "Kitchen downstairs closed at eight.
Anybody feed them?"

She paused at the hallway door. "Not that I saw."

"Then I've got a stop to make."

***

The drive over ran dark and empty against the celebration.
He'd have traded the win. He ran that one the way he ran film,
slow, three times through, looking for the lie in it, and it held.
That was his to keep. Monday at noon he'd stand in a ballroom
and say the right things about toughness and prayers,
and nobody in that room would ever hear this part.

The Blind Mule at a quarter to twelve was wall to wall,
windows sweated over, both TVs running the drive
with the sound off because the jukebox had the floor.
Peanut Kyzer was pulling four taps at once
and pointing people to tables with his chin,
and when he caught sight of Dan he didn't holler
the room into a toast, which Dan would remember.
He tipped his head toward the kitchen window instead.

"Verna called it in an hour ago," Peanut said.
"Said nobody up at that hospital has ate since noon.
It's boxed. There's a fourth in there for the doc."

Sonny Dillard spun half around on his stool.
"Coach! Six plays! Sixty-one yards! I have not sat down since!"

"Sit down, Sonny," Odell Fountain said, and Sonny did not.
Odell's voice came down a shelf. "How's the boy?"

"Resting," Dan said. "Family's with him."

The nearest tables had gone quiet to hear it,
and the answer got passed back table to table,
and for a breath the Mule was two rooms,
one celebrating and one waiting.

Sonny raised his bottle. "To modern medicine —
may none of us never need it!"

Nobody drank to it. Sonny studied his label.

"Board's already going," Odell said. "Folks asking
when he's back before the ambulance cleared the lot."

Peanut snapped a lid on the last box and bagged the four of them.
"They'll sing all winter either way," he said.
"This county forgives a loss by Wednesday.
What it don't forgive is being told to wait."

On both screens the drive ran again, silent,
and it would run all winter, in this bar
and every other one in the county.
Dan paid for the boxes and left the tip in cash.
Sixty-one yards was the cheap part of the night.
The bill was a boy behind a door and a mother
building a record in a spiral notebook,
and there was not one damn thing in his playbook
that touched either, and he knew it,
and he carried supper out into the cold knowing it.

***

He sat in the truck in the visitors' deck
with the boxes warming the passenger seat
and did the thing he did every night of his life in season.
He opened the board.

The top thread was forty minutes old.

> **Prayers + one question (NOT stirring anything)** — *KelliAnn
> Buford:* What a night!! Whole house lost our voices. Prayers up
> for the family at the hospital. Please no names in the comments.
>
> **Denny Gault:** Family here. He is resting and we feel every
> prayer. Not blaming one living soul. We are just asking for
> clarity on the evaluation timeline so a family can plan around
> it. That's all we ask.
>
> **Tammy Rideout:** Nobody appreciates careful more than this
> house. But careful and a bracket are both real things. Just
> being honest.
>
> **MarcyKay Prentiss:** Meal train sign-up in the comments.
> Nobody in that house cooks this month but us.
>
> **Bo Sessions:** Nephew had the same deal two falls back, missed
> five days. Every kid is different I guess. Praying hard.
>
> **Denny Gault:** Thank you all. We know how short a season is.
> The people making the decisions know it too. I have to believe
> that.

Nobody had said a name. Nobody had blamed one living soul.
It was the politest thing he'd read all season.
Denny had typed it from a hallway chair
with his boy on the other side of a door,
and Dan couldn't even call it wrong.
That was the trouble with this board and always had been.
It was never wrong enough to put down.

Upstairs, Denny was in the lounge corner, phone lit, thumbs going.
Missy took the bag with both hands, looked inside, and counted.

"There's a fourth box," she said.

"For Dr. Cole. Verna's orders."

"Good," Missy said, and wrote *12:05 — supper* in the notebook,
because everything went in the notebook now.

He should have gone home. Film at seven, staff at eight;
the season did not observe hospitals.
He sat back down instead, in the chair built for a smaller man,
where a family could find a coach if they came out.

Midnight went by on the hallway clock.
The phone buzzed against his leg, once, then twice —
the thread climbing, the county asking politely in the dark.
Every night since they'd handed him this whistle,
he had read that board down to the last reply
before he let himself sleep, and it had never had more to say
about him than it would have by morning.

He took his last look at the board, unread, and pocketed it.
