# Chapter 3 — Rivalry Saturday

POV: Aisha Cole, third person limited, past tense.
(FIRST DRAFT — wave-1 blind candidate A, card D3 short-declarative.
Raw material for the author's own pass; not done.)

---

Ninety minutes to kickoff, Aisha ran the medical time out
at the mouth of the north tunnel.

Cold night. Breath standing up in the lights.
She went around the circle by trade — her two athletic trainers,
the visiting team's physician, the ambulance crew, the X-ray tech,
the man who kept the key to the northeast gate.

"Cart comes in through the northeast gate," she said.
"Board's staged at the thirty, both sidelines.
Airway kit rides with me.
If we go to a spine count, the count is mine and nobody else talks.
Crew, you're reconfirming the trauma center on the hour."

"Confirmed at five," the medic said.

"Same protocol our side," the visiting physician said.
"One of mine goes down, my tent, your board."

"Agreed. Then we're done. Good game, everybody."

The visiting physician shook her hand. The gate man touched his cap.
It had taken her six months to get this walkthrough down
to eight minutes, and she was proud of that
in a way she could not have explained at a dinner party.

By kickoff the bowl was full.
Ninety thousand people in coats, flags stiff on the rim,
the band under everything like a pulse the county had agreed on.
Rivalry Saturday. They had been talking about this game
since the bonfire, and the bonfire was Thursday,
and before Thursday they had only been talking about it all year.

Everybody in the stadium watched the ball.
She was paid to watch everything else.
Ninety thousand people, and one person looking
for the wrong kind of stillness. That was the job.
She liked the job. She checked her gate sightline anyway.

***

For three quarters it was a football game.

A good one. Seventeen to fourteen, visitors, going into the fourth,
and number seven having the night the county had ordered for him.
Every time the jersey got up, the noise reset itself.
Down the sideline, Merritt worked the game
the way she worked a chart — nothing wasted, nothing loud
that didn't need to be loud.
She noticed it the way you notice weather,
and went back to watching her own sideline.

Her own sideline gave her a tackle with a bad ankle
in the second quarter, walked off under his own power.
She looked at it in the blue tent and taped what she found.

"Give me that ankle again at the half," she told her trainer.

"He'll say it's fine."

"It will be. After I look at it."

Normal night. Normal traffic.
She charted it anyway.
A normal night is a thing you can only prove afterward.

Fourth quarter. Six and change on the clock.
Ashford ball, down three, driving.
The replay board showed the county to itself between plays,
and the county roared at its own face.
Three more minutes of this and the bracket
would have Ashford's name in it by church tomorrow.

Third and six at the visitors' forty-one.
Seven took the snap and held it a breath too long.
He kept it himself. Cut inside the rush.
Got the six, and two more, and the field arrived on top of him
from two directions right at the whistle.

The pile took its time.

Players peeled off one at a time, the way they do.
Seven rolled to his knees. The crowd started the applause
it gives a man who gets up slow.
He got one foot under himself.
Then he sat back down on the grass, careful,
like a man setting down something that belonged to somebody else.

She was already through the gate.

Grass, cold air, forty yards of it.
Her lead trainer got there first and took the helmet in both hands
and held the head still, which is the whole first job.
She knelt at the shoulder. She told the kid whose hands were on him
and why, and kept her voice at charting speed.
The referee moved everybody back without being asked twice.

Airway clear. Breathing easy. He was awake.
She ran her hands down the back of his neck,
one vertebra at a time, and asked about pain while she went.
She had him squeeze her fingers. Both hands. Both worked.
He asked her what happened.
She told him, plain and short.

"You're all right to be still," she said.
"We do the moving. That's the whole arrangement."

"Facemask."
The little driver whined four times. The mask came away.
The helmet stayed on. That is what the helmet is for now.

"Board. We roll on my count."
Six sets of hands. One, two, three, and he was on it.
Straps at the forehead, chest, hips, knees.
It is a slow procedure. It is slow on purpose.
Ninety thousand people stood in silence built for one,
and somewhere up in it a man's voice was praying out loud,
not embarrassed, taking his time too.

The big screen showed the empty end zone. It did not show the play
again. You could feel the whole bowl wanting it to,
wanting one more look so it could call the hit harmless.
The board gave them a graphic about the bracket instead.

The PA asked the crowd to hold quiet
for an injured Ashford player.
He did not say the number.
Ninety thousand people said it anyway, low, to each other,
and the word for that sound is a murmur
but it was the loudest thing she'd ever worked under.

As the cart turned for the northeast gate,
one hand came up off the board, thumb out.
The bowl roared with relief.
Crowds cheer a thumb. She had seen thumbs go up
from spine boards before.
It meant the thumb worked. She kept her eyes on the straps.

***

The tent was warmer than the field and quieter than anywhere.

She unclipped what could be unclipped and went to work.

"Same questions as out there," she told him.
"You'll get tired of me before I get tired of asking."

What field is this. He got it.
What quarter is it. He was slow, and then close enough.
Who scored last. He got that too, which figured.
Then he asked her what happened.
He had already asked her that, out on the grass.
She answered it the same way, in the same words,
and wrote down the time.

She stepped to the flap and gave the crew
the hand signal they had rehearsed at five o'clock.
No lights. No hurry. Just yes.

Above the tent, ninety thousand people bargained —
with God, with the replay board,
with whoever it is that handles these things.
She could hear the whole negotiation through the canvas.

Merritt came down the sideline at a jog
and stopped himself a yard outside the flap,
exactly where the protocol sheet put him,
a sheet she happened to know he had fought line by line in July.

"What do you need from me," he said.

"Your sideline away from my gate."

He turned and cleared it with one arm and no words.
Then he stood there with the game going on behind his back.

"He's awake," she said. "He's answering me.
He's going in for pictures. That's what I know."

"Okay," he said, and looked at the field
like it was a chore he'd been assigned.
"Doors are yours. Nobody talks tonight but me."

Then he went back to his game.

Six months of fighting her on every call,
and tonight he did as he was told
and didn't ask her for the one answer she didn't have.
There was no time to make anything of that, and she didn't.
The ambulance doors were open and the cold came in with them.

"Riding with us, doc?" the medic said.

"Behind you. Keep it easy."

They were loading when the sound came.
It went up in stages, disbelief first, then all at once,
a roar with a bracket inside it,
and the medic looked at her and she shrugged
and they finished the straps.

***

She took her own car and stayed behind the ambulance the whole way.

The radio was still roaring. Everybody on it was.
The backup had taken the last drive eighty yards
and put it in the corner with under a minute to play,
and the play-by-play man had gone up an octave and stayed there.
"He's got the corner — he's got *all* of it," the radio said,
and then said again, because the station kept replaying the call
like the county might not believe it the first four times.
The color man got one sentence in edgewise —
give the kid credit, that was the same look
the scout-team walk-on ran at the ones all week —
and then the crowd noise ate the broadcast whole.

Downtown, the square was already filling.
Horns, flags, strangers hugging strangers
under the lights the town had hung two weeks early,
and the ambulance moved through the edge of all of it,
slow and steady, no siren, and the crowd parted for it
and closed up celebrating behind it.

Thursday night the whole county had stood around a fire
and asked out loud for this exact Saturday.
Tonight it got the winning and the bill both.

There was a thing she would never say out loud,
to anybody, in this county or another one.
Kneeling on that forty-one with a boy's helmet
held still between two sets of hands,
she had been sure of every single thing she was doing.
It was the surest she had felt since June.
She was not proud of that. She kept it anyway.

At the hospital doors she gave the report the way she'd want it
given to her.

"Helmeted athlete, witnessed head impact.
No loss of consciousness observed.
Ambulatory on scene, then not.
Repeat questioning times two, documented.
Precautions held throughout. He's answering."

After that it stopped being hers for a while.
The right people had him.
She stood in the ambulance bay until standing there
was a performance, and then she left.

***

The office light at the Magnolia Court was on.
It was always on. Tonight it was the only lit window on the block.

Verna Poteat sat behind the desk with the space heater going
and the radio up, callers lined up around the county
to be happy on the air one at a time.

"There's cocoa packets in the drawer if you want one," Verna said.
"Some of them's older than the drawer."

Aisha stood in the doorway with her keys in her hand.

Verna turned the radio down one notch. "How's the boy."

"I can't say."

"Can't or won't."

"Both."

"You eat anything since noon?"

"No."

"Figured."

Verna nodded. She looked past Aisha, out the glass,
toward the noise three streets over,
and turned the radio down the rest of the way.

"They can holler on that square all night," she said.
"That's somebody's boy under that tent."

"Yes," Aisha said. "He is."

Verna turned the radio back up, quiet, company-volume.
Aisha went out along the walk to eleven.

Room eleven smelled like clean towels and the heater's first hour.
The two suitcases stood where they always stood.
She sat down on the end of the bed with her coat on
and set the phone face up on the blanket beside her.
The hospital had her number. The square was still going.
The phone stayed dark, and she watched it stay dark.
