> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Kendra Voyles:** Mine says there's ice coming down there midweek
> next week. I asked if the heat in his dorm works. "It's fine, Mom."
> Two states away and I'm the one checking the forecast.


Sonny Dillard had a twenty folded in one hand and his coat half zipped,
and he was paying at the register at a quarter to seven.
The register stood between Dan Merritt's stool and an empty one,
where it always stood.
On Friday, the eighth of January, the portal window had six days left in it,
and it shut on Thursday.
Dan had coffee in his hand and the Coach's Slice in front of him,
set down without a word by the cook.
He worked the griddle alone in winter, and the register with it.
At the window Earlene Tatum had the paper and Tunk Ferrell had her ear,
and that was the room.
Most of Dan's class had signed in December.
Two had said yes in the fall and had not signed,
because their day to sign was in February,
and since Monday neither family had picked up a phone.
He had carried that down the hill with him,
and he ate with it and said nothing about it to anybody.

Sonny took his change and did not go.
He stood at the register in half his gear with the bills folded in one hand,
looking at the sign over the pass-through and not at Dan.
He said one thing, at the volume he used for parts.

"A verbal's a promise off a seventeen-year-old, Coach.
I don't build a pumper on a promise.
I build on the purchase order,
and I keep the old part on the truck till the box shows up."

He put the bills in his shirt pocket and went out to a shift.
Dan did not answer him, and Sonny had not stopped for an answer.

"Two best in the class and neither one's picking up," Tunk said,
to the paper. "That's what the board says."

"The board says a lot," Earlene said.

"They ought to make 'em sign in December like the rest."

Nobody answered that. Earlene did not look up from the paper.
Dan heard all of it and did not turn around,
because a coach who turned around at the Liars' Table
had just told the Table something.

The stool beside him stayed empty.
It was empty because it was a quarter to seven.
At seven a kid with two taped fingers would sit on it and eat on her card.
At half past, any other weekday, it was Aisha's,
her phone on the counter beside the plate.
Dan had worked out both of those in this room and had said neither,
and he was gone up the hill before either of them came.
He looked at the stool once, and it was empty and it was hers,
and he liked an empty stool better than a man had any right to.
At five to seven he paid beside it
and was on the hill before the kid came in.

***

The father called at ten, on the desk phone in Dan's office,
with the door open on the corridor.
Dan had the January roster under one hand and a pen in the other,
and he knew the area code before he knew the voice.
It was the father of the quarterback he had flipped.

The kid was a college player in the window,
and all of it had happened inside the window, in six days.
On Sunday he had said yes to another school, from his phone.
On Wednesday he had said yes to Ashford instead.
That was what flipping a kid was, and the county had watched it on the board.
A coach whose own quarterback had sat out a playoff
had gone and got himself a second one.
The county would read that the way it had read the first-team line
on a December practice sheet.
Dan had done it anyway,
because a roster with one quarterback on it was a prayer.
The kid had an agent and a number before he ever opened the phone.
The father had the phone.

"I'll be straight with you, Coach," the father said.
"He reads that board down there. So do I.
Your doctor sat your starter for a playoff.
Is that what she does, or was that once?"

Dan heard the *your* and let it stand where the man had put it.
A stranger two states off had put the doctor on his side of the lot,
and Dan let him.

"She holds kids out here," he said.
"She held mine out of the biggest game this county has had,
and she'd do it again Monday if the numbers said so.
If that's a problem, there are programs where it isn't one,
and I'll name you two. This isn't one of them."

There was a while of nothing on the line, the kind a man used to think.
"I'll talk to my son," the father said, and that was the call.

Dan put the phone down and did not pick it back up to see if it would ring.
It did not ring again that day.
He had told the man the truth as if it were the building's address.
It might have cost him a quarterback,
and there had not been another way to tell it.

***

At one o'clock Dan was at the same desk with the same roster
and a sandwich he had not started, and Rex Boyd came through his door.
Nobody had walked him up.
Every door in the Fieldhouse stood open by Dan's rule.
Boyd had come in the front one and up the corridor
past a building full of coaches, and none of them had stopped him.
He stood in the open door. He did not come through it.
The coat was dark and the shoes under it were shined.
He had no folder and no phone.
His hands were in his coat pockets, and he was already smiling.
He did not sit. He did not shut the door.

"Coach."

"Mr. Boyd."

"I won't sit. I'm on my way somewhere."
He said it at the volume he said everything,
low enough that the corridor got none of it.
"The pledge is on the February agenda. So is your extension.
They're sequenced, the pledge and then the coach's line behind it,
because that's how a board likes to read a page.
If the medical situation embarrasses the program before then,
they get re-sequenced. That's all it is.
I thought you'd rather have it from me than read it off a table."

Dan sat with it.
He made himself say it over in plain words, because the man had not.
The pledge was Boyd's money for the building,
the glass one with the sunset,
the one Dan had liked out loud in his film room in the fall.
The extension was Dan's contract,
the one the athletic director had called a review after the loss.
Sequenced meant one line on a page and then the next.
Re-sequenced meant the money waited,
and his contract waited behind it, or came off the page.
The medical situation was Trey, and the doctor who had held him,
and whatever she said the next time she looked at him.
Boyd had just set Dan's contract on the far side of that.

He knew why the man was in his door in January and not in December.
On the nineteenth there had been a thing cued on the video board
behind the score, and it had stayed behind the score.
The men in the suite had gone home without their night.
This was what Boyd had brought instead of a stage.

"Say it plain, Mr. Boyd," Dan said.
"One sentence. What do you want from me."

Boyd did not say it.
He left a silence instead, in the door of Dan's office, with the smile on it.
It was the third one he had left Dan, after Delmar's porch and the film room,
and Dan knew the shape of it by now.
In December he had sat in it with his hands on the table and let it stand.
This time he stood up out of his chair and came around the desk.
He stood in his own door with the man, close enough to shake his hand,
and said nothing from there instead.
He had asked the direct question
because that was the man he had been in this building before any of this.
He had it in him to ask it once and not twice.
Boyd waited a while longer.
Then he nodded, once, as if the silence had been the sentence,
and took his hands out of his pockets to go, and went.
Dan heard him go.
The shined shoes went down the corridor past every open door,
not fast and not slow, and the front door took him on its closer.
Then it was one o'clock in a building with a class to close
and a price on it.

The price was a call she had not made yet.
He could not ask her which way it would go.
Tonight she would be across a table from him, in his house,
and he was not going to ask her there either.

***

The coordinator was in the film room at three with the clicker
and a bowl-game cutup nobody in this building had been to.
The door was open, and the hall was empty.
Dan came in with nothing in his hands, stood by the marker tray,
and said it once.

"Get your name out before February. Don't wait on my review.
If that table goes wrong, you're in the room as my man.
Every staff in the league is building a roster this month
and hiring for it, and in March they won't be."

He did not say it twice.
The coordinator kept the clicker where it was
and did not look up from the screen.

"Most head coaches wait till they're fired to start firing people."

Dan laughed, once, out loud, in the film room,
and the coordinator did not, because that was how he told a joke.
It was the first time Dan had laughed all day.
Then the coordinator put the clicker down on the tray.

"I'll make two calls Monday," he said, and picked it back up.

He did not say thank you, and Dan had not said it for that.
Nobody in the hall had heard it.
That was the man who would have sat beside him at the trustees in February,
and Dan had just sent him to the phone.
It was right, and it cost what it cost.

The trainer went by the open door at half past with the cart
and stopped it with his foot.

"The doctor's got Trey on the bike next week, Coach. I'll be in the room."

"All right."

That was the rule's own channel and not a break in it.
The trainer told the coach the schedule,
and the doctor told the coach her call across a sheet at five fifteen
with the trainer in earshot, and nowhere else.
In January there was no sheet.
There was no practice for one to sit on until spring workouts,
so there was no five fifteen,
and her calls reached him through nobody until then.
He had agreed to that in her office in December
with her hand flat on the door, and he had it still.
Next week Trey would be on a bike in a quiet room,
her hand on the chest strap and her eyes on a number.
Dan had never seen her do that and could see it anyway.

***

Her text had come at two minutes past four, timed and short,
as she always wrote.

*4:02. Friday. The house, if it's still yours. Seven.*

He had typed back longer, because that was him,
and he had known what a screenshot was when he did it.

*It's mine. Chili from Wednesday, and I'll sit down for it. Seven.
Walk careful.*

At seven Dan was in his kitchen with the light over the stove on
and the pot on the burner, alone, and the knock came, two knocks.
The RAV4 was where it lived every night,
in Verna's lot in front of room nine.
Aisha had walked from there in the dark,
because a car parked where it always parked
was nothing anybody could see.
Kat was not down this weekend,
so there was no wine night to be missed
and nobody in the Mule's back booth to wonder where the doctor was.
It was the first time she had been in his house since the town came home.

He opened the door.
Aisha Cole was on the step in the parka with the hood up
and her hands in the pockets.
Her face inside the hood was looking straight at him
and not smiling yet.

"You said seven," she said.

"I've had the pot on since six."

Then the smile came, the whole one, and she came in.

She went past him into the hall, and the cold came in on the parka.
Under the cold was the smell of her, which he had no word for.
He stood in his own door a second after she was past it
before he shut it.
She took her phone out of the parka before the parka came off,
laid it on his table screen to the wood, and did not look at it again.
Then the parka went on the hook over his coat,
and she came back into the kitchen in her sweater with her hands empty.
There was cold water in her curls where the hood had not covered them,
going to nothing in the heat off the stove.

He got the beer out for himself and the sparkling water for her,
set the can at her place without asking, and she watched him do it.

"Beer again."

"It's my drink. The other one was yours."

"It was a good night for the other one."

He filled two bowls at the stove and put them on the table with the two chairs,
and sat, and she watched him sit.
He ate standing at that counter every other night he had lived here,
and this was the second time he had not.
She sat across from him and put her elbows on his table
and looked at him in his own kitchen.
Something under his ribs went fast,
in his own chair with a spoon in his hand,
and he had not decided one thing yet.

"The boxes are done," she said. "You wrote the last number Tuesday."

"In my own marker."

"In your own marker." She ate.
"Tell me the week. Not the calls. The week."

He told her the week as anybody in town could have told it.
Two families had gone quiet and the phone had not rung,
and the board had noticed, and the Table had noticed the board.
He told her a father had called and that he had told the man the truth.
She looked at him over the spoon when he said that,
and something in her face went still and pleased at once.

Then he said the other thing, looking at her while he did it.
"Boyd came by."

He said that and nothing else, and she heard the nothing else.
She looked at him a while with the spoon stopped,
her eyes brown and steady on his face,
and her mouth stayed shut on the question.
He watched her decide not to ask it.
She had not told him the rest on Monday, and he had let it be,
and now it was hers to let be, and she did.

"All right," she said, and went back to the chili.

It was later, with the bowls near empty, that he got it wrong.
The trainer's line had been in his pocket all afternoon,
and it came out at his own table before he had looked at it.

"The trainer came by the film room," he said.
"He says he's got Trey on the bike next week, and I wondered if—"
He got that far, and no farther.

She set her spoon down, and it made no noise on the table.
"There's a kitchen now, Dan," she said, level, not loud,
"and I told you in December what I'd do if there was ever a kitchen."

He sat there with the rest of the question unsaid
and put it away.
He picked up the beer instead and did not drink from it.

"That's fair," he said.

Then neither of them said anything, and it went on.
The refrigerator ran.
She looked at the can and not at him.
She turned it a quarter turn on the wood.
She did not pick it up.
He looked at the picture in the hall, the only thing on any wall,
and it was no help to him.
It was the length of a play clock.
Then it was longer than that.
Neither of them got up from the table.
She did not fill it for him and he did not fill it for her,
because the rule was hers and the asking had been his.
The two of them had put this silence here on purpose in December
without knowing what it would be like to sit in.
Her jaw was set.
Her eyes came back to him once and went away again,
and he saw what it cost her, and let her see that he had.

His side of it he had paid at one o'clock,
and hers he could see from where he sat.
She was the wall, in his house, with his food in front of her.
She had built the wall with her own hands
so that nobody could ever say he had leaned on it.

She picked the spoon back up.
There was nothing left in the bowl for it, and she held it anyway.

"The chili's better on the second day," she said.

"It's Wednesday's."

"Then it's a third day. It's better on the third."

He laughed at that, and it came out too loud for the kitchen,
and she smiled at the laugh and not at the joke.
Then she asked him what he would put on the walls,
and he still had not got that far, and she said so.
The dinner went on, because the two of them had decided it would.
He got up and got her a second can she had not asked for.
She drank half of it and told him Verna had asked her at the office window
whether the doctor was eating, and that she had said she was.

At ten she stood up and got the parka off the hook herself.
He stood in the hall with his hands at his sides,
and then in his pockets, because there was nothing in them.

"I'm going to want to ask you again," he said.
"Not tonight, but some night before February.
I'm telling you now so you can stop me again."

"I'll stop you again."
She zipped the parka and put the hood up,
and her face inside it was tired and was his anyway.
"It isn't that I don't want to tell you. I want to tell you everything.
That's why I can't."

She put her hand in his, once, in the hall.
His hand closed around it, and hers was small in it, and he held it
and did nothing else with it.
Her eyes came up to his inside the hood and stayed there.
Neither of them moved for as long as that took.
Then she took her hand back, because she had a walk to make.
He opened the door for her and she went down the step into the dark.
He shut the door behind her and stood with his hand flat on it.
She would walk back to room nine under Verna's office light,
which stayed on till eleven, and Verna would never say a word.
That was the town being home, and those were the terms,
and they ran under four weeks more.

***

At one in the morning the light over the stove was still on.
Dan stood under it in his kitchen with the two bowls in the sink
and the chair she had sat in still out from the table.
Friday had gone over into Saturday, and he was alone.
He took the phone out and opened the board, because he opened it last thing
every night,
and tonight he did not want to and opened it anyway.
He read to the bottom.
The heat post from the top of the board had nine replies under it now,
mothers from three states, every one about the dorm and none about ice.
A flight. A boy who had called on a Thursday for once.
Then, under a name he did not know, from out of state,
a post about the class.
It was polite.
It asked whether the way medical decisions got made down there
might be why two families had gone quiet this week.
It said that was not criticism, it was clarity.
That was a word the board had learned in the fall and kept.
The board had been putting Coach and the doctor in one sentence for months,
and it did not need a new reason to.
Nobody had typed under the post.
Dan typed under it.

*The doctor holds kids out here. She held my quarterback out of a
playoff and she was right to. If your son gets hurt in this program he
sits until he's healed, and there is not one other thing on this board a
parent should want more than that. Dan Merritt.*

He read it back once.
It was true, every line of it, and it had his name on it.
By nine it would be a screenshot,
his name under hers on a board twelve thousand people read,
a coach defending the doctor's call in public.
That was the one thing he had promised her in December there would not be,
and a man in his door had priced it at one o'clock.
He did not post it.
He took it out a letter at a time, so he would have to watch it go.
The name went first, because he had put it last.
*Dan Merrit. Dan Merri. Dan Merr. Dan Mer. Dan Me. Dan M. Dan.*
Then the *Dan* went, and the sentence about the parent went after it,
one letter and then the next.
The *t* came off *that*, then the *a*, then the *h*,
until the box had the first line in it and nothing else.
*The doctor holds kids out here.*
He took that too.
*The doctor holds kids out. The doctor holds. The doctor. The.*
Then the box was empty and the cursor sat in it, going on and off.
He put the phone down on the counter and let it go dark.

He stood there under the stove light and thought about her, in plain words,
because it was one in the morning and the hour was his.
She had sat at his table three hours ago with her spoon down
and told him no without raising her voice.
It had been the right no, and she had hated it.
He had watched her hate it and had not been able to help her with it.
The thing he was afraid of was new since one o'clock,
and it was not the one from New Year's night.
He was not afraid she would make the wrong call.
She would make the right one, on the number, the way she made all of them.
He was afraid of what came after the right one.
She would know what it had cost him, because she knew what everything cost,
and she would carry it.
There was not one thing he could say across any table
that would take it off her.

Then it was her again, as it went at this hour.
The chair she had sat in was still out from the table
at the angle she had pushed it back to, at ten, to stand up.
He had walked past it twice tonight and left it.
It could stay there.
In the morning he would eat standing at the counter and look at it,
out where she had left it.
It would still be hers when she came back through that door.
