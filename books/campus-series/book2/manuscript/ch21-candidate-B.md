Missy Gault carried the chair out of Aisha's office herself,
the one chair left in a room that was boxes to the window.
She set it in the corridor outside the quiet room with its back to the wall.
She sat on it in her coat with the notebook on her knee and the pen out,
her face level under the corridor light.
Aisha stood in the quiet room's door with the chest strap in her hand.
It was Wednesday the thirteenth of January, past two in the afternoon,
and the sky over the practice field had gone the gray the board had promised.
Trey Gault sat the annex's bike in a gray hoodie with the hood up
and his back to the door.
The trainer stood inside the room by the wall with his arms folded,
because he had said he would be in the room, and he was.
Nobody in it was talking, which was what the room was for.

She put the strap under the hoodie herself and it paired to the tablet,
and the number came up on the screen, ninety-one, sitting.
In December she had put Trey on this bike for a step,
and the step had failed at fourteen minutes and the clock had started over.
This was not a step.
There was no practice until spring workouts and nothing to clear him into,
and no page of hers was going to clear him before there was.
This was the check she owed his chart under load,
twenty minutes at the cap, before there was anything to put him in.
The cap was one-forty, the same cap as December's.
She had said it to the trainer at the door, once, and he had nodded once.

Dan had asked for this room in December and she had told him not you,
and he had kept it.
He was across the lot in his own building with the heat on
and his staff in it.
He did not have this number, and he was not going to have it from her.
The trainer would carry it over sometime this afternoon,
at a door, in a sentence.
That was the channel she had built,
and she stood inside it with the tablet.

Twenty minutes.
She asked at four, and at eight, and at twelve,
coming around the bike each time so he could see her ask.
The answers came back short and the same,
and she wrote the minutes on the tape on her left wrist.
Fourteen came, the minute December had stopped at,
and she watched the cadence and not the hood.
The cadence held. The number sat at one thirty-three.
His right hand stayed on the bar where it was.
Sixteen. Eighteen.
At twenty she told him to quit pedaling and stay where he was.
She took the number off the screen with her thumb
and wrote 20 on the tape and the time under it, 2:41.
She typed it standing, before she said a word to anybody.
Twenty minutes at the cap. No symptom. Cadence held.
Heart rate one thirty-three.
Re-evaluation under load, January thirteenth.
Then she looked at the twenty on the screen against December's fourteen.
It was six minutes more than December had given her,
and she looked at it a second longer than she had to.

"7's off it," the trainer said, to the corridor,
to nobody in it but Missy.

"Take him out to the car," Aisha said.

Missy stood up in the corridor with the notebook open.
The pen was already on the page.

"What step does it say he's on."

"It doesn't say a step.
There's nothing for him to step into until spring,
so it says what it says.
Twenty minutes at the cap, held. December was fourteen."

Missy wrote.
Aisha could read it upside down from the door, one word, *Twenty*,
and the time beside it.

"Tonight, whatever he says about his head, and the hour he says it."

"And the next one."

"When there's something to put him in. Not a day before that."

Missy looked at the screen in Aisha's hand and then at her own page.
Her face was the face she had brought up the hill, level,
and it stayed level; the pen was the only thing on her that moved.
Trey went down the corridor with the hood still up
and the trainer two steps behind him,
and Missy shut the notebook and went after them.


Her office at the warm end of the annex was one desk and the boxes,
numbered in his marker, stacked along the walls as high as the sill.
At three Aisha sat at the desk with the tablet propped on the last box
that was not taped, and opened a new file under Trey Gault's name.
Nobody had asked her for it.
A scout was a man a pro team paid to watch a college kid
and say what he was worth in the spring.
The scouts would come to Ashford in the spring for Trey Gault.
The first thing any of them would ask
was what had happened to his head in November.
She was going to have the answer on one screen, in order,
before any of them asked it.

Both scans, the August one and the one she had ordered herself.
The tape times off her wrist, all of them, dated.
Every rung of the ladder in her typing,
the day it was tried and the day it held.
The fourteen minutes in December and the twenty today.
The hold on the nineteenth in the middle of it,
put there as the reason his brain was worth drafting
and not the reason it was not.
That was the file.
She said it to herself once in the boxed office,
so she would know what she was building, and then she built it.

It cost her the evening, and she had nothing on the evening
but the drive down.
It would cost more if the board ever heard of it.
A doctor who sat a quarterback in December
and built his draft file in January
was a sentence the board could type without any help.
She typed the first line anyway.

He had tried to ask her about this bike at his own table on Friday,
and had got as far as *I wondered if* before she stopped him.
She had hated stopping him, and had done it.
This file was his quarterback's next ten years,
and she was building it at a desk he had numbered the boxes around.
He could not ask her about it, and she could not tell him,
and she wanted to.

Missy Gault came back up the corridor for the chair at half past three.
Aisha had forgotten the chair. Missy had not.
She came in without knocking, because the door stood open on the boxes.
She saw the screen before she saw anything else.
Trey's name was at the top of it, the two scans side by side under it,
and RETURN TO PLAY, SCOUT COPY in Aisha's typing across the top.
She read it from the door, all of it, once.
Aisha did not turn the tablet away, and did not explain it.
Missy did not ask.
She took the chair by its back and carried it around the desk
to where it had been.
She set it down and squared it to the desk with both hands.
Then she stood with her hands on the back of it
a minute longer than the chair took.
That was all she did with her hands,
and she said nothing about the screen.
"I'll have him home by four," she said, which was about the road,
and went.
She had driven him up, and she drove him down
while Millrow was still only wet.


By four there was a sound on the window over the desk,
small and steady, and it was not rain.
Aisha looked up from the file with her hands still on the keys.
The lot outside had gone from wet to shining while she typed.
Across it the Fieldhouse lot was half empty
and the front door was going every few minutes.
Dan had sent his staff down the hill before dark,
and their cars had gone down one after another
before the hill went to ice.
His film-room window was lit, and it was the last one lit on that side.
He was the last one up the hill, as he was every night of the winter,
and tonight there was a reason for it besides the window.

The trainer was in her door at half past four with his coat zipped.
He had his keys in his hand and was not going to use them.

"Coach sent everybody down at three. The building's him and me.
Mine's staying up here. I'm not putting it on that hill tonight."

"And mine's me, over here."

"He says he's running us both down in the truck.
He said the doctor too."

Nobody else was in either building.
The trainer had said so, and she walked her own corridor anyway,
past the whirlpool room and the quiet room with the bike stopped in it.
It was hers and empty.
The RAV4 sat at the warm-end door where it lived by day,
with the ice on its glass, and it was going to stay there.
She had driven Millrow in daylight, up at seven and down at four,
and that was all she knew of it.
Dan Merritt drove it down in the dark every night of the winter,
and he knew where the hill was bad before he got to it.
That was the reason, and she did not argue with it from a desk.

At ten to five the lot lit up.
The truck came across it from the Fieldhouse side at a walk,
its lights out ahead of it on the ice,
and stopped short of the warm-end door.
Dan left it running and got out and came the last ten feet on foot,
his hands out of his pockets for the ice.
He stood in her door with the weather on the shoulders
of the sideline jacket.
He looked at her at the desk, at the tablet, at the parka on the hook,
and then at her face, and stayed there.

"Doc. Get your coat.
Nothing's been down that hill since three, and nothing's going to be."

"The RAV4 stays."

"It stays. I'll bring you up for it when it's a road again."

She shut the file and put the tablet in the parka's inside pocket,
and took the parka off the hook.
He did not help her into it,
because the trainer was in the corridor behind him.


The trainer went out first and stood at the truck's one passenger door,
holding it.
He looked at her, and she looked at him.
The truck had two doors and one seat across, a bench,
and three people to put on it.
Neither of them was going to be the one to say who sat in the middle.

"I'm the size for it," Aisha said, and got in first
and slid to the middle.

Dan laughed once from the driver's side, out loud, on the ice,
and the trainer did not.
The trainer got in after her and shut the door on the weather.

Inside it was warm already.
The heater was going hard from under the dash,
and the dash light was green and low,
and the wipers were doing what they could with the glass.
His gloves were on the seat beside him, one on the other,
and he moved them up onto the dash to make her room,
and there was no room.
Her shoulder was against the sideline jacket
and her knee was against his.
On the other side the trainer had put himself
as far into the door as a man could go, and it was not far.
She held her hands together in her lap and looked at the glass.
He had not looked at her since she got in,
and she could feel him not doing it.

He took the truck across the lot at a walk and out onto Millrow.
The road came up through the seat in a sound she did not know,
dry and steady, nothing like the RAV4's on this hill in the morning.
His hands stayed still on the wheel, both of them,
and the truck went down at the pace a man walks.
Nobody talked past the stadium.
The stadium went by dark, the poles off since December.
Below it the town was dark in a way it was not on a weeknight,
the square black under its own streetlights and nothing moving on it.
Boyd's blue chalk on the curbs below Delmar's was under the ice
somewhere, and would keep.

"7 did the twenty, Coach," the trainer said, halfway down,
to the windshield.
"Held the cap the whole way."

"All right," Dan said.

He did not look at her when he said it,
and his hands did not move on the wheel.
Aisha sat between the two of them with the number in her pocket
and heard her call reach him
through the one door she had left open for it.
That was the channel, and she was sitting in the middle of it
with her knee against his.
His face in the green off the dash was the shaved one from Odell's chair,
and the jaw was set.
He was pleased about something and keeping it off his face,
and he was not managing it.

"Where am I putting you," he said, to the trainer.

"Square's fine, Coach. I'll walk it from there. Knee's bad anyhow."

Nobody had anything to say to that,
and the trainer did not seem to want anything said.
He looked at the road and was done talking.

"You know where mine is, Coach," Aisha said, to the glass.

"I know where yours is."

At the bottom of the hill the truck came onto the square at a crawl,

The trainer got out, said "Coach," and "Doctor," and put his hood up.
He went off across the ice on foot at a walk that knew it,

Then it was the two of them on the bench with the door shut,
and the last minute to the Magnolia Court.
She moved over.
She did it before he had the truck rolling,
slid across the bench to the door the trainer had left,
and put her hands back in her lap.
A truck with two people in the middle of the seat
was a thing anybody could read from a window.
A truck with one on each side was a coach and a doctor and a ride.

"Doc," he said, once the truck was moving,

"You could have left me up there with the cot and the army blanket,"

"You'd have come across that lot at seven to see if I'd frozen."

"I'd have come across it at six."

She laughed at that, in his truck, on the ice,

The Magnolia Court's neon was on over the office, pink on the ice,

The space in front of nine was empty,

Her hand was flat on the bench between them
before she had decided a thing about it,

She looked at it there, her own hand, and left it.
He looked at the hand and then at her face, and held her eyes,
and his face in the green light was not a coach's.
He took his right hand off the wheel.
It came across the seat slow, because he meant it to,
and stopped an inch short of hers, and stayed there,

Then it came the rest of the way and went over hers, all of it,

Nobody could see the hand.
It was below the window line of a two-door truck in the dark,

The only person in the county with a window on it was Verna,

It was enough to make the cab a different room
from the one the trainer had been in.

"After signing day," he said. "That's February. Three weeks from tonight."

"I'm not getting out of this truck.

"I know the date," she said.
"You said it in my office with the door shut,

"Say it back to me anyway."

"February. Three weeks."

"That's the one," he said,
and his hand pressed hers once into the seat and let go.
It was the hand that went, not hers.

She took her hand back and opened the door,
and the cold came in over both of them.
She got down onto Verna's gravel without her hand on anything.
She did not say thank you for the ride,
because the ride was not the thing he had done.

Verna Poteat was at the office window with the light behind her
and a cup in her hand, not hiding and not waving.

on a night when nobody in the county was going anywhere.

"Roads are shut till Friday, my winter doctor.
County's going to sit and hate it.
Healing's the one thing in this county that won't hurry for football.
I've watched it try."

She shut the window.
She said nothing about the truck, then or after,

At the door of nine Aisha got the key in on the second try

Then it was him again.
