The roof of the Magnolia Court was letting go of its ice
a cupful at a time onto the gravel outside room nine.
Aisha Cole lay on top of the blanket in the dark with her boots on
and the phone lit in her hand, and listened to it fall.
This was Monday the eighteenth of January, and it was not six yet.
The thaw had come over the weekend, and the hill had been clear
since Saturday.
Another room on Grapevine, the small one that was not about football,
had gone through the parents' board's playoff album overnight
and found her laugh in it.
By six she had found it too, because that room had found it first.
A photograph of the doctor laughing with the coach,
at eleven on the morning she kept his quarterback out,
was the story the county had wanted since the fall.
By breakfast it would have it.

It was up under a local handle, with no name under it.

> **AshfordBornAndBred:** Somebody went through the whole album to find
> this one. Look at the time on it.

The picture was out of the thread pinned at the top of the parents'
board since seven on the nineteenth, *Playoff Pics — Post Here*.
There were four hundred pictures of the lots in it, and this was one.
A boy of eight or nine stood on an ice chest
with a poster-board sign over his head,
WE DROVE 11 HRS FOR THIS in marker.
Dan Merritt was leaned in toward the sign to read it.
She was at his shoulder in the parka with the cuffs pinned
and the lanyard out over it, laughing.
The lid of the ice chest, tipped under the kid's boots,
had thrown the sun back up into all three of them, one hard white flash.
The father's phone had caught the flash and the laugh together.
Under it the album showed the time the phone had put on it.
Eleven in the morning, the nineteenth of December.

She had laughed because the kid had wanted the sign
to say eleven hours and a speeding ticket,
and his mother had vetoed the ticket.
That was not in the picture. Nothing she had laughed at was in it.

The nineteenth was the game Trey sat out.
She had signed him out of it on the Thursday before,
two days ahead of that flash,
and the hold itself had been decided the Monday before that, and papered.
The county would read the laugh at eleven as the reason for a no
that was already on paper when she laughed.

She looked at the picture once, the way she looked at a scan.
It was the walk past him she remembered, and not the laugh.
She had gone by him toward the north stands, close,
and her arm in the parka had gone along his sleeve, wrist to shoulder.
The skin of that arm went warm now, in the dark, under the same nylon.
The warm did not stay in the arm.
It went across her shoulders and down into her chest and stayed there.
Her breath went short, and she could hear it in the room over the water.
Her heart came up, and she felt it in her thumb against the glass.
Her hands wanted a sleeve to hold and held the phone instead, still,
so they would not do anything else.
Her face was hot, and there was nobody in nine to see it.

She wanted him. The picture said so, and the picture was true.
The county was right about that one thing
and wrong about every single thing it had explained with it.
She had held Trey for the fourteen in December, on a bike, off a number,
and not for a man's shoulder in a parking lot.
She could not say that to anybody now,
because the picture said the other thing first,
and a picture was faster than a chart.

She read the post once. She did not read it twice.
She put the phone in the parka pocket and stood up in her boots.

The RAV4 was in front of nine, with the thaw's grit up its doors.
She had walked up Millrow for it on Saturday, when the hill came clear,
and driven it down herself.
At a quarter past six Verna's office light came on across the gravel
and lit the space in front of nine, and the space was full.
Verna Poteat wanted her winter's rent and no talk at her own door,
and this morning she had the first one.
She was a shape at the office window, looking at the car,
and then she was not.
Aisha went out into the sound of the water and started the engine.

Millrow had water running down both gutters at seven,
loud enough to hear with the windows up,
and the RAV4 went up it with the tires hissing.
The annex lot at the top had water standing in the ruts
and the survey crew's blue chalk showing bare again along the curb.
Across the lot, the Fieldhouse lot had his truck in it, nose to the fence.
She did not cross to it.
The terms had sixteen days in them.
A doctor who walked across that lot to his building this morning
would write the line under the picture herself,
and the county would only have to read it.
She parked at the warm end and went in.

The clinic was open at half past seven,
and the first thing in it was the tank running in the whirlpool room.
The trainer had a kid on the near table with an ankle up on a bolster,
and a roll of tape going around it, tearing, going around.
Wet boots had come down the corridor ahead of her
and left the floor shining.
Aisha came in with the tablet under her arm and the parka open.

"Doc," the trainer said, to the tape.
There was something under the word that was not the ankle.

The kid's phone was on the table by his hip, screen up, and it lit.
He turned it face down without looking at it, quick,
the way a kid covers a grade,
and the near table went quiet except for the tape.
That was how she knew the picture was in every phone in the building.

She kept her mouth easy and her eyes on the ankle,
and it cost her something in the jaw to hold it there.
She wanted to tell a nineteen-year-old with a bad ankle
that the picture was true and the rest was not, and did not.

"How's the ankle."

"Better than Saturday." The trainer tore the tape.
"He went down on the ice outside the dorm
and told nobody till it was purple.
Half the roster went down on that ice Saturday night.
He'll walk out on it."

"Can I go to class on it?" the kid said. He said it to the ceiling.

"You can go to class on it," Aisha said.
"You can't go in the weight room on it, and you can't run on it.
If it's purple again tomorrow, you come back up here
before you go anywhere else."

"Yes ma'am. Can I still sit in the film room?"

"You can sit anywhere you want. You can't stand on it."

"It's my left. I kick with my right."

"Then you'll kick in ten days."

The trainer wanted his job,
and he wanted to be on nobody's side of that lot,
and this morning both of those were on his face at the same time.
He had ridden down the hill on the door side of a bench seat
with her in the middle, and he had not forgotten it.

"Quiet room's empty till ten," he said, still to the tape.
"If you wanted it. I can keep the corridor down."

He was offering her a shut door on a morning she could have used one,
and he was not saying why, and he would not.

"The clinic's open," she said.
"I'll be at the desk with the door open."

"All right." He smoothed the last of the tape flat with his thumb.
"There's two more ankles off that ice coming at nine,
and a shoulder at eleven.
I'll send them down to you if they need sending."

"Send them."

At nine the two ankles came down the corridor on each other's shoulders,
and she did them at the desk with the door open.
Neither of them took his phone out while she had the ankle in her hands.
Both of them had it out before the door.

"Is it broke, Dr. Cole?" the first one said.

"It isn't broken. It's sprained,
and you're going to hate the next ten days.
Ice, up on something, and off it. Off it means off it."

"Coach is going to want—"

"Coach is going to want what I write down. Next."

At the one desk left in her office Trey's file was open on the tablet.
It was the record she had built Wednesday night with the ice coming down,
the way back through the hold.
She read the top line once.
Held from competition December 19.
The county had a picture now that said why, and the picture was wrong.
Every line under that top line was right,
and nobody was going to read the lines.
That was her hands' work since November, read as bought.
Bought for him.

Across the lot a door went, the Fieldhouse's side door,
and the sound came over the wet lot and off the annex wall.
She knew whose door it was.
She did not go to the window, and she typed the date on the file.


At noon she was still at the desk,
and the trainer came to her door with his phone in his hand
and the screen turned toward her and not toward himself.

"Doc. Somebody sent me this.
I didn't go looking for it,
and I'd sooner you saw it off me than off a kid on a table."

It was a screenshot of a screenshot.
The names at the top were cropped badly,
half of one and none of the other,
and the picture was whole, and the time under it was whole.
Under the picture was a sentence she had read once before, in December.

"Who sent it?"

"Doesn't matter who.
It came off a chat with forty people in it,
and it's on the next one by now."
He looked at it himself, once.
"I'm not on anybody's side of that lot, Doc.
I just didn't want you walking down that corridor at one
with everybody in the building knowing but you."

"I know it," she said. "All right."

"You want the warm-end door shut at one? They come in the side anyway."

"Leave it open. It's a clinic, and it's open."

"All right, Doc."

He went back up the corridor.
His boots were wet from the lot, and she heard them the whole way.

She opened the parents' board herself.
It was up under a real name, in daylight, with the picture and the time,
and it had three replies under it already.

> **Rhonda Sipes** December 19, 11 AM. If it's who I think it is, this

> **DossCoDadx3** Praying for everybody in this. That's all I'll say

>

> **B. Hollis** Whoever cropped that, crop the child out. He's eight.

Rhonda Sipes drove in from another state for every home game
and had wanted the hold explained since October.
At noon on a Monday she had signed her name under an explanation
and a boy on an ice chest.
That board did not put two adults in one sentence in plain text.
It was the board's own rule, and it had just broken in daylight.
By one the picture was on the kid's phone in the whirlpool room
off a different chat, with the names cut a different way.

Marisol Pruett moderated that board.
Her son Wes was the walk-on the same board had wanted off the roster
since the window opened in January.
The post about her friend was in her room, under her badge.
Marisol wanted her friend off that board and her son on that roster,
and the board was going to let her have one of them.
Nothing under Rhonda's name had gone gray.

Aisha typed one text with her thumb.
The thumb was not steady on the glass, and she sent it anyway.

*Leave it up. Don't spend your son on me.*

She held her breath for the sound the phone made when an answer came,
the way she held it for a number off a chest strap.
She let it go at twenty, and the phone had made no sound.
The post stayed up.
Marisol did not answer.
Both of those held the whole afternoon.
She would not have told anybody she had counted the cost first,
and sent it because the count came out with her paying
and not Marisol's boy.
The accusation stood under her name all afternoon while she worked,
and she carried it high in the shoulders, where a strap goes.


At three she went out the warm-end door for air,
and the water was still coming off the annex roof onto the step.
The survey crew was on the ground between the annex and the fence,
three men and a long tape,
with orange flags in the wet where the ice had been.
One of them called a number across the lot,
and at the edge of the lot a woman in a church coat wrote it down.
It was Delores Fountain-Mabry with a legal pad.
She was the woman who had counted the annex's forty-one boxes
into Mt. Nebo's back room with a pen and not her face.
The aldermen sat tomorrow night, the third Tuesday,
and she had come up the hill for the numbers.
She wanted the town's numbers to be the town's own.

"Doctor," Delores said, without looking up from the pad.

"Miss Delores. They're letting you write it down?"

"They call it out loud. Anybody can hear a number."
She wrote.
"Tomorrow night a man's going to stand up in front of the aldermen
with a drawing of this lot,
and I'd like to have my own numbers in my own hand when he does.
I've found a drawing will say what it's paid to say."

"Ninety-two on the fence line," the man with the tape called.

"Ninety-two," Delores said, and wrote it.

"Twenty-six to the post."

"Twenty-six."

"What are they measuring?" Aisha said.

"The lot, the building, and the fence line.
Everything a man might want to set a glass building on."
Delores turned the page on the pad.
"The last count I took up here was forty-one boxes, Doctor,
and it matched going in and going out.
I'd like this one to match too."

"Sixty-one and a half to the corner of the building."

"Sixty-one and a half."

"Watch that flag there, ma'am."

"I see it."

"We'll be up here till dark, ma'am. You can get the rest off the drawing."

"I'll get it off you."

"Do the aldermen want your numbers, Miss Delores?" Aisha said.

"The aldermen want to be home by nine. They'll get mine anyway."

She did not look at the phone in her coat pocket,
and she did not look at the one in Aisha's.

"That picture's on every phone at my church, Doctor.
I'll say that, and I'll say one more thing,
and then I'll get back to my numbers.
A photograph doesn't lie.
It just repeats whatever it's told."

"Forty on the short side," the man with the tape called.

"Forty," Delores said, and wrote it down,
and that was all she said about the picture.

Aisha stood on the step and did the arithmetic she did every day now.
The third of February was a Wednesday, sixteen days off,
and the class closed on it.
After that, the terms had said, they could go public.
The county had gone public for them at noon on a Monday
with sixteen days to run.
The trustees sat in February.
Her job was on their page under a politer name than her job,
the partner that rode in with the pledge and took over the team's doctors.
She had known that since December, from Dan.
The letter was never the thing.
Eleven polite families asking for clarity were a piece of paper.
A picture under a real name with the time on it
was the paper that let a polite agenda do it in one sitting.
Nobody at that table would have to say her name.
Sixteen days went through her low, under the ribs, once,
and she stood on the step until it had gone by.

Kat's text came at four with three listings in it
and a February in every one,
and *Honey* at the top, and not one word about a picture.
Kat had wanted her to stay since the coat.
Aisha read the three listings twice and answered none of them.


At nine at night in room nine the furnace was running behind again,
because the wind was north and the note on the blanket had said it would.
The water had quit coming off the roof at dark,
and now there was only the wind.
Outside the window the gravel had one car in it, hers.
Aisha sat on the bed in the parka with the light off
and the phone lit in both hands and the picture open on it.
The view count was under the picture.
It had gone up by two hundred since she had sat down.
The replies were still coming, small and polite, one every few minutes,
and each one made the phone go once against her palms, like a knock.
She was not alone in the room.
The county was in it, one number at a time.

Dan read the parents' board every night, every line of it,
down to the last reply.
She had told him once that he read it like it was a chart, and he did.
So the same picture was on his phone tonight.
He was up the hill in the Fieldhouse or down at his house,
and she did not know which building.
She did not know if he had opened it tonight.
She would know in the morning, off his face, across a lot,
and she would not cross the lot then either.
He had told her once what one true screenshot had cost him ten years ago.
This one was true too, and it had her in it.

She wanted him. Not the picture of him.
Him, here, with the ice gone out of his hair
and his coat on the back of the door.
Her mouth wanted his, and she felt it in the lip, plain as a bruise.
Her hands wanted the back of the bare hand that had come off the wheel,
and held the phone instead.
The whole length of her right side wanted his arm along it,
as the bench had put it there on Wednesday.
The side was cold without it, from the shoulder to the hip.
It sat under her breastbone and did not move.
Sixteen days had been possible this morning.
Now the county had said it for them, out loud,
under a real name, with a time on it.
Every one of the sixteen nights would be read like the picture.
A doctor seen anywhere near the coach now was the picture coming true.
She could not have him for sixteen days,
and she could not have him after them either
without the county saying it had been right all along.

She had let one person close, in a county that could read a parking space,
and it was costing her everything she had built.
The chart nobody would read now.
The record for the spring. The protocol with her name on the top line.
She had built all of it with nobody's help,
because nobody had ever spent anything on her, and she had never asked.
He would spend the job on this if she let him. She knew the man.
She would rather be the one who paid.

The phone went once more against her palms, and she did not look at the reply.
The picture was still open under it,
her laugh and his lean and the sign, with the time under all three.
The same picture was open on a phone in a building up the hill
or a house down it, in his hand or face down beside him.
Two phones, in two buildings, with the one picture on both,
and the county between them, reading.
