> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Tim Brasher:** Practice moved to five all week. If your boy is
> late to supper, that's why.
>
> **DeeAnn Prewitt:** Twelve days to the nineteenth. Who else is
> counting?
>
> **B. Hollis:** Signing week too. Busy building up there.


Monday of signing week, the week before playoff week,
and Rex Boyd's people were in the Fieldhouse by eight
with a projector and a stack of laminated boards.
The boards were the performance center,
three stories of glass Dan Merritt had liked out loud in this room,
the building that answered the one question film could not,
which was what a recruit saw when he walked the weight room
with the portal open on his phone.
Across the lot from the film-room window stood the annex,
where Aisha Cole had thirty years of paper to box
by a date on a memo he had read twice,
and the drawing on the boards wanted the annex's ground.
Trey Gault played on the nineteenth only if she signed a form
saying his brain had healed.
The county wanted to hear the word safe before it heard anything else.
And the man paying for the sunset in the glass
sat in the second row of Dan's film room and said nothing.

Boyd had the canvas jacket on and his hands in its pockets,
knees apart, already smiling,
and he had not said a word since he nodded at Dan and sat.
The athletic director stood against the back wall
with his phone in his hand and did not look at it.
The offensive coordinator leaned inside the open door,
arms crossed, same as the night in this room
when he had said whose money it was.
Every door open. That was still the rule,
and Dan had not thought until this morning
about who it let in.

Boyd's man ran the room.
He was a narrow man in his forties,
gray fleece vest over a checked shirt, glasses,
a lanyard from some other university turned backward on its cord,
and a clicker in his right hand that he never set down.
He had nine boards, and he had stood them in a row
along the marker tray of the whiteboard, where Dan's markers lived,
and the projector threw each one onto the screen
while he held the same one up in his left hand,
so that everything got said twice, once big and once laminated.

"Coach, you've seen the wall copy," he said.
"This is the same building with the numbers behind it."

"I've seen it."

The first board was the rendering.
The same sheet hung on the wall beside the depth chart,
where it had hung since the week the tube came,
and the corner Dan had pressed flat with his thumb that week
had curled up again.
He left it.

"The student-athlete experience is the product,"
the consultant said, and clicked.
"Three floors. Strength on one, recovery on two,
nutrition and the lounge on three.
The lobby is the sell.
Every visit starts in the lobby and ends in the lobby."

"How many kids does the lobby hold?" the coordinator said.

"It isn't built for holding.
It's built for the walk from the front door to the elevator.
Forty seconds, door to elevator."

The coordinator was still looking at the sunset.

"That's a rendering convention, by the way," the consultant said.
"We don't promise weather."

The second board was phases.
The third was the recovery floor,
and the consultant slowed down for it.
"Clinical-grade recovery suite.
Hydro, treatment rooms, imaging referral,
the whole return-to-play pathway under one roof
instead of across a parking lot."

"How many tables?" Dan said.

"Six. Two tanks, hot and cold, and an office with a window."

"Who runs it?" the coordinator said.

"The university staffs it. That's a later board."

Dan's phone lit under his hand on the table.
It had been lighting all weekend,
a mother in one area code and a high school coach in another,
and this week the phone was the class.
His first full one, coming in exactly as the card said it would.
He turned it face down without reading it.

The fourth board had three words on it and nothing else,
in type that reached the back wall.
*proactive risk posture.*
The consultant let the room read it.
"That's the posture the university adopts by building.
You'll see the phrase in the materials.
Forty-point, so it reads from the back of any room it's in."

Boyd said nothing.
The athletic director said nothing.
The coordinator uncrossed his arms and crossed them the other way.

Then the window.
The film room had one, on the lot side,
and Dan had never had a reason to look out of it with film on.
The consultant went to it with the clicker still in his hand,
held a thumb and a forefinger up against the glass,
a finger's width apart,
and put the annex between them.
It sat across the lot, low, one corridor of it,
the roofline dark under the practice field's light poles.

"Sight line from the lobby," he said.
"That's your ground.
The footprint starts at the near corner and runs to the fence."

The near corner.
Dan had walked that corridor end to end twice,
the cold end to the warm end where her office was,
and he knew what stood at the near corner,
because he had walked past its door with his cap in his hand.
The whirlpool room — the stainless tank, the two tables,
the parallel bars, the cot with the army blanket —
and past it the records room, and past that the water fountain
that ran cold or not at all.
The footprint started at her rehab rooms.
Not beside them. At them.
The lobby with the sunset in it went in over the whirlpool.
Hell.
He said it to himself once, plainly,
because there was no other honest way to say it,
and he said nothing out loud.
The building he had liked out loud in this room
went where she fixed people.

He sat still.
When Dan Merritt sat still, heads came up,
and nobody in the room this morning knew him well enough to read it.
The consultant looked over for the nod, and Dan gave him the nod,
and the clicker went on.

The last boards were money and a timeline
with no dates on it that anybody read aloud,
and then the consultant stacked all nine under his arm
in the order they had gone.

"None of this leaves the building yet," the athletic director said.

Boyd stood.
He came down the row of chairs with his hands still in his pockets
and stopped at Dan's.
He did not put a hand on it.

"Go sign your class, Coach," he said.
"I don't need a thing from you this week."

He said it at the volume he said everything,
and then he left a silence there beside the chair,
a good wide one, the kind a man was meant to fill with something.
Dan did not fill it.
Boyd nodded, as if that had been an answer too,
and went out through the open door,
and the consultant went after him with the boards,
and the athletic director after them both.

The coordinator stayed by the door.

"You still like it," he said.

"I said so in this room."

The coordinator looked at the curled corner of the rendering
a while longer and went to the projector,
and the first cutup came up, and the fan came on,
and Dan ran the visitors' base front for an hour
with his back to the window the whole time.

***

Halfway down the hall on his way to the phones,
the admissions director stood in a doorway
with a stack of folders against one hip,
in the Fieldhouse all week for signing,
and the receivers coach stood on the other side of the frame
holding out a coat.
The coat went over. Something got said, low,
and the admissions director laughed,
and the receivers coach stayed in the frame a second longer
than the handing took, and then went on toward the cage.
The door behind them was open. That was the rule.
There was no rule in the building against a hallway.
Two grown people easy with each other was nothing to think about,
and Dan did not think about it.
He had a class to sign.
He went on past both of them to his office.

***

Practice moved to five and stayed there through the nineteenth,
film first, then the field,
and the check ran at five fifteen on the turf, printed,
in his own typing, under lights that had come up to white by five.
The cold had held all week.
Breath stood up white out of every helmet on the field
and stayed where it was made.

Aisha came onto the turf from the annex side at five fifteen,
in the parka, and the first thing he saw was her hands.

They were out.
The cuffs were turned back at both wrists and pinned flat,
a row of pins at each, the heads catching the lights,
and her hands came out of the sleeves bare,
the tape on the left wrist, the tablet in one hand
and the sheet in the other,
and for the first time on this field they were out and staying out.
Her curls were tied back hard under the hood she had not put up.
Her breath stood white in front of her and did not go anywhere.

He knew the coat.
He had known it on her from the first morning,
because it had hung on his own hook the night before
with the tags still on it, his spare, the second of a pair,
and he had cut the tags off at his kitchen counter
and carried it to the cage before six
and asked for her name over the patch, straight, block letters,
and then let the cage tell her whatever the cage told her about boxes.
He had never told her.
She had never asked him.
And now another woman's hands had turned his cuffs back on her
and pinned them there in ten minutes,
and that was nearer her sleeves than he had ever been.

She held the sheet out and he took it.
One sheet, for him. The tablet was for the university.
Their breath went up between them into the same light.
The trainer was cutting tape a few yards off,
close enough to hear the coach and not the doctor,
same as every check since the first one.

"Thirty-four," she said. "Hamstring, low grade.
He came off the sled with it Friday.
He ran straight lines for me at four and did not favor it.
Limited. No cutting. The sleeve stays on."

"He'll want to cut."

"Then you'll tell him no."

"I'll tell him. How long on the sleeve?"

"Till I say."

"And Trey."

"Trey. Held overnight. Same as Friday.
The next step is when this one holds, and not before."

"His mother still driving him?"

"Every morning. She sends me the page at seven."

He read it. Held overnight.
The sheet said what she said. It always had.

"I'm not arguing."

"I noticed."

She did not move for the tablet.
Her hands stayed where they were, out, in the cold,
and the pins at her wrists were the brightest small things on the field.

"Did you ask?"

He knew what about.
The annex was behind her across the lot, low, dark at the cold end,
and he had spent the morning in a room
with every man who could have told him how it got on a list,
and the boards, and two fingers on a window,
and he had not asked one of them one thing.

"I'm working on it."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's signing week, Doc. I've been in rooms all day."

"You've had a few days."

"Give me the week."

She looked at the sheet in his hand and not at him.
When she said it, it was dry, at conversation volume,
and it was not a fight. It was the end of one.

"Keep the week, Coach.
Don't ask anybody on my account."

The trainer tore off a strip of tape and did not look over.
He had heard the coach ask for the week.
He had not heard the doctor hand it back.

"Doc," Dan said, and his voice came down to say it,
the size it was for one person.

"Coach."

That was all of it.
She went to thirty-four at the near hash with her own hands,
ran the hamstring through its length with the kid on his back,
watched his face instead of the leg,
and the pins went with her, catching every time she moved,
and Dan watched her hands instead of the drill
and let the coordinator run the period.
Thirty-four kept the sleeve on.
She was gone across the lot by five forty.

The trainer came by with the roll under his arm.

"Thirty-four's asking about the sleeve, Coach."

"It stays on."

He had had one thing she wanted from him
that was not a signature,
and he had spent it in front of a man cutting tape,
on the same soft sentence he had given her over a box
and given the county over a microphone.
She had not gotten angry.
She had taken the asking back, and thanked nobody,
and gone to work.

***

The Blind Mule on a weeknight this deep in the season
was the rail, the pool table, and the TV dark over the back bar,
where DEC 19 was still circled beside the register.
The pegboard mugs hung in their row of churches and bass tournaments,
and the jars stood clean under them,
and the brick that held the door open on a full night
sat inside it against the wall.
Peanut Kyzer set a beer on the wood in front of him
before Dan had the stool under him,
and went back down the pour line without a word about it.
Dan had not ordered.
Peanut had never needed him to.

Tick Moran was two stools down with a mug that said a church on it,
off the air and still in the headphones,
hung around his neck like he had forgotten them.

"Boyd's truck was in your lot this morning, Coach.
Whole morning. I drove by twice."

"Was it."

"Anything for the Table?"

"Practice is at five."

"That's not a story."

"It's the one I've got."

"I'd have the doctor back on, if she'd come."

"Ask her."

Tick waited to see if there was any more of that.
There was not. He went back to the mug.

Peanut came back down the rail with a towel
and wiped at a ring on the wood that was not there.

"You eat?"

"No."

Peanut reached two stools down, took Tick's basket of fries,
and set it in front of Dan.

"Hey," Tick said.

"House rule," Peanut said.
"Fries go to the man who hasn't eaten. Coach hasn't."

"Since when.
I've had a stool in this bar since the jukebox worked."

"Since I said it. Eat, Coach."

Peanut had heard the truck.
He looked down his own bar a while before he said anything,
the whole length of it, the empty stools and the pool table
and the one game going at it.

"Boyd doesn't buy buildings," he said.
"He buys the men who sign for them."

He set the bell straight where it sat on the rail
and moved off.

Dan had not signed anything.
He had sat in the second row and nodded,
and gone down the hall and worked the phones,
and told a woman in his coat to give him the week.
He ate Tick's fries and drank the beer Peanut had decided on.

He got his phone out and opened the board.
Monday's was short. Cold and supper and the letters.

> **DeeAnn Prewitt** Cold enough to see your breath from the car
> at five and they ran the whole thing. I sat there with the heat
> on and felt bad about it.
>
> **Kendra Voyles** For the ones asking about signing week: the
> families hear first, then the school posts. You will know when
> Coach knows.
>
> **Rhonda Sipes** Supper's at seven in this house now and I don't
> want to hear about it.

He read to the bottom. He read to the bottom every night.
Her name was not on it.
Not the doctor, not Dr. Cole, not the hold, not one word of her.
He got to the last post and understood
he had been reading for it the whole way down,
and that it was not there,
and that he had looked.
He put the phone in his coat.

***

The truck keys went toward the hook by the door,
and the hook already had a coat on it.
Navy, team issue, the staff patch on the chest
with nothing stitched over it.
The other one of the pair. The one he had kept.
It had hung there since the night he took the first one down,
and he had worn the sideline jacket every day since
and left this one where it was,
and had not thought about why until now,
with the keys in his hand.

The headset bag was by the door with the script stack squared inside.
The phone was on the charger across the room, face down.
He had read the board once tonight. He left it there.

He had told her he would ask.
He had sat all morning in a room
with the men who could have told him,
and a drawing that started at her whirlpool,
and he had nodded, and gone down the hall,
and at five fifteen he had asked her for the week,
and she had told him to keep it.
That was the thing he had not said, to her or to anybody,
and it would be there in the morning.

He hung the keys on the hook over the coat's collar,
where they had hung all fall,
and turned off the lamp.
