> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Rhonda Sipes:** Clear bags only at the gate Saturday, for everybody
> driving in. Ask me how I know. Then go buy one.


The printer under the window in Dan Merritt's office
pulled Tuesday's practice schedule through at ten to seven
and laid it in the tray warm,
one page, four periods, the minutes down the left margin,
and he read it standing, with the coffee going cold on the desk.
The first-team line had the backup's name on it.
He had typed that at six, sitting here in the dark,
a kid who had gotten one live rep a day behind 7 since August
put down for all of them through Friday,
and he was not going to explain it to anybody,
including himself, past this: the kid could use three days.
Under it, five fifteen, the doctor's half hour, in his own typing,
and that was the line his eye went back to,
which it did every morning now,
and he had quit telling himself that was about the roster.
It was Tuesday, the fifteenth. Ashford played in four days.
Trey Gault was a student today and nothing else,
with Monday's headache supposed to stay gone all day and all night
before anybody so much as showed him the bike again.
Nobody had said out. Friday was the day for that word, if it came.
The county was pricing Saturday in its own money, rooms and a December,
and Dan priced it in his,
a building announced on a field if they won,
and his own name on an agenda in February either way.

Monday's sheet was in the sideline jacket on the hook,
inside pocket, where he had put it on the turf. Stepped down.
Everything else he had, she had said to him in the film room
a foot off with the imaging up.
A student today. It holds tonight. The bike Wednesday at the earliest.
He had called the athletic director Monday night
with the two words off the sheet,
because a coach tells his boss what the doctor's paper says,
and had gotten one sentence back that he was still carrying.
Denny Gault had gotten his five nights off her Saturday night.
Dan had been in her office when she promised them to Missy's page,
and the board since had had no number in Denny's hand on it.
Quiet in the good way, for once.

He printed the staff copies and took them down the hall
to the table in the staff room,
and the coordinator came in behind him with the install on a clipboard
and picked the top one up.
He read the first-team line, and looked at Dan, and read it again.

"I've got the morning," he said.

"You've got it. I'm across the lot."

The coordinator had the install till noon.
Dan had the lot, because at six,
with the backup's name on the screen and nothing else decided,
he had decided that.
He took the truck keys and left the coffee.

***

The F-150 stood backed up to the annex's outside door
at the cold end of the building, by the records room,
at a quarter past nine, with the tailgate down and the bed empty,
and Dan had one hand on the tailgate when the door opened.
Aisha came out in the parka with the cuffs pinned and the tablet
under her arm, and looked at the truck, and then at him.

"Because boxes," he said.

She looked at the empty bed a while.

"They go to Mt. Nebo's fellowship hall," she said.
"There's a room off the back of it with a door that locks,
and Delores has the key.
Verna and Delores worked that out between them on the phone
and told me after,
which is the only way I'd have taken it."

"I didn't know that."

"You weren't asked either."
She held the door with her hip.
"Facilities' mailbox is still full.
The corridor's two boxes high and I've had a clinic running in it
every morning from nine. They go today, Coach."

The records room was colder than the corridor.
The four cabinets stood open and empty to the bottom drawers,
and what had been in them stood along the wall in boxes,
taped shut, a number on every one in black marker.
Aisha Cole had written every number in her own hand
before he got there.
The brown sleeves of film stayed on their shelf,
and the carton that said KNEES, 1994 stayed on the floor under them,
and she did not have to tell him those were not going anywhere.
She stood with the tablet and typed before anything moved.

"Every box number, the hall, the date, my initials.
Nothing gets on that truck that isn't on here first."

"Understood."

"Sealed ones only. If the tape's up, it stays in this room."

He picked up box one.
She let him carry it.
She had labeled every one of them ahead of him,
and when he lifted it she did not stop him and did not take it back,
and that was the first time.

They did the records room in forty minutes,
him to the truck and her behind him with the next one,
and the bed took them two high and then three,
and he had brought straps for that and a tarp for the top,
because it was paper and it was December.
Then the corridor, from the records room end.
Past the whirlpool room, where the turbine was running by ten
and a kid sat on the edge of the tank with one leg in it
and a walking boot on the floor beside him,
and watched them go by and said nothing.
The trainer went past with a roll of tape under his arm,
said, "Coach," and kept walking.
The clinic was open around them the whole time.
Nobody in it asked what the coach was doing
with thirty years of the doctor's paper on a Tuesday in playoff week.
The values had split on this once, at her desk, over a box.
By the twentieth box it was two people and a corridor
and the same reach for the same carton,
his hand and hers on it at once, and one of them letting go.
It was work. He was good at work.

Inside, with the cabinets empty and eleven boxes left along the wall,
he set the one he had down on the stack instead of carrying it out,
and she looked up from the tablet.

"I'm going to tell you a thing," he said,
"and I'd rather you had it from me in the right order
than off that board in the wrong one.
Ten years ago I was an assistant at another program.
My wife and I were separated. That was first.
The board's had it the other way around for ten years
and I've let it, because fixing it means saying the rest.
There was a woman on the same staff.
She was the one who said who played.
She and I were together that fall, and we were careful,
and careful doesn't matter.
She cleared one of mine. A kid who'd been out two weeks.
She cleared him because he was ready, and he played,
and somebody asked why he was ready that week and not the one before.
The county had an answer for that. The answer was the two of us.
There was a screenshot. There's always a screenshot.
The board had it a week before anybody said it to my face.
The school sat me down a year.
That's the part that traveled. That's the part that's fond now."

He picked the box back up, because his hands wanted something.

"I got a season. She got a different life.
The rule's got her face on it, not mine."

The turbine ran down the corridor. She had not moved.

"You told me on a porch I read that board like a chart
and it's the weather," he said.
"I never answered you. That's the answer.
I read it to see the weather coming,
because the one time it counted, I didn't.
Every door open, every room two adults deep, every minute on film.
That isn't Compliance. That's me,
so nothing I do ever needs explaining to anybody after.
And I've been standing a foot off you in a film room
with the door open for two weeks, and I'm scared of it.
Not in general, Doc. In the specific.
In four days you're going to sign a thing or not sign it,
and either way somebody in this county is going to ask why,
and I already know what the answer's going to be,
because I've heard it once. It was us then. It'd be us now."

He should have stopped there. A man like him stopped there.

"Here's the rest, since I'm standing here.
Win Saturday and Boyd's building goes up on the video board
at the final whistle, the rendering,
with the suite full of the people who paid for it.
That's what a win is to them. It's the stage.
The trustees meet in February and my extension's on the agenda.
Win and it's a formality.
Go out in the first round with Trey sitting and it's a review.
I called the athletic director Monday night
with your two words off the sheet, and what came back was,
'I've got a board meeting in February and a donor who counts doors.'
That's my side. Yours is shorter.
If you sign and he goes down, you're done.
If you don't and we lose, you're the reason.
I know the math."

He had the box against his chest and no reason left to be talking.

"And I'd have stood a foot off you anyway. I liked it.
I'd have told nobody that."

Then he stopped.

She did not say anything.
The turbine ran. Down the corridor a door opened and shut.
The fountain ran a second and quit, the way it did.
She stood with the tablet against her chest
and her curls come half loose from whatever had held them at nine,
two hours of lifting in a cold room having undone it,
and she did not fill the quiet.
Not with a question. Not with anything of her own handed back.
He had known she would not. He had said it anyway.
It went on long enough that he heard his own breathing in it,
and then longer than that,
and he stood in it with a box and let it be as long as it was.
Then she looked down at the tablet and typed a line.

"Missy's page came at seven," she said.
"The headache was gone by nine last night.
He slept nine hours. Zero this morning.
That's today's whole record, so far."
She typed another line.
"And the Gaults have booked a second opinion. Birmingham.
After the game.
Missy put it at the bottom, under the hours,
like it was one more thing she'd written down."

"Birmingham."

"After the game," she said, and gave it nothing else.

He carried the box out. He carried the next nine.
She came behind him with the tablet and typed a number
every time one went over the tailgate,
and by noon the tarp was on and the straps were on
and the corridor was a corridor again, with a fountain in it.
The last one was on the floor of the records room by itself.
He had it off the floor and against his chest
when she put her hand on his forearm.
On the bare part, below where the sleeve had ridden up.
It was on purpose.
There was no box to steady and no glare to fix
and nothing on his arm she needed.
She put it there to put it there.
Her hand was cold, cold from two hours in this room,
and it had weight, more than a hand that size had any business having,
and his arm was warm under it from the lifting,
and that was the whole of what he got.
Her hand, the one he had watched across a sheet since June,
on him, because she meant it to be.
He did not put his own hand over it. He had the box.
He did not move, either.
She took the hand back and picked up the tablet.

"Forty-one," she said. "The hall. The fifteenth."
She typed her initials and turned the screen so he could read them.
"Now it can move."

***

Mt. Nebo Missionary Baptist's fellowship hall ran the length
of the church's back side, a long plain room
with a kitchen pass-through at one end and a door at the other,
and at ten past one Dan came into it with box one in his arms
and Delores Fountain-Mabry standing inside the door
with a key on a ribbon and a legal pad.
Aisha had followed the truck over in the RAV4 and parked beside it,
and that was the whole of the trip.

"Coach," Delores said. "Doctor.
The door on the end is the one. It locks, and the key stays with me.
I'll want a count going in that matches the count going out,
so say the number as you pass me."

"Where do you want them, Miss Delores?"

"Along the wall in there, in the order they come.
One."

"One."

She wrote it.
He said the numbers going by and she wrote them,
and Aisha came behind him with the lighter ones and the tablet,
and the room on the end had been a Sunday-school room once
and had folding chairs stacked in it and nothing else,
and the boxes went along the wall in order
because she had numbered them in order
and there was no other way left to put them.
Delores had the aldermen tonight, the third Tuesday,
and she counted the boxes with the pen and not with her face,
the count she would take of that room tonight.

Around thirty she said it to the pad, not to either of them.

"This hall was a note at the bank for ten years.
We paid it a Sunday at a time,
and we paid every Sunday of it straight.
Ten years is long enough to pay a thing, if you paid it honest.
Pay it any other way and you'll still be paying when they bury you."
She wrote thirty. "Thirty-one, Coach."

"Thirty-one."

She did not know what he had said in a cold room that morning.
She was talking about a building whose money she had counted.
He carried thirty-one down the hall and set it on thirty.

At forty-one she read the pad back, top to bottom,
and Aisha read the tablet, and the numbers matched,
and Delores locked the door on the end herself and tried the handle.
Then she looked at the two of them,
standing in her hall with nothing in their hands,
and whatever she made of that, she kept,
and put the key in her pocket.

"Doctor, you get a copy of this," she said. "Nobody else does."

***

At five fifteen on the practice turf the lights were up white,
the team was in drills at the far end,
and Tuesday's sheet was in Dan's hand,
one page off the annex printer, still curled from her pocket,
with the trainer cutting tape at the near hash, in earshot of him.
Two names on it.
Under Trey Gault's, one word.
Student.

"Tonight's the part," she said.

"Missy's page."

"Seven. I'll have it before you do."

"You'll have it before anybody."

At the far end the coordinator's whistle went and the period changed.
He had given her ten years in a cold room this morning,
the whole of it in the right order, and the stakes on top of it,
and what he had back was one cold hand on his arm
and a count of forty-one that matched.
It was not an even trade. He had not made it to be one.
She had told him nothing, and he had known going in
that the chart did not volunteer, and he had said it anyway,
and standing here with her one word on a sheet
he could not have said he wanted any of it back.
He folded the sheet once and put it inside the jacket with Monday's.
She went down the near hash to thirty-four.
Down there the backup took the first snap of the period
with the first team, and Dan watched his feet.
