---

The cold had been living in the stadium's concrete all week,
and by noon on Saturday it had nowhere left to sit, so it climbed —
up through the aluminum benches,
up through ninety thousand pairs of boots,
up into the breath that hung over the bowl in one slow silver sheet.
Kickoff was half past two.
The flags stood straight out from their poles and stayed there.

Aisha Cole had walked the emergency plan at eleven,
the way she had walked it before every home Saturday since June.
Cart staged at the northeast tunnel.
Ambulance backed in under the north stands, keys in it, crew fed.
Spine board on the fence behind the bench.
Airway kit hung where her hand could find it without her eyes.
Her own car sat under the stands too, nose out,
because of days exactly like the one she was paid to hope never came.
Back at the Magnolia Court, Verna had set a traffic cone
behind her bumper at dawn, before the game traffic came.
"You're coned off," was all she said about it.

Her radio clicked.
"Booth to Cole. Clean feed up here, both twenty-twos in frame."

"Copy," she said. "If you catch me watching the ball, correct me."

At the staff gate Sonny Dillard was working the clipboard in two coats.
"Gonna win it for us today, Doc? I wore the lucky shirt.
Can't see it under all this, but it knows."

She held up her credential, and he waved her through.

Ninety thousand people had come to watch the ball.
She was paid to watch everything the ball left behind —
the getting up, mostly.
The getting up, and the wrong kind of stillness.
She had every baseline on the roster on her tablet,
and she knew the roster backwards — by histories and neck strength
and who the GPS vests said was running on empty by Thursday.
It was a strange way to love a game, watching it backwards.
It was hers.

***

The first half took two hours
and gave neither county a thing to sing about.
By the fourth quarter the light was gone off the field,
the cold had turned serious,
every huddle showed its breath,
and the men along the chain-link behind her bench
had quit saying rivalry and started saying bracket.
Visitors sixteen, Ashford thirteen.
On Thursday night, at the fire, the county had told itself
that one Saturday plays for the whole year here.
It was getting its Saturday. Nobody had priced it yet.

Twice in the third quarter
she had been out and back for ordinary trouble.
A cramp worked loose.
A chin laid open on a facemask, closed with four stitches,
its owner back in by the next series.
The kind of afternoon her hands knew by heart.

Third and eight, near midfield, under six minutes.
The seven took the snap and looked left,
and the pocket went bad from the blind side, all at once,
and he climbed up into it and threw the ball away,
and the hit arrived after the ball was gone.
It was not the worst hit of the afternoon.
She had seen worse by two-forty.
It was the second half of it —
the back of the helmet reaching the turf
a half-count after the rest of him.

Flags came down. The bowl booed the flags.

The play stood a long way from finished.
The ball had died out of bounds,
the officials were walking the penalty off,
and ninety thousand people watched the flag,
which was the wrong thing.

He got up slow.
He got up slow the way tired boys get up slow,
one knee, both hands on the knee, helmet down,
and the booing rolled on over him,
and then he sat back down in the middle of it all,
neat, like a man who had decided the grass was fine.

Getting up slow is football.
Sitting back down is information.

Her radio clicked.
"Booth to Cole. Stay on seven. I don't like the get-up."

"Neither do I," she said, and she was over the rope.

The head trainer got there when she did
and took the helmet in both hands without being asked.
Her voice went flat. She trusted it flat;
flat was the sound of her doing her job.

"Hands still. You're all right to be still.
Look at me. Good. Stay with my voice."

She asked him where he was.
He answered her.
The answer took its time coming, and part of it came twice.
He asked her what happened.
She told him, plain and short.
She worked her fingers down the back of his neck
through the collar, asking as she went,
and the answers to that, at least, were the right ones.
Around them the stadium had gone quiet in rings,
the near sections first,
the quiet spreading up and out until the whole bowl stood
with its programs still.

Up in the press box the PA said there was an injury on the field,
and asked for patience, and did not say a number.
Ninety thousand people said it for him, low, to themselves.

They brought him up in stages, a trainer under each arm,
and the bowl clapped him toward the sideline
the way a county claps when it is asking for something back.

The replay board did not show the play again.
You could feel the whole bowl wanting it to,
wanting one more look so it could call the hit harmless.
The board gave them a graphic about the bracket instead.
The tent closed behind them.

***

Gray nylon, a cot, her flat voice,
and the flap's zipper pulled all the way down.
She wrote 6:10 on the tape on her wrist, and under it, *seated*.

She asked what field this was. What half. Who had scored last.
The tablet took his answers down; the tape took the times.
Then he asked her what happened.
He had already asked her that, out on the grass.
She answered it the same way, in the same words,
and wrote down the time.

Her radio: "Booth to Cole. Replay's full view, helmet to turf.
On your tablet now."

"Copy. Time-stamp it and save the clip."

Above her the stadium bargained with God and the replay board,
in that order, in waves she could hear through the nylon.
Somebody's air horn. Somebody's trombone.

He reported the headache at 6:15.
At 6:22 it was worse,
and she wrote both times on the tape,
and those two lines were all the argument
an ambulance would ever need.
The tent's job was smaller and harder than a diagnosis —
out, or not out. He was out.
The rest belonged to imaging, and to time,
and to nobody's schedule,
and she already knew what that was going to cost everybody,
and she charted it anyway, in order, in her own hand.

Merritt came to the flap with his headset down around his neck.
He backed the sideline off the tent with his arms spread,
put himself between the flap and the TV truck's long lens,
a wall of windbreaker with its back to her,
and then put his head through the gap and nothing else of himself.

"Doc."

"He's out. No return today.
That's not a judgment call, it's the rule."

"I know the rule."
He looked once at the shape on the cot and once at her hands.
"What do you need?"

"The northeast tunnel held clear in about five minutes.
And the crowd not told more than it needs.
The ambulance runs quiet because I asked,
not because it's nothing."

"Done," he said, and was gone,
and through the gap she heard him already calling for the backup,
voice level,
a man putting his half of Saturday back in motion
because her half had left the schedule.
He had fought her on every call she had made in this county.
This one he handed over whole.
She did not have time to wonder what that had cost him.

The paramedics came in through the back with the stretcher.
"We're set when you are, ma'am."
The facemask came off with the little driver
she kept clipped to her zipper,
and the bolts went into her coat pocket
because there is never anywhere else.
Helmet on, collar on, straps, blanket.
He went out the back of the tent a jersey number and a protocol,
and that was the kindest shape she could give him for the cameras.

She would not have said this part to a single soul in the county:
her hands had been sure the whole time,
her voice had stayed flat the whole time,
and the eleven minutes under that gray nylon
were the first eleven minutes since June
she had not felt like a visitor here.

***

Behind her, through the nylon,
Ashford sent out the backup to run the reduced package —
the package a scout-team walk-on
had run at the first defense all week —
and the first snap of it gained nine.

She walked the stretcher to the dock under the stands,
one hand on the rail of it,
and the crew loaded him the way she had drilled it in August,
no wasted hands.
"No lights through town," she told the driver.
"He's had enough soundtrack."
The ambulance pulled out with no siren and no lights,
nothing showing but headlights,
one more set of them leaving a ballgame.

The roar came down through the concrete twice while they loaded,
once long, once enormous,
and she did not need a radio
to know what the county had gotten for its Saturday.

Her car started on the second try.
She pulled out behind the ambulance
with the heater loud and the radio louder.

The town was already pouring onto the square
when they came around it,
coats and flags and car horns tuning up,
everybody's breath rising together under the strung lights.
On WDSS, Tick Moran was hoarse past usefulness,
playing the winning call over again between phone calls,
and the ambulance parted all of it without a sound,
riding dark at walking speed, headlights and nothing else,
and she followed in the lane it opened,
both hands high on the wheel.
The celebration went quiet
in a moving circle around the ambulance,
hats coming off in a ring that traveled with it,
and closed up loud again behind her car
like water behind a boat.

At the hospital she gave report the way she wrote charts,
in order, without adjectives,
and stayed while they pulled his August baseline,
because handing that off was the last job that was hers.
After that the night belonged to the hospital,
and the hospital was good at it.
She left a little after eleven.
Porch lights were on all over town,
and the square, three streets over, was still at it.

***

The office light at the Magnolia Court was on
when she pulled in a little before midnight,
and the radio inside it was on low,
running the fourth quarter over again.
The lot was parked solid,
her coned-off space the one gap in it.
Verna Poteat had the storm door open
before Aisha was out of the car.

"There's coffee if you can stand what's left of it," Verna said,
holding the door with her hip. "You eat anything today?"

"Not yet."

"Mm." She pressed a foil-wrapped plate into Aisha's hands
and thumbed the foil down where it had lifted.
"Delores sent plates over for whoever the day ran long on.
You count twice." She frowned out at the lot.
"Game folks parked on my grass clean up to the ice machine again.
One day a year I let it go."

"How's the boy."

"I can't say."

"Can't or won't."

"Both."

Inside the office the radio played the last call one more time.
Verna looked at it and did not turn it up or down.

"They've been playing that boy's touchdown all night," she said.
"I kept it on for the score and then couldn't turn it loose.
But I'll tell you what I told the Lord at my kitchen table,
whole fourth quarter, all that hollering —
that's somebody's boy under that tent."

"He is," Aisha said.

"Your room went at four o'clock," Verna said.
"Game rates, game people, same as every home Saturday.
You're in the back tonight."
She tipped her head at the door behind the desk. "Go on."

The back room off the office smelled like the heater working,
and the radio came through the wall at the volume of company.
Her suitcases had made the trip without her,
stacked square on the luggage stand,
and Verna's note sat on top of them, one line in ballpoint.
*Heat's been on since supper.*
The plate went on top of the suitcases, next to the note,
because the back room had no table either,
and because she had never asked for one.

She set the kit by the door, latched, handle out.
She put her phone face-up on the bedspread
with the ringer all the way up.
From the square, thin against the cold,
the county was still singing.

She sat down on the end of the made bed
and did not turn on the light.
Her hand went into her coat pocket for warmth
and found the facemask bolts, four of them, small and cold,
and she closed her fist around them and left it closed.

The phone stayed quiet.
She kept her coat on.
