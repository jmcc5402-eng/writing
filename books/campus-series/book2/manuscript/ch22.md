> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**

The Fieldhouse's side door had frozen shut in the night,
and Dan Merritt put his shoulder into it from the inside.
It went on the second try with a crack like a bat on a cold ball,
and the sound crossed the lot and came back off the annex wall.
Nothing else out there was making a sound.
"Damn door," he said, to the lot,
and the words hung white in front of him and went.
It was Thursday the fourteenth of January, the storm's second day,
and the college was shut through Friday on the radio's word.
He had slept in his office chair,
because the building was open and the town was not,
and the truck had done that road twice already.
The portal window shut tonight with the quarterback he had flipped in it,
and that was the first clock of the day.

The rain had quit after midnight.
He had heard it stop from the chair,
the tick on the office window going to nothing.
Nothing was falling now.
What had fallen was on everything, a glaze and not snow,
and the lot from the door to the fence was one sheet of it.
Up the hill toward the House a limb came down under its load
and hit the ground, and that was the loudest thing in the county.

He went across the lot on foot,
because nothing with wheels was going anywhere.
His boots did not crunch. They squeaked, high, rubber on glass,
and he walked flat-footed to keep from going down.
The RAV4 was where the storm had left it at the annex's warm end,
nosed up to Aisha's door with the ice on it a finger thick.
Nobody had been up or down the hill since him,
and it was glass to the first curve.
He had come back up it before seven
with the middle of the bench still warm where she had sat.
He put the flat of his glove on her driver's window
and pushed the ice off it, a hand's width, and then the rest of the glass.
When the thaw came, it would come to her side first.
He would not have told anybody that was why.

The film room was the warm room,
because Dan had left the heat on and every door in the building open,
which was his rule.
The phone was on the table under the whiteboard,
and nobody else was in the building.
Every clock Dan had was on that phone,
and he said them over once, in order, the way he said a play sheet.
The portal window shut tonight.
The agent's number had sent two words at seven, *Still in*,
and the father had not called since Friday.
That was not a yes from the father and it was not a no.
The class closed on the third of February, a Wednesday, twenty days off,
and the two families who had gone quiet in the fall were still quiet.
In February the trustees sat down to a page
with Boyd's pledge on it and Dan's extension on the line under it,
in that order, unless something re-sequenced them.
That was every clock he had, and none of them was hers.

Ty Beaumont had texted at half past six, before the door.
The room on Grapevine that was not the parents' board
had a line up about rides home from the facility, and did Coach want to know.
Ty had a truck payment, and this job was what made it.
That room had had the coach and the doctor in one sentence since the fall.
This morning the sentence had a truck in it.
One truck in the county had gone up and down that hill last night,
and it had two doors.
Dan had driven her down a hill nobody else could drive,
with the trainer on the door side, and it had taken ten minutes.
The county had spent the ten minutes by breakfast.
Dan had known the rate at the top of the hill and driven it anyway.

Dan had read the parents' board at six, before any of that.
Mothers two and three states off were on it about the ice,
one with a picture of eggs and one with a car under a limb.
He had gone down it once for anything about the class,
and there was nothing.


At noon he was still in the film room,
with the January roster on the table and the phone on top of it.
The building had nobody else in it.
The letter's follow-up finished on the parents' board
while he ate a bar out of his pocket.
It was the same out-of-state handle as Friday's, and it was short.

> Update for the eleven families. Copies of our letter to the athletic
> director go to the trustees and to Compliance at eight tomorrow
> morning, by email, since the campus is shut through Friday. The
> receipts folder is attached. Public record only: this board's own
> threads, the "medical determination" note from the fall, the
> December practice sheet with the first-team line, and the dates of
> the hold. Respectful and factual, same as the letter.
>
> **Rhonda Sipes:** Thank you. Some of us have been asking since
> October.

Nothing under it was gray.
Marisol moderated that board and had cut nothing from the thread.

The letter itself was not news.
It had sat on the athletic director's desk since the Monday after Christmas.
Eleven polite families were not much at a program this size,
and on its own the letter could have sat on that desk till it was old.
What was new was who got a copy at eight tomorrow,
and what the paper was for.
It was for February.
In February the trustees sat down to three things on one page.
Boyd's pledge was one, and Dan's extension was one.
The third was the group that rode in with the pledge
and took over team physician services. That was her job.
Dan had known it since October and had told her in December.
A letter asking for clarity on medical decisions
was the paper that let them hand her job to the group as a process fix.
It could go through in the same sitting as the building and his contract,
with nobody's fingerprints on it.
Every trustee who sat down to that page in February
would have opened this email first, with the receipts attached.
A pledge sequenced ahead of a coach's line could go behind it
if the medical situation embarrassed the program first.
Boyd had said that to Dan's face on Friday,
and this was the email that made it a live thing.

The medical decisions were her.
They were her in a quiet room with a boy on a bike, watching a screen,
and a no in December that had held.
The letter asked for clarity on her hands' work,
and he could not say that to the trustees or to the board.
He was not going to say it to her either, because the rule was hers.
He put the phone face down on the roster
and ran January's depth chart on the whiteboard until the window went gray.


At half past five Dan drove down Millrow inside his own tracks from last night,
because they were still the only tracks on it.
Tick Moran had called the office at one.
The Table was on at seven, ice or no ice,
because the county was home with the radio on.
It was the second Table ever held out of a season.
The hill had held its ice all day, and the low town had not.
Where the sun had got to the square by afternoon,
the brick was wet and the gutters ran.
The north side under the storefronts was still white in the shade.
Dan put the truck at the corner of the square,
where a man could park in winter, and killed the engine.

Aisha Cole was crossing the square.
She was coming from the Checkerboard's side toward Verna's,
in the parka with the hood down and her curls out in the wet air.
Her boots on the brick were the only sound on the square.
His breath stopped in the cold. He had not told it to.
His hand stayed on the truck's door where it had been to shut it.
The storefronts were dark and the streetlights were on over nothing,
and under the far one she looked across at him.
It was one look, her face turned to him and held,
and then turned back toward Verna's.
His stomach dropped like a missed step in the dark,
and his hand shut on the cold of the door.
She did not slow down.
He did not lift a hand, and neither did she.
Anybody at a window would have seen a woman walking home
and a man with his keys,
and nobody at a window could have read it.

He did not cross the square to her, and he knew why.
The terms had twenty days in them, three weeks less a day.
This morning the county had put her in his truck.
A coach who walked across the square to her tonight,
with the town at its windows,
would give the county its next line under the letter.

Ten years ago it had been a year.
One screenshot, a true one, and the school sat him down a year,
and a man could come back from a year. He had.
A second time was not a year.
A second time was the end of college coaching, anywhere, for good,
and the county's windows were the screenshot now.
That was the choice standing on the square tonight.
Her, or the job.
He stood at the truck until she was under Verna's office light,
and that was the one thing he let himself have.

Delmar's was the first light at the foot of Millrow, and he walked it.
Ray's front steps had been chipped clean with a shovel blade.
The porch heaters were on and just starting to throw heat,
and the smoker was going at the yard end with the lid down.
The porch was thin.
Twenty people stood on it where December had had a full porch,
because of the roads, and the rest of the county was at home with 1340 on.
Tick was at the card table in a coat zipped to the chin,
one headphone pushed back off his ear, and the mixer hummed.
Rex Boyd stood at Ray's serving window with a paper plate,
eating ribs and talking about ribs with Ray through the window, smiling.
That was Dan's stretch of wall, and Boyd had it.
Denny Gault had the northeast corner and had it to himself.

"Cold's good for the bark," Ray was saying through the window.
"That's the crust. It takes it slow in the cold."

Boyd's money for the glass building was on the February agenda,
and he had come to eat ribs on the block it was going to sit on.
Dan let Boyd keep the wall and took the guest chair early.

"You eating, Coach?" Ray said, with a plate out the window.

Dan shook his head, and Ray set it back on the pass.
Tick looked up from the mixer with one hand on the headphone.

"Twenty on the porch and the whole county at home, Coach," Tick said.
"Best crowd I ever had."


At seven the station sent its tone,
and the bulb over the card table came on.
Tick leaned in to the mic.

"Coach's Table, from Delmar's porch,
on a night the county's got no business being on a porch," Tick said.
"We're thin out here and we're loud at home, and I'll take it.
I've got a coach, a phone line, and Ray,
and Ray's got a smoker that never heard about the ice.
Call the number. You've had it since you were twelve."

The porch laughed, twenty people's worth,
and the first line was already blinking.
The caller was a man out on the county road,
on a phone that made him sound farther off than he was.

"Tick, who's plowing out here?
I've had my nose on the window since yesterday
and nothing's come by but a dog."

"Nobody, and nobody's going to," Tick said.
"Stay home till it goes. It'll go. Coach, tell him."

"Stay home," Dan said. "The hill's still glass. Don't try it."

The second caller was a woman,
and there were kids in the room with her.

"Coach, signing day. Is the third still the third, with everything?"

"It's the third," Dan said.

"That's a date, friends, not a diagnosis," Tick said.
"He'll give you the one and not the other, and you know it by now."

The third caller was a mother.
She was polite from the first word.

"I'm one of the families,
and I'll put this to the Table and not to the Coach,
since the Coach has his lane and I respect it.
We'd just like some clarity
on how the medical decisions are being—"

"I'll take that one," Dan said, and she had not finished it.

Tick's hand came off the fader. It stayed off.

"I'll take that one, and you didn't ask me for it,
so put it on me and not the Table.
This program has the best team physician in this league.
I'd say that at any table in any state, and I've sat at a few.
We follow her protocol. It's written down, and it's hers.
In December she looked at 7's numbers and said no,
and I backed the no, and it held.
Since then it's been a step at a time.
One step, a night on it, and then the next, and she has never jumped one.
There was a check this month.
I wasn't in the room for it, because it's her room and not mine.
I know it happened. I don't know the number, and I'm not supposed to.
That's how the medical decisions get made here, ma'am.
The doctor makes them. I don't. Nobody on this porch does.
That's the clarity."

He had not said her name.
The county heard it anyway, at home with the radio on.

Nobody on the porch said anything about it.
The porch went quiet, twenty people at once,
and a fork stopped on a plate at the rail and did not start again.
Ray's tongs stopped on the grate at the serving window, and started.
Boyd, at the window, went on eating.
Denny, in the corner, did not turn around.
Tick looked at Dan once over the top of the mixer with both hands still.
Then he put a hand back on the fader and took the next call.

The next was a mother two states off, from the sound of her.
She was not calling about football.

"Is the college still shut tomorrow?
I've got one down there who won't get up for a text,
and I'd like to know if I should keep sending them."

"Through Friday, ma'am," Tick said.
"Let him sleep. It's the one thing they're good at."

She laughed on the line,
and it came through the porch speaker small and pleased.
Tick went to the feed store's spot while she was still laughing.

"Doss Farm and Feed, friends, since 1946 and open in the morning,
ice or no ice. Sonny Dillard's on the counter,
and he doesn't know how to not open it.
Chicks come in March. You can ask him. He'll tell you the date."

Then he took two more.
A man wanted to know if the stadium had power.

"It's got power and nobody in it," Tick said. "Like the rest of us."

A woman's dog would not go out on the ice.
At eight the tone came down the line again.

"That's the Table," Tick said.
"The county's home tonight, and so are we. Stay off that hill."


The bulb over the card table went off.
Off the air the porch was small, twenty people and a smoker,
and it sounded like it.
Ray scraped the grate, and the coats came off the rail.

Dan got up out of the guest chair and did not go to the wall.
He went along the rail to the northeast corner instead,
where Denny stood with a plate he had not eaten off of,
and Dan stood there with him.
From that corner a man could see Verna's office light up the hill,
and she was behind it somewhere, and Dan kept his eyes on the yard.
Nobody had stood in that corner with Denny since December.
The porch had seen it.
Denny wanted the doctor gone and had blamed her for the boy's fall
since the day the boy went down, and Dan did not like the man for it.
The boy was not the letter, so Dan asked.

"How's Trey," Dan said. That was all he asked.

Denny looked at the yard and not at him.
His coat was buttoned to the top.
"He's all right."

That was all of it,
from a man who in December had told this corner everything he knew.
Dan waited.

"He's on the bike," Denny said, after a while.
"His mother drives him up. I'm at work.
He wants a field, Coach. That's all he wants.
It's all I ever wanted for him."

"I know it."

Denny's name was not on the letter the eleven had signed,
and Dan did not ask why.
He said nothing about any letter,
and he let the quiet and the missing name lie where they were.
They stood in the corner a while, the two of them, under the heater.
Then Denny put the plate in the can and went down the steps,
and a door shut out in the yard and an engine started.

Boyd put his empty plate in the can by Ray's window
and did not come down the rail.
"Ray tells me a cold night's good for a smoker, Coach," he said from there.
He said it no louder than he said anything.
The smile was the man whose money wanted her rooms for the glass building
and her job for the group that rode in with the pledge.
Boyd had come down the hill on ice to hear the coach say her protocol
on the air. He had heard it, and that was what the line about the smoker was.

Dan said nothing to it, and Boyd had not waited for anything.
He went down the yard steps without slipping and was gone.

Tick had the headphones down around his neck
and a loop of cable over one shoulder.
He had two beers by the necks in one hand,
and he put one of them on the rail in front of Dan and kept the other.
Then he stood there, low, for Dan and not the porch.

"Signing day's the third," Tick said.
"I'll run it on the air like a parade, because that's my job,
and I'm good at it.
But it's a holiday for a thing that already happened.
Most things are, by the time this town gets around to celebrating them.
The class was done in December. You know that better than the porch does.
I'm telling you so you know I know it."

Dan drank one swallow of the beer and held the rest.
The van door slid shut out in the dark, and then the engine,
and the headlights came on and went down toward the square, slow,
away from the hill.
He left the beer on the rail, most of it,
and went down Ray's chipped steps.


He sat in the truck at the corner of the square with the engine off
until the cold came in through the doors and found him.
At ten he walked across to the Checkerboard.
It was open, because it was open at every hour there was
and the Phams had never shut it for weather.
The cook was alone with the griddle and kept his back to the room.
Sonny Dillard, the feed store's counter by day
and the pumper when the pager went,
was on a stool down the counter in half his gear and off a call.
Dan had the stool nearest the register, his,
and a glass of water, and nothing else.
The second stool was empty.
Dan's phone was on the counter, face down, by the water.

"Slice is a morning item, Coach," the cook said, to the griddle.

"Make an exception."

"I made one for the ice. That was the exception."

Sonny laughed, once, into his eggs, and did not look up.
Dan had nothing to put back on it, and he let it lie there.

"Refroze at dark," Sonny said, to the eggs. "Square'll be worse by six.
I'd like to eat these and get home before the pager goes again.
That's all I'm after tonight."

Nobody said anything back to that. Sonny went on eating.

For ten years the board had been the last thing Dan read before he slept,
every line of it, down to the last reply.
At his last job the board had had him figured out before the school did.
He did not open it.
It was the first night in ten years.
The porch had seen him stand in Denny's corner.
The county had heard him say her protocol on the radio
the same day the room had said rides home.
Her name was in a sentence on that board by now, under his.
He did not have to read it to know its shape,
or what a second one would cost him.
He was the man who checked the verdict every night
so it could not blindside him.
Tonight he let it, on purpose,
with the phone face down by a glass of water.

The next time Aisha looked at him she would see what tonight had cost,
because she missed nothing on a face,
and then she would pay it too.
If he read it, it was on his face in the morning.
If he did not read it, it was not on his face,
and she did not have to carry what he had not read.
He would not have said to anybody
why he had put the truck at that corner of the square and not the other one.
It was the corner on the side she had come from,
and he had got the one look he had parked for.
Twenty days was too long.
He felt it low in his stomach, like a meal he had skipped,
and the water did nothing for it.

The copies went at eight.
The third of February was twenty days, and the trustees sat in February.
Sonny finished his eggs and pushed the plate an inch and did not get up.
The cook turned the griddle off,
and it ticked behind him while the metal gave up its heat.
Nobody was on the stool beside him, and nobody would be at this hour.
In the morning she would have it, and he would be up the hill by then,
the way the weekdays went.
He left it empty, and the phone where it was.
The second stool sat there beside him with nobody on it.
