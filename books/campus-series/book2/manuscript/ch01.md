# Chapter 1 — Bonfire Night

On the Thursday night before the rivalry game,
Ashford set itself on fire, on purpose, with a permit.

Aisha Cole had the first-aid table at the north edge of the burn
line — a folding table with a red cross in duct tape on the front,
her kit squared into rows only she would ever grade.
Gauze by size. Cold packs she did not trust.
The nonstick burn dressings at the right-hand edge,
where her hand could find them with her eyes shut.
The table's steel found her wrists through both sleeves.
On the fire side of her body the air was August;
on the other side it was the river.
Her breath showed when she counted supplies,
so she counted them again to watch it.

She signed the empty clipboard herself
so the clipboard would not be empty.

Sonny Dillard came down the rope line in turnout gear,
checking the stakes with his boot,
a fire lieutenant's slow inspection-parade walk.
"Hundred ten feet of rope," he said,
"and every kid in Doss County leaning on it.
Only fire in the county with a permit and a waiting list."

The joke landed on nobody, and he walked the rope on down.

The Checkerboard had a card table two steps from hers,
pie under a cake dome, a coffee urn doing steady business,
and a hand-lettered sign taped to the front:
COACH'S SLICE, $4, and under that, smaller, QUARTER OF A PIE.

"Named for Coach Merritt?" Aisha asked.

"Named for his order," said the woman at the urn.
"Man asks for a quarter of a pie cut as one piece
like it's a normal thing to say."

"And he orders it often?"

"Often enough. We quit fighting it and put it on a sign."

Sonny circled back with Earlene Tatum and a thermos in tow.
"Earlene, you know the new doctor? Dr. Cole,
out at the college. Earlene sells the eggs."

"We met in June," Earlene said. "At the eggs."

"Well, now you've met official," Sonny said, satisfied,
and went to stand with his engine.

The first customer of the night arrived at seven forty,
airborne, sideways, in the arms of a den mother
towing four more Scouts like a string of ducklings.
The boy was eight, maybe nine, uniform shirt over his coat,
not crying so much as leaking,
his right hand held out from his body,
carried careful, like a full cup.

"Marshmallow," the den mother said. "Off the stick,
onto the web of his thumb. Don't ask me how."

Marshmallow was worse than flame —
sugar stuck, and kept cooking where it stuck.

Aisha had the water jug uncapped by the second sob.

"Hold it right there for me. Just like that. You're doing it."
She poured a thin steady rope of water over the thumb,
tilted so it ran and did not pool.
"Water first. Water's the whole treatment.
We pour till it quits cooking.
You're holding nice and still."
Her voice stayed at chart-reading pitch.
Her hands did everything else —
turned his wrist as if it were the last one in the county,
thumbed the sleeve back dry, kept the pour going
long past the point anybody watching thought was the point.
The sugar went from lava to shell.
She lifted it off in one patient piece and did not rub.

Somebody's grandmother appeared at her elbow
with butter on a saucer, already unwrapped.

"That's kind," Aisha said, "and butter holds the heat in.
Water takes it out."

"Butter's what my mother did."

"Mine too. We've all been lied to by good women."

The grandmother laughed.
"I'm keeping my saucer. And I'll supervise."
She did both, which was correct of her.

A blister was rising, small, honest, intact.
"Blister stays," Aisha told the den mother.
"It's the best dressing he'll ever own. We don't pop it,
we don't peel it, we cover it loose."
Nonstick pad, two turns of gauze, a thumbhole.
"You'll have this ruined by morning," she told the boy,
and probably he would.
"Ibuprofen tonight, dosed to his weight, it's on the label.
If the blister opens, or it's redder tomorrow than it is now,
bring him to the clinic and ask for me. No charge on a Thursday.
Give me your number and I'll send the follow-up link."
She sent it while they stood there.

The boy inspected the bandage, then her, then the bandage,
and signed off on the work with one grave nod.

"They told me you were good," the den mother said.
"They did not tell me you were this good. Thank you, doctor."

"He held still. Holding still is most of it."

"I'm buying you a slice," the den mother said.

"That's not necessary."

"The regular kind," the den mother told the woman at the urn,
as if Aisha had not spoken,
and she set the slice on the first-aid table
where it steamed for a minute in the cold
and then quit.

Aisha typed the note into the clinic app one-thumbed —
time, mechanism, intervention, disposition —
out of a habit a decade older than the app.
Somewhere behind her the pallets shifted
and the fire ate its way to the middle of the stack
and stood up taller than the courthouse.

A good burn call, she thought. Water, patience,
and the sense not to litigate 1962 with a grandmother.
If the whole county could be run off a folding table,
she would never lose an argument in this town again.

***

The lull came at eight, the fire too big now for marshmallows,
the crowd backed to the rope and glazed gold, faces only.
Verna arrived at the table in a coat with a fur collar
older than the interstate.
"Come out where they can see you," she said, took Aisha's arm,
and presented her to the fire like a debutante.
"Earlene. Have you met my winter doctor?
I rent her the quiet end."

"Good to meet you," Aisha said.

"Twice now," Earlene said, eyes on the flames.

Aisha let it stand. That made three people this fall
who had introduced her to somebody she already knew,
and she had corrected none of them,
because new came with a season of grace and six-months-here
came with questions about how you were settling in.
Being brand-new three times in one fall
was the most company she'd had since June,
and she would have denied that under oath.

She got out her phone.
Verna Poteat had added her to the parents' board in June,
unasked, "so you'll know things before they know you,"
and Aisha had never posted, not one word.
She read it like a river gauge.
Tonight the board was running its rivalry thread,
four hundred comments and climbing.

> **FridayNightFaye:** Bonfire is LIT (at 7, by professionals,
> calm down everyone). Band comes down Millrow at 8:30.
> Get there early if you want to smell like victory all weekend.
>
> **AshfordBornAndBred:** New families!! Tonight's bonfire is
> where Cal Sutter first kissed our Marisol back when they were
> in school. In the rain. Donna's cousin was standing right
> there. That's what you're part of now 🧡
>
> **GrammyInSectionC:** It was raining. I remember the rain.
>
> **PieBeforeKickoff:** Ok since it's rivalry week: has anybody
> else noticed Coach Merritt's Wikipedia just SKIPS seven
> seasons? The Wikipedia years. Theories below, keep it kind.
>
> **DossCoDadx3:** Asked him about it at the fish fry once.
> He said "long story" and complimented my wife's beans.
> Never felt more handled in my life.
>
> **GrammyInSectionC:** He was a monk. You can tell by the
> posture.

Aisha had read that page herself in June,
the week she took the job —
you read up on the man whose roster you are about to guard.
Seven seasons, not there. A gap was a question,
and questions were not hers to answer on a parents' board.

She tilted the phone toward the fur collar.
"Is the kiss true?"

"It's how we tell it," Verna said.

She found she liked the town better for that, not worse,
and she put the phone in her pocket
and let the county keep its rain.

Down the rope a man in a rivalry shirt was hollering,
"Forty points and a running clock!"
and his friends hollered "Forty points!" back at him,
and the noise went straight up with the sparks.
Earlene poured her thermos cup half full and did not offer it.

"One Saturday plays for the whole year in this county,"
Earlene said, to the fire more than to anybody.
"Go on and win it."

***

The band came before it came —
low brass arriving through the shop glass down Millrow,
a shiver in the hardware store's windows,
then the drumline turning the corner and the whole square's sound
doubling, a house's sound when the family gets home.
On the hardware store's roof a row of lawn chairs
had a cooler open against the rail,
grown men wrapped in stadium blankets they'd deny owning,
passing something warm.

The flatbed was parked at the courthouse steps,
the width of the square away from her table and then some —
her at the right end of the county, the rope between,
Tick Moran up on the truck with a microphone
and that hot-tube AM voice you could have poured on pancakes,
live on WDSS, working the crowd's dial from the middle out.

Then Merritt came up onto the flatbed
and the crowd's verdict came back off the storefronts at volume.

Aisha watched him take it.
Or rather she watched him not take it —
he stood still in a coat with a whistle lanyard showing,
let the roar spend itself down to embers,
and then made the microphone look like Tick's idea.
He said maybe forty words. She caught half at that distance.
He named his seniors and stepped back for each name
so the applause had somewhere to land that wasn't him.
He pointed at the fire crew and started that clapping himself,
and Sonny's whole engine company stood there
getting famous against their will.
When the chant started, one syllable with his name in it,
he turned it with one hand, flat palm, patting the air,
until it came apart and reassembled around the team instead,
and the crowd never felt the steering.

That was triage. She knew triage at any distance.
Take the heat off the wound.
Keep both hands where the family can see them.
Give the room back its own noise.
She ran the same protocol at every hard bedside of her life,
and she had never watched a man run it off a flatbed
with a whole county for the patient.

The burn table sat idle for every minute of it.
She watched anyway,
until the slice went cold on the table beside her.
Professional interest, she'd have said, if anybody had asked.
Nobody asked.

The band played the fight song twice and the alma mater once,
and the fire came down by degrees into a red country
the fire department began to wet at the edges,
steam standing off the coals and drifting through the crowd,
woodsmoke and wet ash and somebody's dropped cocoa.
Families peeled off into the dark by porchfuls.
The Scout went by on his mother's shoulder
and held up the bandaged thumb to her, high,
a trophy over the crowd,
and Aisha raised the cold pie in salute
and watched them out of sight.

She packed the kit the way she'd built it, rows in reverse,
and signed herself out on a clipboard
that had her name on it twice and nobody else's all night.

***

The Magnolia Court's neon was on when she got in,
VACANCY dark, the pink script lying out across the gravel lot
and up the doors of the sleeping trucks.
Verna's office glowed at the end like a lit aquarium,
the television in it talking to the empty chairs.
A grocery sack hung on her doorknob.

Her room was the quiet end, and the room had a system.
The near suitcase lived open on the luggage rack
and gave up each day's clothes without complaint.
The far suitcase sat by the window, latched,
and had sat there latched since June.
She could have this whole room in the truck in nine minutes.
She had timed it in June, and once more in October,
to be sure the number still held.
The heater ticked and caught.
The bathroom glass wore its paper cap.
Six months, and the room still squared away daily,
a fact of the room she noticed
as a number on a monitor gets noticed,
and moved past.

Not bad, she thought, for a town that had met her twice.

In the sack was a wool blanket, heavy,
smelling of cedar, brushed clean,
and a note in pencil on the back of a receipt.

*Furnace in 9 runs behind when the wind comes north.
Use the blanket. Don't fight the dial. — V.P.*

The blanket was the warmest thing in the room.
She spread it over the bedspread.

Her phone lit up once on the nightstand,
the den mother reporting in.
Three exclamation points,
and a photograph of the Scout asleep in full uniform,
bandaged thumb held to his chest like a medal.

She showered the smoke out of her hair,
and it was still there anyway when the light went off —
in the wool of her coat, hung alone on the closet rod,
the whole square's worth of woodsmoke
keeping the bonfire going in the dark, in the one thing
in that room that smelled like the county.
Under Verna's blanket the cedar held its own against the smoke,
and the far suitcase sat shut beneath the window
with the neon lying pink across the lid.
