Dan Merritt's right knee had been going under the table
since the reviewer sat down,
and he put his palm flat on it to make it quit.
The independent review of Aisha Cole's December call
had him in on Monday the twenty-fifth to answer for his side of it,
and by Thursday he would know exactly what he had said to it.
Its findings were due on Wednesday the third.
They would say whether her no on Trey in December
had been good medicine or a playoff thrown away,
and the county had been waiting since before Christmas
to be told which one it was.

[TK who conducts the review; the room and its building — on no page]

The university's lawyer sat at his left,
the man in the gray suit who had shut Dan's office door on the twentieth,
and he wanted nothing said that did not have to be said.
The reviewer wanted a true answer to every question,
and that was a fair thing to want.

The first half hour was dates,
the fourteen minutes on the bike in December
and the form with her whole name on it.
Dan gave every date right,
and the whole time he was standing at the RAV4's window in the rain.
Four inches of glass had come down, and her face was in them,
with the vent's air moving her curls inside the hood.
His throat shut on it now, at a table with a reviewer across it,
and he drank from a paper cup of water to get it open again.

That morning his knuckles had brushed the coat on the hook by his door,
the mate of hers,
and he had stood in his own hall with the keys in his fist
a good while longer than keys took.
Some of him was still standing there when the reviewer turned a page.

"Coach, one more, and then we're done," the reviewer said.
"Knowing what you know now,
would you have played your quarterback on the nineteenth?"

Dan's heart went once, hard, against his ribs.
He heard a football question, because he had heard them his whole life,
and he answered it as the coach he had been every fall before this one,
with his mind on a car window.

"Every coach in the country plays him," Dan said. "I'd have played him."

There was a second half, and Dan had it ready.
He opened his mouth on it, and the reviewer had already looked up, finished,
and the lawyer had put one hand flat on the table between them.
Dan's jaw closed.
The rest of it stayed where it was.

"That's all we need, Coach," the reviewer said, and shut the laptop.

The hall was cold after that room,
and Dan could feel his pulse in the palm that had held his knee.
Something had gone wrong at that table, and he did not know what yet.

"The review holds its hearing on Monday the first," the lawyer said.
"You're not on its list, Coach, and that's good.
Whatever comes to you on the drive home, don't add to it.
Anything you add makes it worse for her."

"I left something off," Dan said.

"Everybody leaves something off. Leave it."


Three days later, on Thursday the twenty-eighth,
the athletic director had Dan in at eight and did not ask him to sit.
He wanted a quiet February, and he said so first,
tapping the edge of his phone on the desk.

"The group comes in July, Coach," he said.
"Whatever the review finds, her line goes to them either way.
So you tell me why I'd spend a season
defending a line that's gone by July."

Her line was her job, a line in his budget with her name on it.
Dan had heard the shape of that sentence before,
from a man in his own office door who would not sit down.
A program with a partner coming in July could let its doctor go in January
without anybody ever calling it a firing,
and every trustee at that table in February would call it housekeeping.

"One more thing." The athletic director put the phone face down.
"A department out west called me Tuesday for a reference on her.
They've offered her a directorship,
and they want an answer by Saturday the sixth.
I gave her a good one. It was true."

"What did she tell them?"

"They didn't say, and I didn't ask."

[TK the western school; its chair — unnamed on every page]

Dan's hands were in his coat pockets by then.
The right one found the truck keys
and shut on them until the teeth bit into his palm.
Heat came up the back of his neck,
and his chest went hollow under the coat.
She had not told him, and he had not given her one reason to.
He did not know her answer.
His own read was that she would take it,
and nobody could have blamed her.

He had not called her since the radio had it Tuesday,
because the lawyer said anything he said about her made it worse for her.
A head job in this league was the chair he had wanted
since he was old enough to draw a play on a napkin,
and a man only kept it by winning it back every week
from men who wanted it as badly as he once had.

His staff's jobs rode on his extension.
If the trustees let it go in February, the coordinator went with it,
and Ty went with his truck payment,
and so did every man on that staff
who had come here because Dan asked him to.

And if the county caught a coach and his team physician together
in the season, the school let them both go the same day.
He would do what the lawyer said.
He would say nothing through the hearing and nothing after it,
and every day he said nothing, the county would have his eleven words
about her and not one other word from him to set beside them.
He knew what that cost her.
It cost her the only man who had ever said her protocol on the air,
and he let it cost.


At eleven the trainer called up from the annex.
A lineman had turned an ankle off the squat rack in the weight room,
and a head coach went to look at his players' ankles himself,
so Dan went across the lot and in at the warm-end door.
It was Aisha's door. He had come in by it at night in December.
The man the department had sent to cover her clinic
was in the whirlpool room with the tank off, on his phone.
Down the corridor her office door was shut.
Dan looked at it once, and his chest went tight,
and he went into the rehab room.

The kid was on the near table with his shoe off and his sock rolled down,
and the trainer had the foot up at a right angle,
tearing the tape off the roll in short hard strips.

"Hold his toes up, Coach, if you're going to stand there," the trainer said.

Dan held them. It gave his hands something to do.

"Whit, I got a test at one," the kid said. "Can I walk on it?"

"You'll walk on it. You'll walk slow."
Whit smoothed a strip down with his thumb.
"My girl's a sophomore here, same as you.
She says they give every test in this school on a Thursday."

The trainer was Carl Whitlow, and the staff called him Whit.
Dan had never asked him one thing about his life.
Whit wanted that girl through this school
and his own job still standing after whatever came through the annex in July,
and he wanted nobody looking at him while he got both of them done.
Dan saw a man there for the first time,
a father taping another man's boy.

"Luck to your girl on her Thursday, Whit," Dan said at the door.

Whit's face went red to the ears,
and he looked at the tape and not at Dan.

On the way out Dan stood on her step, where she used to come out for air.
Last week there had been orange flags on the ground
between the annex and the fence, and today there were stakes.
They were wood with orange paint on the tops, driven in hard,
in a line from the near corner of the annex where the whirlpool room was
all the way out to the fence.
The flags had marked the ground,
and the stakes marked where the building would stand.
The glass building would go up on top of her whirlpool room,
and in February the trustees would hand her job
to the group that came in behind it.
His breath went out white,
and the cold got in under his collar and down into his chest.
He wanted her out on the step beside him with her arms folded,
telling him exactly what she thought of that line in the ground.


At five Dan was down the hill in the chair at Fountain's on Fountain Row,
Odell Fountain's barbershop, with the cape to his chin.
Signing day was Wednesday,
and a head coach stood up in front of the county with his hair cut.
A man in a work jacket waited on the bench by the window
with a boy of about ten beside him.
The boy watched Dan in the mirror with his mouth open.

"That's the coach," the boy said.

"I know who it is." The father found Dan's eyes in the mirror.
"No offense, Coach. I heard the radio Tuesday.
You'd have played 7, and I believe you would.
The doctor was right, though.
My boy plays peewee, and I want the one who'd sit him."

He wanted a doctor who would sit his own boy,
and he had said so out loud to the one man in the county
whose words the radio had read out at half past seven Tuesday.

Dan had the whole answer, and a cape to his chin, and he said nothing.
His throat worked on it and let it go.
His neck went hot under Odell's hand.

Odell shut off the clippers and wiped them on the towel over his shoulder.
He wanted the coach to hear this one.

"Nineteen heads in this chair since that radio Tuesday," Odell said.
"Nineteen men, and every one of them could tell me what you think, Coach.
Not one of them has heard a word out of the doctor.
Nineteen to nothing. Head down."

Nineteen to nothing, and all nineteen of them were his.


At half past six Dan walked from Fountain Row to Delmar's
at the foot of Millrow and took the guest chair early.
Tick Moran had called a Table for that night,
the Thursday before signing day,
because the class signed Wednesday and he wanted the county
to hear it from the coach before it heard it anywhere else.
It was the third Table ever held out of a season.
The lawyer had cleared Dan for it on the phone,
for football and the class and nothing else.
The heaters were on, and Ray's smoker was going at the yard end.

Tick had his headphones on and the mixer humming.
He wanted a good hour of radio and a porch that went home warm.
At seven the bulb came on over the card table.

"Coach's Table, friends," Tick said.
"No game till fall, and here we are anyway."

For forty minutes it was the class,
the two families still to sign in February,
and a mother two states off whose son was on the roster
and would not wear a real coat.

"Tell him to wear a coat, Coach," she said.

"Wear a coat, son," Dan said. "It's January."

Nobody on the porch laughed, and Tick took the next line.

At ten to eight Tick leaned in to the mic,
and his voice did not change at all.

"Coach, this station read something of yours Tuesday morning," Tick said.
"The county heard it.
If you want to put anything beside it, the air's yours."

The porch went quiet. Ray's tongs stopped on the grate.

On the fourteenth, from this same chair,
Dan had said her protocol into that microphone.
He had not said her name, and the county had heard it anyway.
Tonight it would hear the gap where her name had been.
His throat had closed, and his heart was going in it.
He wanted to say her name so badly that his hands ached from holding still.

"I'm here for the class, Tick," Dan said.

"Then we'll do the class."
Tick's hand went back to the fader,
and he took the next call as if it were any other.

Dan let it stand.
He let his eleven words about her stand in every kitchen in the county,
and his heart knocked the whole time he did it.
He had made that choice in the morning, and out loud it was worse.


After eight Dan drove the truck across the bottom of the square
to the Magnolia Court, the motel where Aisha kept room nine by the month,
because he had heard about the offer from his boss and not from her.
He put the truck at the far end by the ice machine, nose out.
Verna Poteat was at her office window when his lights came across the gravel.
She looked at the truck once and let the blind down.
She wanted her tenant kept, and his truck was no help with that.
This same truck in that gravel after dark had been a post once already,
and he had driven it in anyway.

The RAV4 sat in front of nine with its back seat full.
Verna's office light came through the side glass and lay across the top box,
and on the box lay the parka, folded flat with the name up.
This was her answer, packed where the county could read it.
His breath went out of him and did not come back right.

The lamp in nine was off and the curtain was open.
None of it was for him.

He knocked with one knuckle, twice.
Aisha opened the door with her hand still on the knob.
Her curls were tied back,
and her eyes went to his face and then past him to the truck.
Her mouth went flat.

"Doc," Dan said.

"You can't be in this gravel, Dan."

She looked at Verna's window, and then she stepped back from the door.
A coach on her step under Verna's light was worse than a coach
behind a shut door, and she knew it before he did.

"Get in here."

He came in, and she shut the door behind him and stayed against it,
with her back to the inside of it and the gravel on the other side.
The room was stripped to a made bed, a bare rod,
and a can of sparkling water on the sill.
She looked smaller in it than he remembered.

"Monday, at the review," Dan said. "What I said to them, Doc.
I want you to know what I—"

"Don't." Her eyes came up to his and held, bright and angry,
and she did not blink.
"Don't hand it to me at night like a present.
If there's a second half to that sentence, it isn't mine.
It belongs to the people who wrote down the first half."
Her hands had found the knob behind her back and held it.
"And you're not going to give it to them, Dan. I know you're not.
I don't want you to."

"I heard about the job out west this morning.
From my boss, not from you."

Her eyes went to the bare rod and back.
"They asked for Saturday the sixth," she said. "I keep my dates.
Keep quiet, Dan. Sign what they put in front of you in February.
I never unpacked. You've always known that.
Let me be what it costs you, and call it cheap."

Her chin was up, and her eyes had gone wet at the rims,
and she let him see it.

"No." It came out of him too loud, and he brought it down.
"If somebody pays for Monday, it ought to be me.
Whatever it costs, I'll take it. It's mine to pay, Doc.
I'd rather it came out of my hide than yours."

"It's not yours to spend. Not on me." She shook her head once.
"Nobody's ever spent a thing on me, Dan,
and I'm not starting with your job."

Neither of them took the other's way out.
His hands did not know where to go.
He put one on the doorframe by her shoulder,
and then he felt about fourteen.
She was angry with him and she was looking at his mouth,
and he could not make those two things sit still together.
She looked at his hand on the frame
and then back up at his face, clear-eyed and furious.
For a long second neither of them moved.
Then her hand came up off the knob and closed on his coat at the chest,
not pulling, only holding on.
He bent his head slowly, and she did not turn hers away.
He felt her breath on his mouth before he felt anything else.

Then she kissed him, or he kissed her, and it did not matter which.
Her back went against the door, and the whole length of her came against him,
and the heat of her went through his shirt and his open coat
as if neither one were there.
His heart slammed up into his throat and kept on slamming.
His free hand found her waist over the sweater and held there,
and he could feel her breathing hard under his palm.
Her fingers went into the short hair at the back of his neck,
where Odell had cut it that afternoon,
and the skin under them went hot and stayed hot all the way down his spine.
He could not get his breath, and he did not want it.
She kissed him hard and angry, and he could taste both.
His knees had gone loose under him.
He had come to say one thing and had not said it,
and now he had her against a door with the thing still unsaid,
and he wanted her more than he had wanted anything in his life.

She ended it.
Her fist opened on his coat and her palm went flat on his chest,
and she pushed him back an inch, not hard.
He went back the inch.
Her mouth was wet and her eyes were wet, and she did not wipe either one.

"Go on, Dan," she said. "Go on home."

He looked at her face a long time,
the wet eyes and the loose curl at her temple.
Then she opened the door behind her, and the cold came in off the gravel.
He went out past her, and the door shut behind him.

He sat in the truck at the mouth of Verna's lot
with the engine running and the heater on his hands.
The lawyer said don't.
If Dan stood up in front of the review on Monday the first,
the extension was gone in February,
and the chair he had wanted since the napkin went with it.
His staff went with it, every man who had come here for him.
He was afraid of that.
He was more afraid of her car on the interstate on the morning of the sixth,
with the parka on the top box,
and the county still believing the eleven words he had given it.
She had said the second half belonged to the people
who wrote down the first, and she was right.

He could still feel her fist against his chest,
and the back of his neck was hot where her fingers had been.
His chest ached with it.
The lamp in nine had not come on.
At the road he put the turn signal on, and it was the wrong side for home.
It ticked in the dark cab, loud as a clock,
and he sat through six of them with his foot on the brake.
Then he turned the wheel away from home, the signal still ticking in the dark.
