# Chapter 27 — The Hearing

POV: Aisha, third person limited, past tense.

> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Paula Kinnard:** My daughter asked for a small care package. I sent
> socks, granola bars, and a note. Her verdict: socks good, granola good,
> note "a lot." The note was four sentences.


The top button of the good jacket would not go through its hole,
and Aisha Cole stood in the hallway outside the trustees' boardroom
working at it with fingers the cold had made clumsy.
It went through on the third try.
Behind the wall the independent review would sit this morning
to decide whether a doctor had been doing her job
when she kept Trey Gault off the field
for the biggest home game this town ever had.
It was Monday, the first of February.
The findings would come in writing on Wednesday the third,
and if they went against her the university could let her go for cause,
two words a doctor carried into every job after.

It was the only good jacket she had brought to Ashford,
and until this morning it had never been off the rod in room nine.
The wool coat was still on the rod.
The parka was folded on the top box in the back of the RAV4,
and the RAV4 sat loaded in Verna's gravel, where it was staying.
A loaded car at this curb would have told the county more
than anything anybody said inside all morning.
So Verna had driven her in and let her out at the curb.
Verna had not come in.

"I'll be back when they let you go," Verna had said,
with both hands on the wheel and her eyes on the building.

The hallway smelled of wet wool and radiator heat.
Coats hung two deep on the rack by the boardroom doors,
and the floor under the rack was tracked gray from boots.
The radiator under the window knocked twice and quit.
Through the open doors she could see the rows,
a coat over the back of every chair,
and not one seat left.

Missy Gault sat in the third row with her purse on her knees.
Denny beside her had his coat buttoned to the top.
Missy wanted her son safe and wanted him playing,
and she had come this morning to hear which of the two
the county was going to let her keep.
Marisol Pruett had the aisle seat halfway back,
with two sheets of paper in a clear sleeve on her lap.
At the end of the front row a man in a gray suit sat with nothing in his hands.
He had not brought a folder.
He was the university's lawyer, and he wanted nothing said by anybody.
The athletic director had not come.

She did not look for Dan.
She knew exactly where she was not looking.
It was the back wall by the doors,
where a man his size would stand if he stood anywhere.
Her heart went up hard anyway, and her breath caught on it.
She stood still by the coat rack until both came back down.
They had not been seen together since December,
and this morning of all mornings they were not going to be.
The terms held till Wednesday, when his class closed.
And nothing between them had been settled at her door on Thursday night.
She had pulled him in by the front of his coat,
and then she had sent him home and told him to say nothing today.
He had said he would pay whatever it cost.
She had said no.
She wanted him at the back wall anyway.
She would not have told a living soul that.

Earlene Tatum came out through the doors against the crowd going in,
in her church coat, with a flat cushion under one arm.
Earlene sat in the front row of everything this town held,
and what she wanted this morning was the county made to say out loud
whether a woman who did her job right got to keep it.

"All you ever did here was the work they brought you in to do,"
Earlene said.
"This morning the county has to decide if that's permitted."

"I know it," Aisha said.

Earlene lifted the cushion an inch.
"I've sat in those chairs before."

She went back in with it.

Inside, the boardroom was long and too warm,
with a long table across the front for the review
and a small table facing it with one chair.
Aisha took the one chair.
Her copy of Delores's count was folded in the jacket's inside pocket.
She put her hand over it once through the cloth,
and then she put both hands flat on the table.

The reviewer read two things into the record before anything else.
The first was that the review would hear anyone it chose to,
at the reviewer's own discretion,
and that every word went into a transcript.
The second was the rule.

"The NCAA's Independent Medical Care rule
gives the team physician authority over return to play
that it calls, in its own words, unchallengeable and autonomous,"
the reviewer said.
"Nobody here disputes whose call it was.
The review is asking whether it was made well.
Dr. Cole, you may sit or stand to answer."

Aisha stood.
She delivered standing.
She always had.

"The checklist most programs work from
lets a mild headache ride on the bike, if it's brief," the reviewer said.
"At fourteen minutes your patient had one.
You stopped him and started him over from the bottom.
Other programs would likely have had him back for that playoff.
Why did your ladder run slower than theirs?"

It was a fair question.
It was what the man in the feed-store cap had said
over his eggs at the Checkerboard last Tuesday,
only put in better clothes
and asked by somebody who could do something about the answer.
The reviewer wanted the true answer and had asked for it straight,
and she liked the reviewer for that and was afraid of it at once.
Her fingertips pressed the table.

"The checklist says a step takes at least a day," Aisha said.
"It never says a step takes only one.
At step two, sixteen days after the hit,
the patient was on the bike with his heart rate under the cap.
At minute fourteen his hand came off the bar and went up under his hood.
The headache wasn't mild, and it wasn't brief.
It was more than the checklist lets a physician ride through.
So I did what the checklist says.
I stepped him down, and his clock started over."

The reviewer wrote.

"Past two weeks, the checklist says re-evaluate," she said.
"I did that in January, under load.
Twenty minutes at the cap, and he held all twenty.
His family took him to Birmingham for a second opinion.
They booked it themselves.
I sent that office the patient's whole chart the day before they drove,
before anybody asked me for it.
What Birmingham told them is theirs to say."

Each time she said *the patient* she lost a little more of him,
because in her head he was Trey on the bike
with the hood up and his hand on the bar.
His name was the one warm thing she had carried in with her.
She kept it out of the transcript
so that nobody reading it in ten years or twenty,
not a scout and not a stranger,
would find a boy's name sitting beside the word *headache*.
Nobody noticed.
It cost her anyway, a small cold place under the breastbone
every time she said it.

"And the record?" the reviewer said.

"Every rung is dated," Aisha said.
"The August baseline. Both studies after the hit,
the twenty-eighth of November and the third of December.
Every order signed, with the reason written under it.
The tape times, fourteen minutes and seven fifty-nine.
The family's log, every morning at seven.
The annex records are forty-one boxes,
locked at Mt. Nebo Missionary Baptist since December,
and my count matches Mrs. Fountain-Mabry's pad to the box."

"The review will want to see that pad."

"It doesn't leave the church.
She'll show it to anybody who drives out there and asks her."

In the front row Earlene nodded, as if she had been asked too.

Two rows back a man said, not quite low enough,
"Merritt's not here."

"Course he's not," the woman beside him said.

Aisha kept her eyes on the reviewer.
Her face went hot all at once, and she kept it to the front.
She sat down.
Her hands found the table and pressed flat,
the way they had pressed on a tablet on the turf every Friday of the season
while a man pushed on her sheet.

A trustee at the end of the front row put up a hand to be heard,
and the reviewer nodded.
The trustee wanted the university never to be the one
explaining a hurt boy to his mother, and wanted that on the record short.

"If Dr. Cole had cleared him," the trustee said,
"and he'd gone down on that field,
what would all of us be saying this morning?"

Nobody answered.
The reviewer wrote it down.
The trustee sat back with a coat folded over both knees.
It was one voice.
The man in the feed-store cap had been another, and the radio a third,
and the county was split down the middle of every row.
Aisha could hear it in the chairs.

[TK the trustee's name]


After the trustee, Marisol Pruett asked to be heard,
and the reviewer let her.
Marisol moderated the parents' board.
That board had carried the families' letter about roster priorities,
and her own son Wes was a walk-on at the bottom of that roster.
She wanted the room to see where the letter's words had come from,
and it was costing her to stand up and show them
in front of the very families who had signed it.
Aisha could see what it cost in how carefully she stood.

She came down the aisle and laid two sheets on the reviewer's table,
side by side.
She had the families' own copy from her board
and the consultant's pages from the trustees' February agenda packet,
which anybody in the county could print.

"This is the letter eleven families signed," Marisol said.
"I know most of them. They meant every word of it.
This is the consultant's printed material for the new building,
out of the trustees' own February packet.
Read the middle of each."

The reviewer read.
Then the reviewer read one line of each aloud,
and it was the same line.

"'Proactive risk posture,'" the reviewer said.

The phrase went back through the rows in a whisper.
Aisha heard it pass behind her,
three words nobody in this county had ever said over eggs.

"No family I know talks like that," Marisol said.
"Somebody handed them those words, and they signed in good faith.
I can't prove who wrote it.
Half this county will be saying Rex Boyd by lunch,
and I can't prove that either, so I'm not saying it."

A sound went through the chairs that was not quite a laugh.
Marisol picked up her two sheets and went back to her seat.
Her hands shook a little on the clear sleeve.
She held it flat against her coat until they stopped.

Aisha wanted Dan to see those two sheets side by side.
He took a true thing well, even one that went against him,
and this one had gone for her.
It was a foolish thing to want with forty people between her and the doors.
She wanted it anyway, and her chest ached with it.

consultant's materials in the trustees' February packet — proposed canon]


It was past noon when the reviewer asked
whether anyone else wished to be heard before the recess.
The radiator knocked.
Nobody moved.
The reviewer squared the stack of paper with both hands.

"I'd like to be heard," a man said from the doorway.

She knew the voice before she turned.
It went down her back like a hand.
Dan Merritt stood in the open doors in a dark suit.
He hated the suit.
She could see it in his shoulders, which were built for a sideline jacket
and were fighting every seam of this one,
and in his right hand,
which went up to the knot of his tie and came away again.
The haircut from Thursday was still sharp at the back of his neck.
He was not on the review's list.
The whole front row knew it, and the whole front row looked at the lawyer.

The lawyer half stood, one hand on the back of the chair in front of him.

"Coach," the lawyer said.

Her fingertips went white on the table.
Her weight came forward onto her feet.
The word *don't* was right behind her teeth,
the same word she had used on him at her door.
She knew what this cost.
She had told him herself on Thursday.
It cost the job he had wanted his whole life,
and it cost every man on his staff.

"The review will hear Coach Merritt," the reviewer said.

The lawyer sat down slowly.

She did not stand.
She took her hands off the table and put them under her on the chair,
palms down on the wood,
and she sat on them the way a child sits on her hands in church.

Dan came down the aisle between the rows.
He went by her table close enough that she felt the air he moved,
still cold from outside, with starch in it.
He did not look at her.
He did not look for her before he spoke,
and she understood exactly why.
It went through her anyway, low and hard.
He stopped at the end of the reviewer's table
with his hands at his sides.

"Dan Merritt, head coach," he said.
"Last Monday I gave this review half an answer.
I'm here with the rest."

The rows went so quiet she heard the radiator tick.

"The hold on my quarterback started the night of the twenty-eighth of November.
Dr. Cole's no-go form is dated Thursday, the seventeenth of December.
That was before the families' letter got to the athletic director's desk
the Monday after Christmas.
It was a month before anybody put up a picture."

Her breath had gone high and shallow.
She made it go down, and it would not stay.

"Every Friday since June I stood on the turf
and pushed her for the Saturday word instead of *probable*," Dan said.
"Once I went around her to the athletic director
to get my quarterback into a film meeting,
and he told me no in writing.
She held both times.
My staff did what I told them.
Whatever pressure came out of our building came out of my office,
so put it on me."

Every sentence was one she could have said better.
She could hear where she would have put the dates
and where she would have stopped him,
and her mouth opened on it.
She shut it again.

"She cost me the best player I've got,
in the biggest game this program's played in years," Dan said.
"If 7 walked in tomorrow with that same headache,
she'd cost me him again.
And I'd argue with her again, and I'd lose again."

The laugh came out of the rows before anybody could stop it.
It went from the back wall to the front,
Earlene in it and the trustee in it and two of the families from the letter,
a real laugh, the first she had heard in a week.
Dan did not smile.
He let them have it and waited.
Her own laugh came up and stuck,
and her eyes went hot behind it.

"Last Monday this review asked me whether I'd have played him," Dan said.
"I said I'd have played him. That's exactly why the rule says I don't decide."

He let that sit.
In the third row Missy Gault's hand shut on the clasp of her purse.
It stayed shut.

"On the radio in January I called it her protocol," he said.
"Her name is Dr. Aisha Cole."

Her whole name went into the transcript in his voice.
It landed under her breastbone and spread from there,
warm and then hot, out along both arms.
Her breath went out of her and did not come back for a moment.
She pressed down on her hands until the edge of the seat bit her knuckles,
because every part of her that corrected people for a living
wanted to stand up and take the record back and say it right.
He had not asked her.
Nobody had ever spent a thing on her in front of anybody,
and she had made sure of that in every city she had worked in.
Now a man was spending his whole name on her in front of forty people,
and the only thing left that she could do for him
was to sit still and let it count.
She sat still.
Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in her knuckles under her.
Her face was hot to the hairline, and she let the rows see that much.
And some part of her she would never say out loud to anybody was glad.
It was not pride in him.
It was the plain gladness of being paid for,
the one thing she had never let herself have.

At his side Dan's right hand had shut.
She watched it open again, slowly, like a man setting down something heavy.
In the third row Missy's thumb pressed the clasp until it clicked.
At the end of the front row the lawyer was looking at the ceiling.

"Thank you, Coach," the reviewer said.
"The review will take a recess.
It sits again Wednesday morning, and the findings will follow."

Dan turned and went up the aisle
and out the doors before the first row was on its feet.
He went left in the hallway.
She saw that much through the open doors,
the dark suit going left toward the back of the building
where a green sign over a steel door said STAIRS,
while the rows were still getting to their feet.
The forty went right, toward the front hall and the coffee urn by the coats.
The lawyer went straight to the reviewer's table.
Whatever he had to say, he wanted it said there first.


Aisha waited until the doors were clear.
Then she went out and turned left, as if the restrooms were all she wanted,
and past them the hallway ended at the steel door with a small window in it.
Behind it the back stairs went down in a square turn to a landing.
The building's heat stopped at that door.
Dan was on the landing below with his back to the cinderblock.
Nobody could see the landing unless they came through the door
and down the first flight,
and everybody in the building was at the other end of it.
He looked up when the door shut behind her.

She came down the stairs one at a time.
He watched her come and did not move off the wall.
She stopped on the last step, and it put her eyes level with his.
His jaw was set, and then it let go.
His eyes did not leave hers.
Neither of them said a word.
His right hand came up between them, palm up, and stopped there.
She looked at the hand.
She looked at him.

She put her hand in his.
His fingers closed over it, warm, and the warm went straight up her arm
into her chest.
Her heart knocked against her ribs hard enough to hurt.
Her breath went out, and for a moment she could not get it back in.
Her knees went loose,
and she put her other hand on the cold rail so she would stay on the step.
His thumb moved once across her knuckles,
and she felt it at the base of her spine.
Her face went hot, and she did not turn it away from him.
She wanted him so badly it frightened her,
on a cold back stair with forty people drinking coffee
at the far end of the building
and the university's lawyer one hallway away from finding them.
Her head was perfectly clear.
That was the frightening part.
Wanting him and being afraid for him had turned into one feeling,
and she could not pull them apart with his hand around hers.

He held it for one count.
Then he let go, and his hand stayed open in the air
a second after hers had gone.
He went down the last flight and out the door at the bottom,
and she heard it shut on its closer.
She stood on the step with her hand closed on nothing
until her breath came back.
Then she went up the stairs to the warm side of the door.

After the recess the reviewer had questions for her until nearly three,
the dates again and the orders one at a time,
and she answered every one of them standing up in the good jacket
with her hands at her sides.
Verna was at the curb when they let her go.
Neither of them said anything on the drive back to the Court.

By half past five the light was going off the gravel at the Magnolia Court.
Aisha sat on the end of the bed in room nine,
still in the good jacket, with the lamp off and the curtain open.
Taking the jacket off would have made the morning over.
The RAV4 sat outside the window, loaded, with the parka on the top box.
The acceptance was still typed on her phone under the chair's email.
She had not sent it.

Verna knocked twice and came in without waiting,
in her cardigan with her own phone in her hand,
and the cold came in around her off the gravel before she got the door shut.

"The trustees put their February agenda back up at four,"
Verna said. "Amended. I've read it three times."

She held the phone out.
Aisha took it, and Verna's hand was cold where it touched hers.
The screen had two lines on it that mattered.
The pledge item said *re-sequenced to a later sitting.*
The item for the head coach's contract extension had been struck.

Re-sequenced meant moved to a later meeting.
The pledge for the glass building that would stand where her clinic stood
was not coming to the trustees' table in February,
and it had moved on the same day Dan said her name.
His extension was not coming to the table at all.
She knew what that was.
It was the job he had wanted his whole life,
gone off a page in one afternoon,
and every man on his staff out looking for work by spring.
Her stomach went hollow.
Her hands went cold on Verna's phone,
and her eyes burned, and she let them.

"I don't read agendas," Verna said.
"I read that one three times so the radio wouldn't be the one to tell you."

"I know what it cost him, Verna," Aisha said.
"I watched him pay it this morning,
and I sat on my hands and didn't stop him."

Verna did not answer that.
She took the one chair by the window and sat down in it,
and she did not reach for the lamp.

Wednesday the review would say.
Saturday the chair out west wanted an answer,
and the answer was typed and sitting in her lap.
She thought about him in the suit he hated,
going left in the hallway toward the back stairs
so that nobody in the building would see where he went,
and waiting on a cold landing for a woman who might not come.
She was afraid of Wednesday.
She was more afraid of what he had spent,
and of how glad she had been to let him.
Her right hand still had the shape of his in it.
She held it closed in her lap,
and Verna sat with her in the dark and did not get up to go.
