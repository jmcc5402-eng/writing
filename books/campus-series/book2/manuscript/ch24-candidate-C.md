The wipers were the loudest thing on Millrow,
and Dan Merritt drove up the hill behind them with the heat on his boots.
It was the first rain since the ice, a straight cold rain with no wind,
and it came down the windshield faster than the blades could clear it.
The truck's roof took it like gravel thrown a handful at a time.
The water off the hill ran both gutters full
and crossed the road at the first curve in a sheet.
This was Wednesday the twentieth of January, before seven,
and nobody in the county had anything new to know yet.

Monday night he had opened the board at the house
and read it down to the last reply,
her laugh in the ice-chest picture and Rhonda's sentence under it.
Tuesday he had been in the Fieldhouse fourteen hours with January's roster
and had not set foot on the square.
Ty had texted at ten that night that the aldermen had passed the Millrow thing,
because Ty read the town's rooms too.

His key was in the Fieldhouse's side door when the phone went in his coat.
He stood in the rain with the key in the lock and read it there.
It was from the athletic director, and it was to him.
Dr. Cole's name was under his on the line where the names went.
There was no heading on it and no name at the bottom.

*Effective this morning, Dr. Cole is suspended from her duties
as team physician, pending an independent review
of the December medical determination concerning your quarterback.
The reviewers will report within fourteen days,
and this office will make their report public.
Medical coverage for the program continues without interruption.
This office will speak for the department on the matter.
Direct any inquiry to it, and make no comment of your own.*

He read it twice, with the rain running off the bill of his cap onto the screen.
Pending meant she was out of the building while other people read her chart.
The fourteenth day from a Wednesday was a Wednesday, the third of February,
signing day, the day the class signed.
"Medical determination" was the athletic director's phrase.
The athletic director wanted the county to do this for him,
and he had found a word for it that sounded like fairness.

Her name under his on a screen in the rain went into Dan's chest
and sat there, flat, like a hand laid on it.
Nobody in the county had this yet but the two names on the line.
The review was a fortnight of some other office's letterhead
laid over her chart.
When it was up, the trustees could hand her job to the group in February
as a thing the review recommended, and nobody would have to say her name.
A head coach who put his own name beside hers this week,
with that picture on every phone in the county,
would be gone the same day she was, and he would not coach again.
He turned the key, and the door gave, and the rain came in with him.


Before eight the university's lawyer and the department's press woman
were in his office.
The athletic director's office had sent them up
before another coach was in the building.
The lawyer was a man in a raincoat with the shoulders wet through.
The press woman had a folder she did not open.
Dan had the January roster on the desk
and the rain on the window behind him.
The annex lot across the way had no RAV4 in it yet.

The lawyer shut the door.
Dan's rule was that every door in the building stood open,
and the lawyer had shut this one with his back to it and had not asked.
Dan let it stay shut.
That was the first thing he let go of that morning.
The lawyer wanted no statement from anybody, on anything, at any time.
The press woman wanted the athletic director's words
to be the only words the county heard from the building.
Neither of them was wrong.
That was what made it hard to hate either one.

"You've seen the email," the lawyer said.

"I've seen it."

"The department's statement goes out at ten.
It's the athletic director's statement, and it's the only one.
Nothing from you.
Not to a reporter, not on the radio, not to a parent who has your cell."

"I said something on the radio Thursday."

"That was before there was a review," the press woman said.
"There's a review now. The statement covers Thursday.
If a reporter calls, it comes to me.
If you get asked at a counter, you've seen the statement
and you've got nothing to add to it."

The lawyer had not sat.
He stood with his back to the shut door
and the rain going down the window across from him, and said the true thing.

"Coach, any statement from you makes it worse for her.
I'd like you to hear that as arithmetic and not as advice.
The day the head coach speaks for the team physician,
with that photograph where it is,
the review quits being about a number on a screen in December.
It starts being about the two of you.
Then it isn't a review anymore. Then it's a finding."

It was true.
Dan had known it was true before the lawyer got to the end of it,
and that was the trap.
The sentence he could say was in his head, whole.
*Dr. Cole made the right call in December, and my name goes under hers on it.*
Sixteen words, and a coach could say them at any table in the state.
He had said the half without her name in it on the radio Thursday.
The half with her name in it was the half that counted.

He was in the Fieldhouse fourteen hours today and fourteen tomorrow,
and eleven months of the year the same,
and that was not a thing that had happened to him.
It was what he was.
A man who said the sixteen words this week was not that by dark.
He put his hand in his coat pocket to have somewhere to put it,
and found the truck key there, and closed on it.

"Who's doing the review?" Dan said.

"Outside people.
The names come to the department when the department gets them."

"All right," Dan said.

He said nothing else, and the lawyer heard the two words as a yes,
which they were.
The press woman opened the folder then
and put one page on the roster, the statement.
It said what the email said in fewer words, and it said suspended.
The lawyer opened the door on his way out and left it open behind him.
Dan sat with the door open and the roster under his hand
and did not pick up the pen.
He knew what he had just done, and he knew what it was called,
and he did not call it that.


The department put the statement on its own page at ten
and sent it to the paper's inbox,
and the parents' board had it at one minute past.
That was how the county learned it.
Dan read the board at his desk with the rain loud behind him.
A board had known what he was once before the school did,
and the football on a board hid in the ordinary talk.
He was reading for what the town knew about the review, and there was nothing.
Rhonda Sipes, who had wanted the hold explained since October,
had had her sentence on the parents' board since Monday.
By eleven it was in the town room, and that was a different thing.

DeeAnn Prewitt sat in a car at practice every day of the fall with the heat on,
and posted under her own name.
She wanted to be first into any room with a thing worth carrying.
She had carried Rhonda's sentence into the town room,
where the county went for lost dogs and road work,
and put a line of her own under it.

> **DeeAnn Prewitt** Since the college put it out this morning I'll say
> what the parents already know. December 19, 11 AM, look at the time
> on that picture. This explains a LOT about the hold. Not naming
> names. Y'all can do the math.
>
> **Gene Ashcraft** This isn't the room for that, DeeAnn. Some of us are
> on here for the recycling schedule. Recycling is Thursday, for anybody
> who came in for that.
>
> **Wanda Teel** Speaking of Millrow. If they're changing that whole side
> of the street can somebody tell me where the church van parks on
> Sunday. Asking for the van.

Nobody else answered DeeAnn.
Two people answered Wanda about the van, and a third about the curb,
and the room went on to a dog that had got out on the county road.
Nobody deleted anything, because nobody in that room ever did.
The town room had turned on the woman who brought the thing in,
and not on the doctor.
It had done it in the voice it used for the recycling.
Her name was in a room that did not care, and it had cost nobody but DeeAnn.

Beside it was the vote, in three replies about parking.
Tuesday night the aldermen had changed the zoning on the town's side of Millrow,
so a building that started on campus could keep going across the street.
Her clinic stood on the university's ground, and no town vote touched that.
That ground went to the trustees in February on the same page as her job.
A street on Tuesday night, and the doctor on Wednesday morning,
and the county's whole answer to both of them was where the church van parked.
Dan read it twice and closed it.
The town's verdict was a shrug, and a shrug was worse than a sentence,
because there was nothing in it to answer.


At noon the film room's window, the one on the lot, had the RAV4 in it,
nosed up to the warm-end door with the exhaust standing white behind it.
Aisha was in the driver's seat with the engine running.
Dan went down the corridor and out the side door into the rain
without a hat and without deciding to.
The lot had water standing in every rut,
and the rain hit the standing water and made it jump,
and his boots went through it.
The survey crew's flags were where the crew had left them on Monday,
between the annex and the fence, orange, and beaten flat by the rain.
A car with a staff tag sat at the fence with nobody in it he knew.

The trainer was in the warm-end door with the door held on his shoulder.
The trainer wanted his job,
and he wanted to be on nobody's side of that lot,
and he had put himself in the door.
The door was neither side.
The corridor behind him was empty at noon on a day with three ankles due.
The trainer had moved the ankles.
Nobody in that building had watched her carry the box out,
because the trainer had seen to it that nobody was in the corridor to watch.
It was the one competent thing done for her all morning,
and he had done it without a word.

Both her hands were on the bottom of the wheel.
The box was on the seat beside her, one box,
with the tablet in it and a mug and the parka folded on top.
She was in a coat of her own.
He stood at her window in the rain.
Her face was there behind the glass and the water running down it,
a foot off, and she had watched him come across the whole lot.
His hand went up, and one knuckle of it went against her window, twice.
The window came down four inches and stopped.

The four inches of her face did their work before he had decided anything.
His breath went out of him and did not come back for a while.
The heat off the car came through the gap onto his wet face,
and it had her in it, the smell of her under the smell of the heater.
His stomach went down and stayed down, like the step off the truck onto ice.
His hand wanted the door handle.
He knew it wanted the handle because it had already moved.
He put the hand in his coat pocket and shut it on the key.
His heart was going in his throat, where he could feel it against his collar.
His face was hot in the rain, and it had no business being hot.
He wanted to open the door and get her out of the car,
in the rain, with the trainer in the door and a car at the fence.
He wanted to say the sixteen words through four inches of open window.
He was not going to, and he stood there and knew both things at once.

Her eyes were on his and stayed there.
The corner of her mouth went up, half, and came back down,
and her eyes went past him to the car at the fence and came back.
Nothing on a face got past her, and his had all of it on it.
Her mouth opened a little and shut again, and nothing came out of it.
She did not ask him to say her name to a reporter,
or on the air, or to the man in the raincoat.
He watched her not ask.
Nobody had ever spent anything on Aisha Cole,
and she was not going to start over by asking a coach to.
He knew what it was, and it was worse than if she had asked.
He would not have told anybody that he had wanted her to.
If she asked, the quiet would have been hers to carry.
She did not, and it was his.

"Film's at four," he said.
It was all a coach had for her that day, and he said it.

"I know," she said.
Her eyes stayed on his while she said it, and then she looked at the wheel.

The window went up.
The glass had the rain on it again, and her face was behind the rain.
She put the car in gear, and the RAV4 went out of the lot
and down Millrow in daylight, at noon, with the water running beside it.
He stood in the lot and watched the taillights to the first curve.
Where she went from the curve he did not know.
Then he walked back across the lot in the rain,
a coach who had gone to a car and said three words a kid could have said.
A coach walking to a suspended doctor's car at noon
was a line under the picture,
and he had written it with his boots and bought nothing with it.
He had known that at the window and crossed anyway.


Film was at four, in the film room, with the rain still going on the glass.
The coordinator had the clicker and January's cutup on the screen.
Ty Beaumont came in last with the receivers' notes
and sat on the end of the near couch.
"Coach," Ty said, and nothing else.
Ty wanted the job that made his truck payment,
and he knew how to be on a staff,
and being on a staff on that day meant one word and the notes.
Nobody said statement.
Nobody said doctor.
Dan watched feet for ninety minutes.
The sixteen words sat behind his teeth the whole cutup,
and he ran the receivers' tape over them and did not get them down.

At six he was on his stool at the Checkerboard,
the one nearest the register, with a glass of water and nothing else.
The rain was on the square's glass, steady, running.
Earlene Tatum had the window with the pot in her hand,
and Tunk Ferrell had the chair across from her,
and the cook had the griddle and his back to the room.
Tunk wanted the story, and wanted to have it first.
Earlene wanted the county counted straight.
The cook wanted his griddle and nothing said across it.
The cook came the length of the counter and set the Slice in front of Dan,
unasked, at six at night, and went back.
Dan ate it.

The vote had come in the door on Tuesday night by mouth,
off people who had sat in the hall,
and the statement had come at ten on every phone in the room.

"Street Tuesday, doctor Wednesday," Tunk said, to the window.
"Both in the paper by Friday, and forty answers on where to park
and not one on what they're parking at."

The cook laughed at that, once, one bark at the griddle.
Dan had nothing in him for it, and he let it go by.

"They ought to review the parking while they're at it," Tunk said.

Nobody answered that.

Earlene shifted the pot to her other hand.

"Nobody up at that college put the doctor out of her building.
They'd have had to sign that.
They hung a word on her door and stepped back from it,
and the county's supposed to do the walking.
It'll do it, too. It's been practicing since October."
She looked at the rain on the window and not at anybody.
"And the same week a street got moved.
The rest of this week a body can count on one hand,
and I've counted it, and I didn't need the paper or the board."

"Whose building's it going to be, Earlene?" Tunk said.

"Ask me Friday."

Tunk had the name that was in both, and he shut his mouth on it.
Nobody at the counter said it.
Dan heard all of it with his back to the window.
Earlene came up the counter with the pot, looked at the water glass,
and went back down to the window without pouring.


At dark Dan drove the truck up Millrow in the rain
and put it in the annex lot instead of his own, by the warm-end door.
The clinic was still lit at that end.
Whoever the school had sent to cover her had the corridor light on
and the tank off, and was somewhere down the far end with a kid.
The warm-end door was unlocked, because the clinic was open.
He went in out of the rain and stood in the corridor
with the water off him on the tile.
The heat was on in the corridor, and the rain on the annex's flat roof
was the only sound, steady, like a shower running in another room.

Her office door stood open.
The chair was pushed in square to the desk, as she left it every night.
The desk had a clear place on it where the tablet had sat,
and the file drawer was shut.
The parka was not on the back of the door.
Dan stood in her door and did not go through it.
The sixteen words were in his head one more time, whole.
*Dr. Cole made the right call in December, and my name goes under hers on it.*
He did not say them.

Aisha was down the hill somewhere with one box and no building,
and she had not asked him for a thing.
He was afraid of the plain version of it.
The day would come when he could say the sixteen words,
and it would be past the day they could do her any good.
The trustees sat in February.
The review ran out the day the class signed, and he had chosen the class.
The man standing in her door at dark would have chosen it again at seven,
and knew it, and that was the worst part of the standing.
Her chair sat square to her desk with nobody in it.
The clinic was open around him, lit, with the rain on the roof,
and the doctor was not in it.
He stood in the door a while longer, and he did not go in.
