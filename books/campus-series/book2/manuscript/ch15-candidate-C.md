> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Tim Brasher:** Flying in Friday. Drove it in November and the drive
> was the better idea. Whoever runs the tailgate cooler, I have ice and
> a folding chair and nowhere to put either one.


Her pen was on the last line of the page when the annex heater
came up behind her with its morning sound, a fan finding its speed,
and she signed over the noise of it the long way, in ink,
Aisha Cole and the letters after her name,
on paper, because nobody could say later
that a screen had changed it.
The page said, in her words and no coach's,
that Trey Gault was not cleared for competition on the nineteenth,
that he stood on step three of six,
and that every step held overnight before the next.
It was Thursday, the seventeenth, and Ashford played Saturday,
and Wednesday's bike, hers to run, had held,
and it changed nothing, because a rung on her ladder
was not a rung until it had slept.
Wednesday was two. Today was three, and Friday four,
and Saturday five, and five slept a night before anybody wrote six,
and that put six on Sunday at the soonest, and nobody played on Sunday.
Out was a Friday word by her own habit,
and she was writing it a day early on purpose,
so the Gaults and the team had two days to stand in it instead of one.
The county had sold every room for Saturday,
and by noon it would know the game was going to be played
without its quarterback, and that the doctor had signed the paper.

Missy Gault's page had come at seven, the night held, slept, zero,
and at seven Trey was across the lot in the Fieldhouse meeting room
for the twenty minutes his coach had held three weeks for him,
no film, projector unplugged, his mother in the hall.
That was where Aisha called her, before the page went anywhere.
She heard the Fieldhouse hall before she heard Missy,
a long floor with a door shutting somewhere down it.

"It's Dr. Cole. He isn't cleared for Saturday.
It goes to the athletic director and to Coach in ten minutes,
and I wanted you to have it from me first.
He's fine today. Fine today isn't five nights,
and five nights is the rule. That's the only reason."

Missy took a breath on the other end.

"He's got nine minutes left in there with the projector off.
I'll tell him in the car. I'm not telling him in this hall."

Missy hung up first.

She scanned the signed page at the annex printer
and sent it to the athletic director,
at the address his medical-determination sentence had come from,
with a copy to Merritt,
and the ink original went in Trey's file.
She entered it in the chart the long way
and did not go across the lot to watch anybody read it.
She had signed it knowing whose jacket it would end up in
and that he would not fight it,
and she would not have said so to anybody, the chart included.
At eight the trainer ran Trey's step three on the empty turf,
lanes and no ball, football-shaped and nothing else,
and by nine Trey was a student again with a night to hold.

***

At ten past one Aisha was at her desk at the warm end of the annex
with the clinic done and the parents' board open on her phone,
reading it as weather, and the weather had gone quiet in the bad way.
The county had known since ten; a coach tells his boss.
Nobody had typed her name.
DeeAnn Prewitt had typed three sentences at eight minutes to noon
and let them sit an hour with no reply, and the sitting was the point.
*Interesting season. Interesting choices. Praying for the boys.*
Under it the letter with the count on it had a new first sentence,
and its subject was the doctor, and the count had gone up.
Two weeks ago that board had put her in a truck with the wrong man;
now it had the coach and the doctor in one sentence again,
and it liked that story better.

The trainer came through her corridor at half past one
with a roll of tape under his arm,
and stopped in her door, and did not come in.

"Coach had his captains in at ten," he said. "Door open.
I was taping in the hall."

She put the phone down.

"He told them 7 sits Saturday on the doctor's paper,
and the paper's right,
and anybody with a word to say about it says it to him first.
Nobody said anything back. That room always says something back."

"Figured you'd want it from a man who was in the hall."

He went on down the corridor,
and the tape tore off the roll in there, once and again.
He had done it at ten with her page in his hand,
before she knew, and had not come across the lot to say so.
She had it from a man with tape under his arm,
and that was the only way it could have counted.
A man who tells you what he did for you is asking for something.
She had watched him agree with her in a room once
and go upstairs and lose to his boss in an email,
and this was that habit turned inside out.
She wanted him for it.
Plainly, at half past one on a weekday,
she wanted the man who had done that and said nothing about it.

***

At four she drove across to the Magnolia Court,
because the clinic's last kid had gone at three
and the turf did not want her until five fifteen.
The lot was half plates from other states already,
and a man at an RV door with a phone at his ear
stopped talking when she got out of the car
and started again behind the office door.
Verna Poteat was at the window with the book open.

"Through Saturday," Verna said, to the book.
"Room nine tonight. The back room tomorrow, and Saturday.
I had a man once who'd have sold this motel for one Saturday.
He'd have got the one Saturday,
and I'd have been at this window the Monday after with no motel."
She shut the book on her finger.
"The whole county wants Saturday.
That boy needs the next sixty years."

She said it about the boy and not about the doctor,
and not as comfort, and did not look up from the book to say it.
Aisha did not say one word in her own defense.
Verna would have heard a defense as an admission and kept it.

She went back across at ten to five,
because thirty-four was on tonight's sheet and the sheet was hers to type.
She had not looked for the F-150 in Verna's lot;
coming into the Fieldhouse lot she looked for it
before she looked at her own building, and it was there, two doors.

***

Five fifteen, the practice turf under the poles,
and Aisha crossed to the near hash with tonight's sheet in her hand,
one printed page, two names,
and Dan Merritt came off the drill to take it,
with the trainer at his tape a few yards off.
Under Trey Gault's name: Step three. Holds overnight.
Not cleared for the nineteenth.
Under thirty-four's, one line she had never typed for him:
Sleeve off. Cutting cleared.

Dan read it.
He had the jaw on him that a week put there,
and he read the top name a long time for two lines
he had had in her ink since eight,
and the corner of that jaw moved once, and he looked at her over the page.
He had had his captains since ten,
and he did not say one word about it,
and she watched him not say it, and knew,
and he did not know she knew.

"Thirty-four," she said. "He cuts for me first, then it comes off.
I'm revising Monday."

"He'll want to hear that from you."

"That's why I'm going down there."

"Doc." His eyes came off the page and stayed on her
with one more sentence behind them,
and he put it away with the sheet, inside the jacket,
and the whistle went and the period turned over.

Thirty-four was at the near hash with the sleeve on,
and she stood where she could see his face and not his leg,
because a hamstring lies and a face has not learned how.

"Cut for me," she said. "Left, then right, and I want it full."

He cut. She listened for it, the cleats biting on the plant
and the leg taking the whole weight over,
no hitch, no half step to protect anything, twice each way.

"Sleeve off. You're cleared to cut.
Monday I told you the week.
I'm the one who said it, so I'm the one who moves it."

He slid the sleeve down his own leg,
looked down the field at his coach and back at her,
and did not say a word, and went.
She had cleared one and held the other on the same page,
in plain view of the car that idled in the practice lot every day,
and she knew what the board would make of that by dark.

***

At ten past seven Aisha was at her desk at the warm end of the annex
with Friday's sheet blank on the screen,
and Dan was somewhere across the dark lot in the Fieldhouse
with tonight's page inside his jacket.
The parka hung on the back of her open door.
The field poles had gone dark at seven,
and the film-room window across the lot was a black square
in a black wall.
The board had it by seven that she had cleared one and not the other;
she had read it once and put the phone in the drawer.
The building at night was the heater, running steady, no clank in it,
and once, down the corridor,
the fountain running a second on its own and quitting.
Then the outside door at the warm end, the one by her office,
opened and shut,
and she knew the step before it had gotten anywhere.
She had listened to that walk come down a sideline all fall.
It took her corridor slower, as if the floor were not his,
and stopped in her door.

He stood in her doorway with one shoulder on the frame
and the other still out in the hall,
because the frame did not have room for both,
the cap in his hand, in the sideline jacket.

"Doc."

"You're on the wrong side of the lot at seven, Coach."

"I've been on the wrong side of it since five fifteen."

She got up. He moved his shoulder off the frame to let her by,
and she put her hand flat on her own door and pushed it shut
with him on the inside of it,
and the parka swung on its hook against the wood and stilled.
Every door in his building stood open. This one was hers.
He watched her hand do it and said nothing about the door,
and his eyebrows said the rest.

"The trainer came through my corridor at half past one," she said,
"and told me you had your captains in at ten with the door open.
That you told them Trey sits Saturday on my paper,
and the paper's right,
and anybody with something to say about it says it to you first.
That nobody said anything back."

He looked at the diploma box against the baseboard, and came back.

"That's what happened."

"You didn't come over here at ten to tell me."

"No."

"What did it cost you."

"The room, for a day. Two, maybe." He turned the cap once.
"I said I'd fought you across a sheet every Friday of my life here
and lost every one of them on the merits,
and I wasn't going to start winning in a room you weren't in."

She laughed then, once, short, and it surprised them both.

"One of them asked was it the same doctor that took the mic
at Delmar's," he said. "I said there was only the one."

When a man gave her something she weighed it, found the bill in it,
and handed it back.
She stood in her own office with the door shut and did not.

"I took Boyd's Saturday off him this morning," she said.
"He'll have something for me by February. The memo says so."

"He's already got it. That's what I came across for.
I should have brought it Tuesday with the truck."

He looked at the date at the top of her whiteboard,
and then at her, and kept his eyes there.

"The Monday the class signed, Boyd's man had nine boards
on my marker tray. You've seen the drawing.
You haven't seen six.
Six is the recovery floor. Six tables.
A regional sports-medicine group runs the floor
and provides physician services under contract. His words.
That's how the numbers work. The university stops carrying that line."
He said that part faster, and his eyes never left her.
"I knew whose line it was when he said it.
So it's said plain: a group runs the floor over there,
the team's doctors work for the group,
and the university quits paying for its own.
Your job's the price of the building.
The athletic director nodded, Boyd smiled, and I nodded along,
in my own room, and I've stood across a sheet from you
every night for ten days with that in my jacket."

So the drawing had a name for the place her rooms were not.
A group. A floor with six tables on it, and her job as the price,
and a loan that did not care what she had picked.
She had signed this morning against the county for one boy
without knowing she was signing that away with it,
and here was the man who had known for ten days,
inside her shut door, telling her what his nod had cost her
on the same day he had spent his locker room on her call.
She was not angry at him.
She was angry at a board in a room,
and he had carried the board across a dark lot
so she would have it before Boyd did.

"You told me Tuesday there was no way," she said,
"with a box in your arms, and I put my hand on you for an answer,
and you didn't put it back."

"I had the box."

"You had the box." She came around the desk to where he was.
"Then you went and told your captains I was right,
and tonight you carried that board over here.
You've paid for two things of mine today, Dan,
and I'm not handing either one back."

He took a breath she could hear.

"Aisha," he said, and her name in his mouth, in that room,
was a sound she had never had anywhere,
low, with the heater under it.
"I'd have said that in June if I'd known how it sounded."

She took the cap out of his hand and set it on the desk behind her,
so there was nothing in either of their hands,
and put both of hers on the front of the jacket and pulled,
and he bent, and she kissed him.
What she had never had was the sound of him this close.
The breath he let go through his nose against her cheek.
The jacket's nylon when his arms went around her, both,
and closed, and the small sound at the back of his throat
when her mouth opened under his,
a sound a man makes and would swear he had not.
His jaw under her palm, the rough end of his week.
She had to tip her head back farther for him
than she ever had for anybody, and her neck knew it,
and she did not care.
Under all of it the heater,
and nothing else coming down that corridor tonight.
She had the hour.

She did not go far when it stopped. Neither did he.

"Here's mine," he said, against her hair,
and then moved back enough to say it to her face.
"Nothing anybody can see. Not a truck at a curb, not a booth,
not a hand on a rail, until the first Wednesday in February.
The class closes that day, all of it, every name,
and after that there's nothing left in this season
anybody can say they traded you for."

He had lost a year to a rule with another woman's face on it,
and here he was writing the rule himself, and she let him.

"Then here's mine," she said, "and it's a rule, not a favor.
I write them. I wrote the one that's holding Trey.
You get my calls on paper, at five fifteen,
with the trainer close enough to hear.
Never in here, never in a kitchen, never in whatever this is.
Not one word between us about a step or a sleeve or a boy
that isn't on a printed sheet in front of a witness,
so nobody can say a call got softened in a room with the door shut.
Not the board. Not Boyd.
Not you, and not me either, at two in the morning in February."

She watched him hear it through,
his eyes on her, steady, and then the nod he gave a right flag.

"That's the worst rule I ever agreed to," he said,
"and I've agreed to one about coolers."

"You'll get Friday's sheet at five fifteen like any other night,"
she said, "and you'll read it in front of him,
and that's the only place you hear from me about Trey
until this is over."

"And what do I hear from you in here?"

"In here you can hear from me about anything else you want."

He laughed, the low one that carried further than his field voice
and did not need to in a room this size,
and she took the second kiss out of the middle of it.
