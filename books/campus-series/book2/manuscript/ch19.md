The second stool from the register had thirty-four on it
at half past seven on Monday morning.
Aisha Cole stood inside the door with her card in her hand
and let thirty-four keep the stool.
It was the fourth of January.
The county had come home on Friday, and the square had cars on it again,
nosed in on both sides all the way around.
She had circled it once before she found a space.
The Checkerboard was full.
The one cook had the griddle and the pass-through and the register,
the same as he had had them all winter.
The noise in the room had come back with the cars.
The window had been open since Saturday.
A boy could put his name in it from his phone
and be on another school's roster in two weeks.
This was the week the county found out what that felt like.

The stool nearest the register was empty.
On a weekday Dan Merritt was gone by seven-thirty.
He had been gone by seven-thirty every weekday since June,
and it had not meant anything before this one.
It meant something now.
His truck was in the Fieldhouse lot by day, nose to the fence,
and he was up the hill in a building with his staff in it.
That was what the two of them had instead of the week.
She looked at the empty stool once.
Then she went the length of the counter and took the last stool,
under the pass-through, under the sign with his order on it.

Thirty-four ate with the fork in his right hand
and his left hand flat on the counter, the two fingers still taped.
He did not look up when she went by him, and she did not stop.
She had told him the stool was his till the semester started.
Nobody had said the semester had started, so it was his.
The cook set her eggs down without asking what she wanted.

"Kid's here every morning," he said. "Seven on the dot."

"Count them."

"I'm counting."

He went back to the griddle.
At the window the Liars' Table had Tunk Ferrell and Earlene Tatum
and the paper between them.

"Can't park on the square again," Tunk said. "Town's back."

Nobody answered him. Earlene turned a page.

Aisha ate with her phone on the counter beside the plate
and read the board while she ate, at arm's length.
It was parents in other states, mostly.
A flight that had landed. A boy who had called on Sunday.
Then, at the top, an old one.
Denny Gault's thread from October was back at the top of the board,
the one that had asked for clarity on how the medical decisions got made.
Under three months of nothing there was a new reply.
One line. The families were owed answers before the window closed.
The account had no posts before it.
Nobody had typed under the line.
She put the phone in her pocket and finished the eggs.
She paid at the register beside the empty stool,
and thirty-four was still eating when she went out.

Fifty-six was in the door of her office at ten past twelve
with his coat zipped to the chin and his phone in his hand.
Aisha had a marker in hers and a box open on the desk.
He stood half in, like every kid who came to that door,
as if the office might not want him.

"Doc."

"Come in. Shut it or don't."

He left it open.
He was the shoulder from rivalry week in November,
the stinger on a Tuesday night.
She had run him through his range at the far wall with her own hands
while Dan argued with a yes.

"I need my medical," he said. "The records. All of it.
They said get it from you, that you'd have the whole, the—"
He stopped, and started over.
"I'm in the portal. Since Saturday."

She was already up.
The tablet was on the desk, and his chart came up on it in two touches.
The printer under the shelf started pulling pages
before he had got to Saturday.
That was the thing about a chart kept right.
It could go anywhere in five minutes.
She took an envelope out of the one drawer that still had envelopes in it
and wrote his full name on it in block letters.
Then she stood at the printer and took the pages off warm.

His numbers went on top.
The first ones, from that Tuesday night, in her own hand,
the ones she had turned the tablet around for and let him read first.
The tape-times page went under that.
Half the lines on it were hers.
The other half were the trainer's, the daily checks between hers,
a Wednesday, a Thursday, a Friday at four.
Beside every one of those she wrote the trainer's name in the margin,
so the next school's doctor would know who to call
about a Thursday in November.
The trainer was down the corridor with a roll of tape
and was never going to see it.
She put the release on top for him to sign,
and he signed it standing up against the doorframe with her pen.

"That's everything I have on you," she said.
"Whoever reads it will know what I knew and when I knew it.
There's a letter that goes with it, from me to their doctor.
Come back at two and it'll be in there."

"Okay."

"Wherever you land, hand it to their doctor the first day.
Before anybody runs you."

He nodded and went down the corridor with the phone in his hand.
He had never unzipped the coat.

He was one of Dan's.
By tonight he would not be.
Dan had known since Saturday, because a kid told his coach
before he told a doctor.
He had not said a word of it to her, and he would not.
A roster was his side of the lot and a chart was hers,
and the kid had walked from one to the other with a phone.

The letter took her until two.
It said what a chart could not.
It said what she had seen in his face when she moved the arm,
and how long she had waited before she wrote the number down.
The annex had five weeks left and one box packed,
and she gave the kid the afternoon anyway.
He came back at two.
She put the letter in, sealed the envelope, and handed it to him,
and he held it flat against his coat.

"Good luck," she said. "I mean the shoulder and I mean the rest."

"Okay," he said, and went.

He did not say thank you.
He was a kid with an envelope, and that was all right.
She had left three buildings before this one,
and nobody had kept her at any of them either,
and nobody had handed her a thing at the door.

Rex Boyd was standing in the door of the whirlpool room at three,
with his hands in the pockets of a dark coat and his shoes shined.
Aisha came down the corridor from her office
with a roll of packing tape on her wrist and found him there.
Nobody had let him in.
The warm-end door was unlocked in the daytime because the clinic was open,
and he had opened it and walked in.
The consultant was a step behind him in the corridor
with the survey clipboard against his vest.
It was the same gray fleece and the same lanyard turned backward,
and he was writing without looking at what he wrote.
Behind Boyd the tank was running.
The trainer had a kid on the near table with an ankle up on a bolster,
and the kid was looking at Boyd, and the trainer was not.

"Doctor," Boyd said.
He said it low. He said everything low,
and he was already smiling when he turned around.
"Don't stop on my account. I'm looking at a building."

"It's a clinic, Mr. Boyd. It's open."

"I see that. That's a good tank."

He looked at the tank a while longer.
He did not ask her anything and he did not ask the trainer,
and his hands did not come out of his pockets.
Then he walked.
He looked into the quiet room, at the bike and its one chair.
He looked into the records room,
where the four cabinets stood open to the bottom.
The brown sleeves of film were still on their shelf,
and the carton that said KNEES, 1994 was on the floor under them.
The consultant wrote something in every door.

"Is the film staying?" the consultant said.

"It isn't mine to move."

Boyd read nothing and wrote nothing and touched nothing.
He stopped at her office and looked in.

Her office was the thing that was boxing.
The shelves were half down, the books on the floor in stacks by subject.
Three boxes stood taped and numbered by the door in her marker,
forty-two, forty-three, forty-four.
The numbers went on from December's,
because the log on the tablet was one log.
The diploma box stood on its edge against the baseboard
where it had stood since June, still taped.
She had never unpacked it, and now it did not need unpacking.

"You keep a good list," Boyd said.

"It's the only way to move paper."

"Lists write themselves, once somebody starts one."

Then he said the other thing, in the door,
with his hands where they were and the smile where it was.
He did not step in for it.

"Your name came up last week. A directorship, out west.
A whole department, and a building with its name on it already.
I pass names along when they come up. That's all I do.
I'd hate to lose you."

That was all of it.
He let a silence stand there after it.
It was a wide one, and it was hers to fill if she wanted to.

"Mr. Boyd," she said.

That was all she put in it.
He waited a while longer, smiling,
and what came into the silence next was not from her.
The warm-end door opened at the end of the corridor,
and Dan came through it in daylight
with a stack of flat boxes against his chest.
There were a dozen of them, folded,
and he had his cap on and his coat open.
He saw Boyd from the door.
His feet did not stop and his face did.
It shut, as it shut on a sideline when a call went against him.
Then it opened again on purpose, and he came on down the corridor.

"Mr. Boyd," Dan said.

Boyd nodded.
He looked at the boxes against Dan's chest, and at the three by her door,
and at Dan.
It was one look, and it took as long as it took.
Aisha knew what he was pricing,
a coach who had liked his building in December,
now on her side of the lot with her boxes against his chest.
It was legal, staff helping staff in daylight with the door open,
and nothing a screenshot could hold.

Boyd went.
He went past Dan in the corridor with room to spare,
and the consultant turned the clipboard to his chest and went after him.
The warm-end door shut behind them both on its closer.

Aisha went to the window.
Her office window looked across the lot at the film-room window,
and she had looked out of it every night since the terms
to see when his went dark.
She looked out of it at three in the afternoon now.
Boyd came out from under the annex roof into the lot,
the consultant a step behind, and crossed it toward his truck.
There was a wind on the lot, a north one.
It got into the skirt of his coat and moved it.
Nothing else on him moved.
Not his hands in the pockets, not his head, not the length of his step.
He got to the truck and got in, and the consultant got in the other side,
and the truck went down the hill.

"Son of a bitch," she said, to the glass, quietly.

Dan was still in her doorway with the boxes.
He was too big for it and had knocked on the frame anyway, once,
with his elbow, because his hands were full.

"What did he say to you," Dan said.

"He liked my list."

She did not tell him the rest.
He looked at her face and knew there was a rest, and let it be.
Then he looked down at the boxes he was holding.

"Where do you want me."

He heard it as it came out.
He looked at the ceiling of her office, and at the boxes, and at her.

"The boxes. Where do you want the boxes."

She pressed her mouth flat and kept it flat,
because the trainer's cart was in the corridor
and a laugh in this building was a thing anybody could hear.

"On the floor, Coach. By the shelves."

"Facilities never sent a box to this building," he said,
putting them down.
"I had forty of these flat in the equipment cage. I walked them over."

"You walked them across the lot in the daylight."

"I walked them across the lot." He straightened up.
His eyes came to hers and stayed a second past what an open door allowed,
and did not move off.
"It's a lot, Doc. People walk it."

She let that stand.
She had told herself on Friday night, with the lamp on,
that he was going to be Coach in every room with a third person in it.
She had told herself she was not going to mind it.
She minded it. That did not change one thing about the boxes.

They reached for the tape gun on the desk at the same time
and both stopped with a hand out over it, and neither took it.
He put his hands on his hips.
She picked it up and held it out to him by the handle,
and he took it by the handle, and their hands did not meet.

The rhythm was December's and both of them had it in their hands.
He folded a box, taped the bottom, and turned it up.
She filled it, said what was in it, and typed it on the tablet,
the number, the contents, the date, her initials.
Then he taped the top and wrote the number on it in her marker,
in block letters that were nothing like hers.

"Forty-five," she said. "Reference. The orthopedics shelf."

"Forty-five." He wrote it.
"You've got more books than my building."

"Your building has a playbook and a Bible."

"It's got a very good playbook."
He grinned at her over the box with the marker in his teeth.
Then he took the marker out and put the grin away,
because the trainer went by in the corridor with the cart,
said "Coach," and did not slow down.

They did the shelves.
The binders went in forty-six, her own protocols,
the return-to-play ladder she had written in June
before anybody in this county had needed it.
She put her hand flat on the top one before he closed the lid.

"That one goes wherever I go."

He looked at her hand on the binder and then at her,
and he did not ask where that was, and closed the lid.

The diploma box was the last thing on the floor,
and he picked it up as it was.

"This one's already done," he said.

"It never got undone. I came in June and it stayed against that wall.
I know what they say. I never needed to look."

He held it a second longer than the lifting took,
and set it on the stack without a word.

She moved the stapler, and the memo was under it.
Five sentences, the one that said boxed by the eleventh,
and it had lived under her stapler since October.
She folded it once and put it in the parka's pocket.
The desk was going, and the memo was hers till the eleventh.
He watched her do it and said nothing about it, and that was right.

"Forty-seven," she said. "Desk. The top drawer."

"Forty-seven."

At half past four the tank went off down the corridor,
and the trainer went out the cold-end door with the kid on crutches.
The building was the two of them and eight boxes.
He stood in the middle of her office with nothing in his hands,
and looked at his hands, and put them on his hips again.

"That's what I had on me," he said.
"The rest of the flat ones are in the cage.
I'll walk them over tomorrow at three, if you want them."

"I want them."
She said it to him and not to the box.
"Dan." Low, once, because the building was empty and she could.
"You didn't have to walk them across that lot with his truck in it.
You saw the truck before you saw my door."

"I saw the truck.
I'd have walked them over if he'd been standing on the hood."
He looked at her and did not look away from it.
"Facilities was never going to send them.
He can price that however he wants to.
I've sat in a room and nodded at a drawing of this floor,
and I'm done nodding."

She knew the room.
Boyd's people had been in his film room the week before the game
with a drawing of a building on her ground.
He had sat in it and nodded, and he had never told her that.
He said it to the boxes and not to her.
Then he made himself look up, and it took him a second to do it.
It had cost him to hand her that, and she let him see that she had it.

"He'll price it. That's what he does."

"Let him. I'll be here at three with the tape gun.
Have the next ones ready to number."

She smiled at that, with her mouth shut, and let him see it.
He went out the warm-end door at a quarter to five
and across the lot to his own building,
because the town was home and those were his terms.
She watched him from the window.
He crossed the lot with his hands out of his pockets and his coat open.
The wind moved all of him, the coat, the cap, the walk.
She watched that until the Fieldhouse door took him.

The Blind Mule at nine on Monday night had the TV dark over the back bar
and one game going at the pool table.
Four men from the facilities crew were at the far end of the rail
with their coats still on.
Aisha took a stool at the near end by herself, with her keys in her hand.
She had never sat at this rail.
From the booth it had been a long piece of wood with Peanut behind it.
From the stool it was close, worn pale where the elbows went,
and Peanut Kyzer was right there.
He put a can of seltzer down in front of her
before she had asked for anything.
He had never served her at the rail, and he knew her can.
He had heard her order it once at the booth in December and kept it,
and that was the nearest thing to a welcome this bar gave.
She put her hand around it.

She was at the rail tonight because room nine had a lamp in it
that was his.
The town was home and he could not walk down to it.
She did not want to sit under a lit lamp with nobody coming.
That was the reason, and she had it, and it was enough.
DEC 19 was still up beside the register, circled, from before the game.
She had stood at her end of that field for all four quarters of it,
and nobody here had wiped the date off.

Peanut came back to her end with the towel over his shoulder.
He did not wipe anything and he did not lean.
He looked away from her first, down the length of his own bar,
the crew, the two at the table, the door.
She watched him take the room before he gave her a sentence of it.
The crew at the far end had been on the hill today.

"Boyd walk your building today."

It was not a question, and she did not treat it as one.
She looked at him and not at the can.

"He walked it."

"January's when the money says out loud what it whispered all fall."
He said it to the bar and not to her, and no louder than the rest.
"That money's whispered since September.
You'll hear it plain by spring. Everybody will."

He took the towel off his shoulder and went back up the pour line,
and did not look back to see what she did with it.
She drank the seltzer.
Peanut heard everything in this town and put none of it anywhere.
He had just set one thing in front of her on purpose,
the same as the can.
Her phone lit on the wood beside it.
His name.

*Eight boxes. Tape gun's in the truck, and I've got a marker of my own,
so you can keep yours. Three o'clock.*

She typed back in her own register, short and timed and finished.
She did not need to read his twice to answer it.

*9:40. The Mule, the rail, a seltzer. Three is fine. Bring the marker.*

Aisha sat on the end of the bed in room nine at ten
with her boots off and the phone in her hand, Dan's text still on it.
She was alone, and the furnace was running behind under the north wind.
She had turned the lamp on for him at nine
and pulled the curtain across it, so it showed nobody but the wall,
and he was not coming.
Those were the terms, and they held.

She thought about it in plain words, because that was how she thought.
He had knocked on her doorframe with his elbow, once, with his hands full,
and nobody had seen that but her.
He had said where do you want me and heard it.
He had walked back across the lot at a quarter to five,
because that was the arrangement till February.
Eight boxes. He was bringing the tape gun at three.
She would not have told him that she had looked for his stool
this morning before she looked for hers.

Boyd had said her name had come up out west.
A directorship. A department, a building with its name on it already.
A department with her name over it would keep her,
doctors under her and a building nobody was going to box.
That was the one thing three buildings had never given her.
The man offering it was the man who wanted the ground under this one,
and he was offering it so that she would go.
She thought about that once, here, on the end of the bed,
and set it down where it was, unanswered.
She was not going to answer it tonight.

What she was afraid of was plain too.
The building had five weeks.
Then the tank, the two tables, and the bike went somewhere
she had not been told,
and the job she was good at went with them or did not.
A man with his hands in his pockets had told her today
that he had somewhere for her to go.
She was good at this job.
She had been good at it in three buildings that had not kept her,
and she was good at it in this one, and this one had five weeks.

Up the hill the lot between the two buildings was dark and empty.
A man had crossed it at three with nothing on him moving,
and had not said one word about what he had seen.

Then she came back to him, because that was where she ended up.
He had stood in the middle of her office with nothing in his hands
and looked at his hands.
He had said the rest with the building empty,
and she had said his name once, low, and he had heard it.
Tomorrow at three he would come through the warm-end door
with the rest of the boxes.
She would say Coach and he would say Doc,
and she would let him carry every one of them.
