> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Tim Brasher:** Great win. Just want to say the staff has done a
> tremendous job this season.
>
> **Rhonda Sipes:** Agreed. Now let's see what they do with it.

The family lounge on the third floor had two lamps,
a coffee machine wearing an OUT OF ORDER sign gone soft
at the corners,
and a television running the highlights of the biggest win
of Dan Merritt's career with the sound off.

He watched himself take a bucket of ice water on the fifty
and could not remember it landing.

Out the window, twenty minutes up the highway,
the glow over the square was still going.
The county was having its night.
He was having this one,
in a room built for the kind of waiting
that doesn't improve with company.

Denny Gault was up again, pacing the short way,
window to door, a big man's version of sitting still.
"I want it on record that nobody in this family
is blaming a living soul," he said,
third time now, a little louder with each printing.
"You hear me, Coach? Not you. Not her. Nobody.
But somebody in this building can give me a ballpark,
and I have been asking since nine o'clock."

Missy Gault sat under the far lamp
with her coat folded over her lap
like she didn't plan on staying,
a spiral notebook from her purse open on her knee,
and she hadn't moved from that chair in three hours.
She watched Denny. She watched the door.
Every so often she wrote something down.
She had looked at Dan exactly twice,
the way you check a door you already locked.

Years of living rooms had taught Dan
that the loud ones were mostly the scared ones,
with better volume.
He had sat on flowered couches in five states
learning to hand a family exactly what it could carry,
and tonight the whole trade came down to a styrofoam cup.
He filled it at the machine by the elevators
and put it in Denny's hands,
because a man holding coffee paces slower,
and pulled the better chair around for Missy,
who did not use it.

"You've never heard me sell anything in your living room,
Denny," he said. "I'm not going to start in a hospital."

"I know it." Denny took the coffee.
"I know it. That's not—"
He stopped himself and drank instead.

"You're handling me, Coach."

"Little bit," Dan said. "It's what I've got on me."

The door opened at eleven-ten and it wasn't a nurse.

Aisha Cole came in still dressed for the sideline,
credential on a lanyard, tablet under her arm.
Sixteen hours on her feet, and the curls had come loose
from whatever she had tied them back with at noon,
and he noticed that, and then noticed that he had.
Denny set the cup down.
Missy did not move, except that all of her turned.

"Mr. Gault. Mrs. Gault."
Aisha stayed standing, which Dan understood.
You deliver standing.
"Here's everything I have, and I'll give you all of it.
He's stable. He's answering questions.
The imaging came back clean, and clean does not clear him.
A concussion doesn't show up on a scan.
Tonight is observation. Tomorrow we start testing him
against the baseline we took in August,
one step at a time, and his symptoms set the pace.
What I don't have is a date,
because the date does not exist yet.
His brain gets to write it, and it hasn't.
Anybody who hands you a timeline tonight
is selling you something.
You'd keep the number
and lose the part where it was a guess,
and in ten days the guess
would be standing in this room arguing with me.
I won't do that to him."

Denny stood very still,
a man reading a scoreboard he didn't like.
"The bracket," he said.
Not mean. Just out loud,
because it lived in the county's mouth
and his was part of the county.
The playoff. Two weeks out, the first one in county history,
and the boy down the hall was the reason they were in it.

"The protocol doesn't know about the bracket," she said.
"I do. I know exactly what this week is,
and I know what he did to get you all there,
and none of it moves a single step.
I'm sorry. That's the honest version.
Nobody knows yet."

Denny looked at Dan then,
and here was the thing Dan would have paid money
not to learn about himself in that particular minute:
his mouth was already loading the soft version.
A range. A "most guys." A "we'll know more Tuesday."
Years of it, smooth as a headset.
He looked at the doctor standing there
holding nothing but the truth,
at the exact hour the other thing
would have been cheapest,
and he shut his mouth
and let the silence run a full lap of the room.
It was the first time in his working life
he'd let a family leave without a number.

"Okay," Denny said finally, to the floor. "Okay."
Then, with his voice coming loose at last,
"That's my whole boy down that hall.
I'm not mad at anybody. That's the problem.
I want somebody to be mad at,
and there's nobody in the whole building,
and that's worse."

Dan believed him.
You could hear the love in the volume.

"I know," Aisha said.
"And every decision from here gets made
like he's mine.
That's not a comfort I'm offering you.
It's the job."

Missy clicked her pen.
"You were in with him at 9:12 and 9:40," she said,
reading it off the notebook like a deposition.
She looked up.
"What do I watch for tonight
that means I push the button
instead of waiting for morning?"

The room went still in a new way.

Aisha didn't blink.
"A headache that gets worse instead of better.
Vomiting more than once.
One pupil bigger than the other.
If he's hard to wake,
or anything that looks like a seizure —
you don't wait for morning,
you don't wait for me, you push it."
She nodded at the notebook.
"Keep that going. All of it, times and all.
In two weeks that notebook will tell me more
than anything I can order."

"I know it will," Missy said.
"That's why I'm keeping it."
She wasn't warm about it,
and it wasn't for anybody's benefit.
It was a woman who had decided at some point tonight
that if nobody could give her a date,
she would build the record the date would come from.

A nurse put her head in,
a chart hugged against her arm,
and said the room could take two more visitors
before the floor went quiet.
The Gaults went. Denny first, fast.
Missy stood, put the coat on,
and at the door she turned around,
and for a second Dan thought something was thawing.
It wasn't.
She looked at Aisha, level.
"He's asking about the game. In there."

"Tell him they won," Aisha said.
"That part's confirmed."

Missy nodded once
and went down the hall after her husband.

And then the lounge was two people,
a dead coffee machine,
and the silent television
starting the fourth quarter over.

Dan went down to the machine by the elevators,
which offered French vanilla or French vanilla,
and came back with two.
She took hers without looking up from the tablet
and drank half of it like it was part of the protocol.

"Sit down, Coach. You're looming."

He sat. The chairs were plastic
and had opinions about everybody.
"You could've given them a range," he said.
Not pushing it. He wanted to hear the shape of the thing.

"A range is a promise wearing a hedge.
They'd have kept the short end
and built a calendar on it."
She set the tablet down.
"You'd know. Your business invented the soft verbal."

"We did. Second you say probably, the kid hears yes.
His mama hears it in writing."

"Then you know why."

"I know why."

He took the card out of his jacket,
the one he scripted openers on,
and wrote NO CALENDAR WORDS across the back,
and she watched him do it
and didn't say anything about it.

"That was the best no I ever heard called," he said.

"It wasn't a no. It was the answer.
There's a difference,
and I need you of all people
to have it by Monday."

"I've got it tonight."

"Good. Then quit studying me like I'm film."

"I'm studying the cup."

"It's French vanilla, Coach. There's nothing on it."

"This is a bad room," he said.

"It's the best one on the floor."

"Then it's a bad floor."

She laughed, all of it at once, and he had not heard that before.
"It's a hospital."

Past the cold glass the glow was still up over the square,
and arguing with her about a room was the easy part of his night.

She looked at him over the cup.
"Question. And I need it straight, not loyal.
Is he a kid who'll tell me the truth about a headache?"

He gave it the extra second,
because she'd asked for straight,
and straight takes longer than loyal.
"No," he said.
"He's never been a backup in his life.
Kids like that answer symptom questions
like there's a depth chart in the room.
He'll tell you whatever gets him back in the huddle.
You'll want his roommate, and you'll want Missy."

"Missy," she said, and typed it. "Not Denny."

"Denny'd carry the stretcher himself
if he believed it was right.
But Denny believes out loud. Missy watches."

She typed that too.
"That's what I needed."

That should have been the visit.
The film wasn't going to grade itself,
the staff was due at seven,
and sixty kids would want his face calm by morning.

Neither of them got up.

Down the hall a cart went by on one clicking wheel.
Aisha closed her eyes.
Not asleep; her thumb kept going around the cup rim.
Just off duty for the length of a hallway.
He turned his cap over in his hands
and worked the brim like a rosary
and stayed where he was,
because getting up felt like a substitution
the situation hadn't asked for.

She spoke with her eyes still shut.
"You're supposed to be somewhere."

"Probably."

"The county's going to want you tonight, Coach.
Go be had."

"In a minute," he said,
and the minute ran long,
and neither of them called it.

When she finally stood and squared her coat,
she stopped in the doorway.
"Good win," she said.

Six months of her calls,
and that was the first one all season
he let stand without an argument.

"The backup won it," he said.
"I just held the sheet."

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a person
leaving a note in the margin of a chart,
and was gone down the hall.

***

The lot was down to a dozen cars and a hard little wind.
The biggest night of his career
smelled like hand sanitizer and French vanilla,
and up on three the bill for it
was lying in a dark room with the television off.

"Goddamn," he said to the empty lot. Once, quiet.

It didn't help, and he hadn't expected it to.
He got in the truck.

***

The Blind Mule at midnight held every soul in the county
who wasn't at the hospital or in bed,
and the mood had curdled somewhere between the two,
the fight song and the ambulance
trying to share one room.
Peanut Kyzer worked the taps
like a man running sixty conversations by pour alone.
The room found Dan before the door shut behind him.

He shook what needed shaking.
He said "proud of our kids" enough times
that it stopped sounding like words.
Twice somebody asked him how it felt,
and he gave them the face they'd come for,
because that was the job too,
and it cost less than the true answer.

"Any word on 7, Coach?" That was the rail, all of it,
one question wearing forty faces.

"Nobody knows yet," Dan said,
and heard whose sentence it was as he said it.
"That's not me holding back.
That's the whole answer there is tonight."

"Well," Sonny Dillard said,
"hospital jello never hurt nobody's throwing arm."

Nobody laughed. Sonny studied his beer.

Peanut came down the rail
and set a glass in front of Dan.
Whiskey, two fingers. No invitation, no bill.

"How're they taking it?" Dan asked him, low,
meaning the room, the county, all of it.

Peanut looked down his own bar for a while.
"This county will forgive you a loss
by Sunday dinner," he said.
"Being told to wait, they'll nurse till Easter."
He tapped the rail twice and moved off down the pour line.

Somebody started the fight song for the fourth time.
Dan raised the glass where raising it would be seen,
and stayed another half hour,
and inside, where nobody was watching,
he ran the trade the way he'd run any trade:
this night, the rail, the song, the bracket,
straight up for one healthy quarterback.
He'd have taken it before the coin came down.
He'd have taken it every time.
Nobody in this county could ever hear him say so,
starting with the ones singing.

***

His office at the Fieldhouse kept one lamp on
and the good kind of quiet.
He cued the film out of habit and didn't start it,
and got his phone out for the nightly read.

The board had a new thread on top, an hour old,
already carrying more replies than the win.

> **Denny G.** Proud of these boys tonight. Nobody prouder
> than this house, and nobody here is blaming a soul. Just
> asking for clarity on the evaluation timeline so families
> know what to expect going forward. That's all this is.
>
> **DeeAnn Prewitt** Praying so hard for #7 and his sweet
> family tonight. Does anybody know how long these
> evaluations usually run?
>
> **B. Hollis** Brother-in-law does sports medicine two
> counties over. Says days not weeks, most cases. Not saying
> that's this case. Just perspective for everybody.
>
> **PomPoms&Prayers** She is being SO careful and we all
> appreciate that!! Careful is right. Just hoping careful
> comes with a calendar, for the family's sake.
>
> **Denny G.** Nobody here is questioning anybody. We love
> this program. Clarity is not criticism.

He read it twice.
There was not one sentence in it
anybody could be made to apologize for.
It was polite the way weather is polite,
right up until it's on the radar.
And he knew the first voice.
He'd filled its cup at nine o'clock.
Denny Gault, who had not blamed a living soul all night
and wasn't lying about that.
He'd gone home and started it anyway.
Asking for a timeline was how this county went to war.
Politely, in writing, under its own name.

He put the phone face-down on the desk
and ran the film after all — the backup's drive,
eleven plays of a kid doing a job
nobody had promised him — and when the tape ran out,
the phone had thirty-one new reasons to turn back over.

He stood and killed the lamp.
Every night of his working life had ended
with that board read to the bottom,
and the board knew it, and waited.

Not tonight.

He picked the phone up without turning it over.
His last look at the board, unread, pocketed.
