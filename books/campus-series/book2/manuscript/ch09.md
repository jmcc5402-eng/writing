# Chapter 9 — The Wall in the Annex

> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **Rhonda Sipes:** Slept on it. Families deserved better than
> "it can live there."
>
> **Kendra Voyles:** It's Saturday, Rhonda.
>
> **Tim Brasher:** Nobody's questioning the medicine. The tone,
> maybe.


The tape gun ran out halfway across the fourth box,
and Aisha finished the seam off the spare roll
and typed the box's number into the tablet
before she wrote it on the cardboard.
It was Saturday, and there was no game,
and the annex was empty except for her
and thirty years of other people's paper.
Facilities was going to provide boxes.
Facilities' mailbox was still full,
so the boxes were hers, bought flat on her own card
and carried in from the truck two at a time.
Merritt had asked her Wednesday to wait before she carried anything,
and she had not waited,
and what went into the boxes was going to be evidence.
Some day somebody with a title would ask her
why she had held the boy out of a game the whole county wanted,
and when they asked, this paper would answer,
in order, in her own typing, with nothing missing from it.
Under that, two nights old,
the parents' board was still saying she talked down to families.
She had read it at six with her boots on.
She had not answered it, because she did not post,
and because it was not a question.

Saturday was the roster's off day.
The annex had no reason to be open,
and it was open anyway, the corridor lights off,
the records room's one bulb on,
the cold coming up through the floor into her socks.

Four cabinets. Two walls of boxes that were already boxes,
her predecessors' handwriting going back past ballpoint.
The X-ray film in its brown sleeves.
No machine on this campus could read that film anymore,
and she was not going to throw it out
just because nobody could.

She did the cabinets by drawer,
the drawers by folder, the folders by year,
and every folder went into the tablet before it went into a box.
Name, year, box number, her initials.
Chain of custody. Nobody had asked for it, and nobody would till they needed it.

The carton marked KNEES, 1994 went into the tablet as itself.
She did not open it.
Whoever had taped it had wanted it shut,
and the inventory said KNEES, 1994, one carton, sealed,
and that was the truth about it.

The parka went with her drawer to drawer.
Team issue, navy, her name over the patch in block letters,
the cuffs rolled twice and holding, like the cage had promised.
It was a good coat for a cold room.
She had quit noticing it was on.
That was the most a coat could do.

Friday's check had run at five fifteen, on the turf, printed.
She had read the sheet standing in the wind,
and the numbers were right,
and neither of them had said the word porch.
He had said, Doc.
She had said, Coach,
and Merritt had gone back down the field at volume,
and she had stood there a second with the sheet
and thought about the rail.
Not the hedge. She had already spent Thursday on the hedge.
The other line, the small one,
that she had not answered either.
*So would I.*
Dan Merritt had said it to a yard, looking at the yard,
and it had stopped her where she stood,
and she had covered it with a joke about a check
and gone and eaten one bite of a rib
and left the plate on his rail with the rest of it.
She could still see her plate there.
One rib. One bite gone.
It was a stupid thing to keep, and she kept it.

***

The top screw came out on the third turn
and the bottom one did not come at all,
so she worked the hook back and forth on it
until the plaster gave up the screw and some of itself.
The hook was brass gone brown, one prong,
screwed into the wall by the door at hand height,
and it had held nothing the whole time she'd had the building,
because the one key anybody had handed her
had come without a hook and gone on her ring.
Everything came off the walls. That was the rule she'd set at seven.
Everything off the walls went in the tablet.

Behind the hook the wall was not a wall.
The screw had been holding in a patch,
plaster over lath over a gap the size of a hand,
and when the patch came away with the screw
the gap was there, dark, with something pale standing up in it.

She got it out with two fingers.

An envelope, small, the kind that came with a card once,
gone the color of tea, soft at the corners,
never stamped, never mailed.
Somebody had walked it down a hall and put it in a wall.
Across the front, in a hand nobody was taught anymore,
all loops and pressure, the ink gone brown,
it said *For the night nurse.*
On the back flap, where people put their own name
when a letter was going by hand, was the name.
Cordelia Hartwell.
Hartwell, like the House at the top of Millrow.
Aisha knew the name from wine nights
and had never heard it said about a woman.

She opened it because it was open.
The flap had lost its glue years back
and lifted on its own.
One sheet, folded twice, the brown ink again.
She read it once, standing by the door
with the hook still in her other hand.

It did not take long.
When she was done she stood there and did not read it again,
and found she was breathing carefully,
and could not have said when she had started.
It was a letter from a woman who was dead
to a nurse who had worked nights in this building,
and it had been put in the wall to be found,
or to not be, and she could not tell which.
It had not been written to her, and she was in it anyway,
because what it was about was being wanted in this building on purpose,
and nobody had ever put that in writing for her before.
She had come in this morning to make a record
nobody could argue with.
Somebody had left one in the wall before her,
in a hand nobody wrote anymore.

That was as much as she let herself think about it,
and it was more than she had thought about anything
that was not the boy's brain or the paper on it since the freeze.

The parka had an inside pocket, left side, zippered.
She had not used it for anything.
The letter went into it and the zipper went up over it.
She put the hook in the tablet. One brass key hook, removed.
She did not put the wall in the tablet.
She did not put the letter in anything.
She had found a letter from a dead woman
in the wall of her building, and she was keeping it,
and she was not going to tell anybody that.
It was the first thing in the annex that was not on the inventory,
and it was in her coat, against her ribs, and she left it there.

***

The used towels made an armload that kept coming apart,
and Aisha got them down the corridor by walking fast
and let them go on the whirlpool room's floor,
where Verna Poteat was already counting the clean ones
onto the table in stacks of two.
The outside door had gone at the cold end
while Aisha was still zipping the pocket,
and she had shut the records room behind her on the way out.

Verna had ridden up with Sonny Dillard on his way to a shift,
towels and all, and Sonny had gone on,
and she had walked the last of it from the lot in the fur collar.
The arrangement was new, and it was hers.

"Fourteen," Verna said. "You had twelve.
Two are mine till you lose them,
and then they were always yours.
Your college doesn't know it has a whirlpool up here.
I do. These go down to my washers on Saturdays
and come back up, and that's all it is."

"What do I owe you for it."

"Twelve back."

"I don't lose towels."

"Everybody loses towels."

Aisha squared the used pile with her foot.
Verna looked at the whirlpool, and the cot with its army blanket,
and the parallel bars, and then at the coat.

"You've still got the sleeves rolled."

"They hold."

"Tomorrow, after church. Pins. I said."

"I remember."

"Mm." Verna sat on the end of the cot, in her coat,
and did not look like a woman who was leaving.
"That was some porch Thursday."

"It was cold."

"It was that." Verna looked at the whirlpool again.
"You didn't fight anybody.
You told a woman what a word meant
and you told her where to find you.
I was on the step. I heard the whole thing twice,
once off you and once off the trucks."

"I read the board. Rhonda Sipes slept on it."

"Rhonda's been up since five. I know her porch light."
She smoothed the army blanket beside her with one hand.
"I put you on that board.
That coach sat there three seconds with a live microphone
and didn't say one word.
I have watched that man on a sideline all fall
and I have never known him to do nothing for three seconds.
You might ask yourself what he was doing instead."

Aisha folded a towel that was already folded.

"Sonny's got me at four on his way back," Verna said,
and stood up. "I'm not walking that hill in this."

She was at the door of the room when she stopped,
one hand on the frame,
and said it to the corridor and not to Aisha.

"Cordelia Hartwell stayed in room nine.
I don't say that to everybody."

The letter was against Aisha's ribs, under the zipper,
and she could feel the exact size of it.
She had the name in her mouth. Hartwell.
She had it in her coat, in a hand Verna would have known.
She could have said, I found something,
and Verna would have sat back down on the cot,
and the town would have had it by supper.

"I know the name," Aisha said. "From the House."

"That's the one," Verna said, and went down the corridor,
and the outside door did its two-stage bang,
and the letter stayed where it was, and so did Aisha.
She was not going to tell Verna.
She was not going to tell anybody.

***

The eggs came before she had the cuffs rolled,
and she ate them at the second stool with the coat zipped
and the Liars' Table full at the window,
the Saturday paper going around it a section at a time.
On Wednesday the Table had had jokes for her, and today it had the paper.
A man came in for a to-go order
whose forearm she had dressed at the bonfire's burn table,
and he paid, and took his sack,
and looked past her at nothing on his way out.
The den mother came in behind him with two of the Scouts
and nodded at Aisha from the door,
one nod, all the way down and back.

"He still shows that thumb to everybody," she said on her way past.

"Tell him it healed because he held still."

That was the town, two nights after the porch.
One of them looked past her and one of them did not,
and she had treated both.

Earlene Tatum came up the counter for the pot
and stopped at Aisha's stool on her way back with it.

"You've got plaster on you."

Aisha looked at her sleeve. She did.

"Inventory," she said.

"Up there?"

"Up there."

"By yourself."

"By myself."

That was twice in three days she had given that answer,
and the first time had been to Merritt, over a box, in her own office.

Earlene held the pot and looked at her a while.
"That building was the WPA's infirmary
before the college ever wanted it,
and the fevers came through it when they came.
That annex buried more fevers than the hospital ever did.
Buildings remember."

"I hadn't heard that."

"Now you have. Nobody up at that college does."
Earlene went back down to the Table with the pot.
"Eat your eggs."

***

The truck was in second by the first block and still in second at the top,
because she had turned up Millrow at Delmar's
instead of across the square toward the motel,
and Millrow did not care what gear you took it in.
She had done it on purpose,
to look at the house with the letter's name on it.
She could have gone back to nine.
The motel was six minutes the other way.

Hartwell House sat at the top where the street ran out,
wrapped to the eaves in blue tarp and scaffold,
the tarps lifting and settling in the wind.
A crew was on the east side, four of them up on the boards,
and the low sun came through the scaffold
and lay across the tarp in bars.

Marisol Pruett came down the scaffold path from the porch,
the plank walk the crew had laid along the east wall,
with the steel thermos of coffee in one hand
and a stack of cups in the other,
and Cal Sutter came down off the boards to meet her at the bottom
and took his cup.
Whatever he said was two words.
Whatever she said back was one.
He drank. She went on down the line of them with the thermos.
That was all of it.

Aisha knew both of them. She had sat at their table.
She could have put the truck in the drive
and been handed a cup she was not going to drink
and stood in the cold with people who were glad to see her.
She let the truck roll on past the drive
to where Millrow ran out of pavement,
turned it around in the gravel,
and came back down past the House with the sun behind her,
and did not look at it twice.

***

The zipper was already down and the letter already out
by the time the door of nine was shut behind her,
and she stood with it in the lamplight and did not open it.
She had read it once.
A thing like that did not get better on a second read.
It got smaller, or it got bigger,
and she did not want either one tonight.

The wool coat hung on the closet rod with its month-old woodsmoke.

She put the letter in the wool coat.
The inside breast pocket, buttoned,
the one she had never had a use for.
The city coat, the one the wind went through,
the one thing in the room that smelled like the county.
That was where it would live.
She would not have to look for it.

A woman named Hartwell had slept in this room once.
Verna had said so, to the corridor,
and Aisha had let it go by with the letter against her ribs.

Then she stood in the middle of the room with the team's coat still on,
and let him into the room,
because there was nobody in it to see her do it.
She wanted him.
She had said so to herself at the whiteboard
and she had not taken it back,
and it had gone exactly nowhere since,
and it was not going to start now, on a Saturday, in a motel,
with a dead woman's letter in her other coat.
It was not a thing she was going to tell anybody.
Not Verna, who had all but asked.
Not him.

*It won't be the last,* he had said on the rail,
about agreeing with her.
She would like to see him try.
She would like it more than she had let herself find out.

She took her boots off.
She had read the board at six with them on
and had not had them off since.
She set them under the rack, side by side,
where they would be in the morning.
Tomorrow, after church, there would be pins,
and after the pins the next check,
five fifteen on the turf, printed,
and Merritt across it from her with the sheet between them.
