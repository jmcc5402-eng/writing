>
> **Kendra Voyles:** My son and his roommate have been at war over the
> dorm thermostat since August. Today he sent a picture of the roommate
> asleep in a ski mask. Apparently we're winning.


The plate came down on the Checkerboard's counter at Aisha's elbow
with a knock she felt through the wood,
and the cook was back at the griddle before she had turned her head.
It was the Coach's Slice, Dan Merritt's own order,
and she had not asked for it.
She took it as the cook's way of saying sorry about her leave without a word.
Eggs hit the griddle with a sound like rain starting.
Earlene Tatum came up the counter with the pot and filled Aisha's cup,
looked at her once, on her side and not caring who saw it,
and took the pot back to the window table.
Aisha had always carried her own cup to the urn.
Eight days from this Tuesday, the twenty-sixth of January,
an independent review would say whether Aisha had been right
to hold Trey Gault out of a playoff game in December,
and until it did she was a team physician on leave with no team.

For a second she wanted Dan on the stool nearest the register with his cup,
his shoulder a foot from hers and nothing said,
and the want came up under her ribs like a breath she could not let out.
At half past seven the radio over the pass-through went to the news,
a man's voice on 1340, flat and quick,
and the counter went quiet to hear it.

"The independent review of the university's team physician
called in head coach Dan Merritt on Monday,
sources close to the review say.
Asked whether, knowing what he knows now,
he would have played the quarterback in the December nineteenth playoff,
Merritt said, quote, 'Every coach in the country plays him.
I'd have played him.'
The athletic department said this morning,
'Coach Merritt has full confidence in the independent review process.'"

The department had called its statement on the twentieth the only one,
and this was a second, with his name in it and none of his voice.
It did not say one word of his was wrong.

The fork had stopped in the air over the plate.
Heat came up the back of her neck into her face all at once,
and the griddle went far off behind the blood in her ears.
Her fingers shut on the fork until the metal bit her palm.
The radio had said it in a stranger's flat voice.
She heard it again in Dan's, low, the way he talked in the dark,
and that was worse.

*I knew it.*
It came up whole in her own voice, and it was not sad.
It was furious.
Nothing was ever really going to happen for her, not here and not anywhere,
and she had let herself forget that for one December
because of one man with a quiet voice and big hands.
She had let him into nine with the lamp on and the curtain shut.
The first time it cost him anything he had sat down with the review
and told it she was wrong,
and this morning the county had it over its eggs at every counter in town.

Twelve days ago on this same station
he had said *her protocol* to every radio in the county,
and she had let it into her chest and kept it there.
Nobody had ever said a thing for her out loud before.
Now the same station had him again, in eleven words.
She had never been fired, and she had never been kept.

She picked up the cup.
It shook, and the coffee chattered on the saucer, loud in her own ears.
She set it down with both hands so it would quit.
Her throat had shut on something she could not swallow.
Her eyes went hot,
and she held them open on the pie until the heat went back down.
Her heart was going hard and high under her collarbone,
and she could hear it over the fan.
She did not let the room see it.

Two stools down a man in a feed-store cap, in from the county for eggs,
set his cup on its saucer.
He said it to the man beside him, not loud.

"I'd want her careful with mine. I would.
But any other doctor in this league has 7 back for that game,
and now the coach says so himself. He'd know.
He's the one has to put the boy out there."

She did not answer him.
He was not a mean man, and he was not wrong about the other doctors.
She had known that in December and held Trey anyway.
Fourteen minutes into the bike test his hand had gone up to his head.
His brain was not healed yet, and the test had shown it.
The radio went on to the weather.

"Cold rain ending by noon. Clearing this afternoon,
and a hard freeze tonight, so mind your pipes."

The cook wanted a quiet griddle and did not turn around.
The slice stayed at her elbow, and she did not eat it.
She could not have got a bite past her throat,
and she was not going to push Dan's order away with that man listening.
When she put money down for the coffee,
the cook came down the counter
and pushed it back across the wood with two fingers.
The town was kind to her at the counter
and against her on the radio over it,
and she sat between the two of them until her cup was empty.

She walked the three streets back from the square in the last of the rain,
and by nine she was on the end of her bed at the Magnolia Court.
At five past, the offer came in writing,
an email from the chair of the department out west.

*Dear Dr. Cole,*

*I'm glad to put in writing what we talked about. We would like you to
take the directorship. The department is yours to build, and the building
is ready. We would need your answer by Saturday, February 6.*

At the word *directorship* her breath went out of her.
For a second she was glad all the way through,
in her chest and down into her hands,
and she hated being glad this morning.
It was what she had wanted since the first city she ever unpacked in,
a place that would keep her.
Rex Boyd had put her name in front of the school out west so she would leave.
Nobody had told her why. It was a guess, and she trusted it.
The review could clear her on Wednesday the third,
and she would still lose the job.
At the February sitting the trustees would hand the annex's ground to his pledge
and her job to the group that came in with it.
Saturday the sixth was the clock that mattered, eleven days off,
and she felt it low under the ribs.

She had Dan's name up on the screen before she had decided anything,
and her thumb sat over it.
She scrolled their thread back to the night before the boxes.

*Eight boxes. Tape gun's in the truck, and I've got a marker of my own,
so you can keep yours. Three o'clock.*

She could hear him say it, pleased with himself about the marker,
and it was the voice under the radio's at half past seven,
and her chest went warm and sore at once.
Her heart went fast and high, the way it had gone on the hill in his truck.
She was angry at him, and she wanted him, and both were true at once.
If the phone had lit with his name she would have answered
before the first ring was done.
She put it down on the blanket.

She was not going to call him, and she knew why.
At the car window he had chosen the job,
and she had let him.
In the rain all he had for her was when film started,
but for the review he had found eleven words about her with no trouble at all,
and this morning the county had them.
A doctor on leave who called the coach eight days before the findings
handed the county its caption.
If anybody caught her and Dan together in the season,
the university would fire them both the same day.
He worked fourteen hours a day, every month of the year but one,
and she was not going to be the phone call that cost him the job.

[TK the western university; its chair; how the chair reached her]


At one she drove up Millrow in the wool coat with an empty box on the seat,
and the gutters ran loud enough to hear through the glass.
The key was still on her own ring,
and she let herself in at the warm end, where her office was.

The man the department had sent to cover her
came out of the whirlpool room and stopped.
He wanted no trouble.

"Doctor. They told me the office was cleared."

"The clinic's is boxed. I'm getting what's mine. Half an hour."

"Take what you need, ma'am.
I'm not going to be the one who says anything about it."

The trainer was in the rehab-room door with a roll of tape.
He had a job to keep and a building full of people
watching which door he stood in, so he stayed in that one.

"Doc," he said.

His face went red to the ears,
and he looked down at the tape and not at her.

Her office had one desk and the clinic's numbered boxes along the wall,
and those stayed with the clinic.
The last box was numbered in Dan's marker, blocky and pressed hard,
the marker of his own he had texted her about.
Her throat went tight, and she put two fingers on the number.
Then she turned the box around so his number faced the wall,
and left it there with the clinic's.
The diploma box stood on its edge against the baseboard,
taped since June, and she took it the way it was.
The boots, the charger, and the sweater went in the other.
Seven months of hers fit in two boxes, and one of them had never been opened.

Trey's paper chart was in the shut drawer.
She put her hand flat on the drawer front and left it there.
The chart was the clinic's,
and a doctor on leave did not carry a patient's chart down the hill.
The cover man watched from the office door as she took her hand away.

Then she went down the corridor for Verna's towels.
Twelve were owed back, and ten were on the shelf by the roaring tank.
She was folding the tenth in thirds, the way Verna's went back,
when the trainer came in behind her with the other two
from the rehab tables, folded to match.
He set them on her stack with his back to the corridor,
and he was in his door again before she could say a word.

Outside, Dan's truck sat in the Fieldhouse lot
across from the survey flags, nose to the fence.
Her chest went tight at it, and her hands went tight on the box.
She wanted to cross the lot
and say one sentence to him through his window,
and she did not know whether the sentence was angry
or something she had no word for yet.
She put the two boxes on the back seat of the RAV4 with the towels on top
and did not cross.


At three she drove across town to Mt. Nebo Missionary Baptist,
where the annex's forty-one boxes of records had sat in a locked room
since December.
Verna had phoned Delores for her that morning, and Delores had said three.
If the review asked where every page was,
her copy of the count had to match Delores Fountain-Mabry's pad.

The fellowship hall was long and plain,
with folding chairs in rows for a meeting.
By the pass-through the men's choir had a sign-up
with two lines blank under TENOR.
Delores had the fifth-grade contest essays squared on a table,
her key on its ribbon beside it.

"Doctor," Delores said. "Read me yours, and slow."

Aisha read her copy out loud, and Delores ran her pen down the pad.

"Thirty-one," Aisha said.

"Thirty-one."

Dan had carried thirty-one down this hall in December
and set it on thirty, saying each number as he went by her
with his breath still white from the lot.
His voice came back to her low, the voice that had talked to the review,
and heat went up her throat.
She wanted to shake him for this morning.
She wanted him going by her again with a box,
close enough to feel the cold coming off his coat.

"Forty-one," Aisha said.

"Forty-one." Delores put a check at the bottom of the pad.

"The review may want your pad," Aisha said.

"Then the review can come to this hall and ask me for it.
It doesn't leave the church. I heard the radio this morning."
She did not look up from the pad.
"A coach can play that boy all he likes in his own head, Doctor.
The boy's own head was yours to answer for."

It was the first thing anybody had said for her since half past seven,
and Aisha's throat closed on it.

The far door banged, and Odell Fountain came down the hall
in his coat over a barber's smock, smelling of talc and clipper oil.
He cut hair on Fountain Row and was a deacon here,
and he was judging the essays, late.

"Delores, I have a man in my chair with half a haircut
and forty essays on your table. Tell me which one I'm late for."

Delores did not look up.
Aisha felt her mouth start to go,
and for a second, on the worst day of her month, she nearly smiled.
Odell saw it and looked pleased with himself.
He sat down and squared the stack again, though it was already square.

At a quarter past four she was back at the Magnolia Court,
with the sun out low over the gravel for the first time in a week.
Sonny Dillard, who did Verna's small repairs,
was on his knees at the office step getting it poured while the sun held,
his trowel going scrape and slap.

"Forty-three on the office thermometer," Sonny said. "I'll take it."

She left the two boxes on the back seat
and carried the twelve towels past him in their thirds.
Sonny looked at the stack and at her, and went back to the step.
She put them in Verna's washers behind the office,
and the count was square.
Verna Poteat was in the laundry door, looking at the thirds.

"Mm," Verna said.

Aisha followed her through to the office.
February's rent was in an envelope in Aisha's coat,
because rent at the Court was by the month.
"It's February's," Aisha said, and set it on the counter.
Verna pushed it back across with one finger.

"The heater rattled," Verna said.

The furnace in her room ran behind when the wind came north,
but Aisha had never heard it rattle.
Verna wanted her tenant kept.
The rattle was her story,
so she could take nothing for February from a doctor on leave
without either of them calling it anything.
A motel in January had no month's rent to spare.
Aisha put the envelope back inside her coat
beside the other one, the color of tea,
because Verna had already turned to the window.
Out that window the low sun lay on the gravel by her door,
where Dan's truck had sat with the engine running the night of the ice.
Her hand had gone over his on the bench there,
and his had turned up under it and closed.
Her palm remembered it, cold from the wheel and then not,
and her pulse came up in her throat.
The warm of it ran up the inside of her wrist.
Her chest went tight, and she could not see the gravel for the cab.


By nine at night the sky had cleared,
and the gravel outside her door had frozen hard enough to crack under a boot.
She had been in her room since Verna's office, coat on,
because the wind had come north with the freeze,
and the furnace ran behind.
When she took the wool coat off at last she felt the letter against her ribs,
buttoned into the inside breast pocket where it had ridden all day,
and she sat on the bed with a can of sparkling water on the sill
and unbuttoned the pocket.
The envelope was the color of tea,
with *For the night nurse* on the front in brown ink
and Cordelia Hartwell on the flap, Hartwell like the House,
from a woman writing to a nurse who had worked nights in the annex.
She read to the end.

*You keep asking what I stayed for, as if a town were a reason.
It was never the town. It was being needed by it on purpose.*

Her eyes went hot, and this time there was nobody to hold them open for.
The ache came up under her breastbone and sat there, and she let it.
Nobody had needed her here on purpose.
The county had needed a doctor who said yes.

What she wanted was up the hill in a truck.
What she could do was go, and her going would cost him nothing.
That was the part that burned.
He had made his choice at the car window.
She was the one packing for it.
She was taking the offer.
She typed the answer under the email.

*I accept the directorship. A. Cole, MD.*

She did not send it.
They had asked for Saturday the sixth, and she kept dates.

Then she had Dan's name up again, the second time that day,
and her thumb stayed over it until the screen went dark on its own.
The phone went once in her lap.
It was Kat up the road, who had been sending her houses since November.

*Honey. The farmhouse dropped its price. The roof did not get any better.
Friday?*

Kat did not know about any offer.
Aisha laughed once through her nose, and it hurt,
and she typed *Not this Friday* and sent it.

She packed the room in nine minutes, the way she had timed it in June,
and left out what she needed until the sixth.
Then she took the parka off the rod
and folded it to go back to the equipment cage,
because it was the team's coat and she was going.
Sleeves in, hood down, in half and in half again, with the name up.
She did it hard, and the nylon snapped under her hands.
She had worn it in his truck the night of the ice, warm to the chin.
Her neck went hot the way it had at the counter this morning,
and the heat did not cool.
It turned into wanting him.
She found she had the folded coat pulled in against her chest,
and her heart was knocking on the name.
Her pulse beat hard against the zipper.
She held it longer than she meant to.
Then she held it longer than that.
She wanted the man who had told the county she was wrong,
and wanted him harder for being angry,
and she did not understand it.
Then she folded the hood in again where her arms had crushed it.

She carried the suitcases out one at a time,
and the frozen gravel cracked under her boots
while she put them in the hatch
and laid the parka flat on the top box on the back seat.
The hatch came down with a sound that went to the end of the lot,
and a car loaded in Verna's gravel at night
was a thing the county would read by morning.

The storm door opened at the end of the lot,
because Verna had heard the hatch.
She stood in it in her cardigan with the office light behind her,
and Aisha went across the gravel to the foot of her step,
her breath going white in front of her.

"I've watched a good many people load a car in that gravel,
my winter doctor," Verna said.
"It goes quickest when there's nobody asking them not to."

"I know it," Aisha said.
"I've had that car half loaded since June, Verna. Tonight I finished it."

Verna did not answer that.
She did not go back inside, and she did not shut the door.
She wanted her winter doctor to stay, and she would not ask.

From the foot of Verna's step Aisha could see into the back of the RAV4,
where the light from Verna's open door came through the side glass
and lay on the parka she had folded on the top box.
She would have stayed if he had asked her.
She had not let herself know it until the hatch came down,
and she knew it now at the step, with Verna in the door above her,
not asking either.
He had not asked.
He had told the county he would have played Trey, and she was going.
The name was up, A. COLE in block letters over the staff patch,
folded flat on the top box and ready to go back.
