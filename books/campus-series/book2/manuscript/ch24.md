The wipers had not moved since the ice,
and the first pass smeared the whole windshield.
Dan Merritt ran them twice more going up Millrow before the glass came clear.
It was raining.
It was the first rain since the storm,
straight down and light, with no wind in it,
and the hill ran with it in both gutters.
This was Wednesday, the twentieth of January, before seven in the morning.

Monday night Dan had opened the board
and read the picture Rhonda Sipes had put up with the time under it.
Rhonda was a parent, out of state, in for every home game.
The picture was her laugh and Dan's lean over a kid's sign on an ice chest,
at eleven on the morning of the game Trey sat out.
Tuesday he had put fourteen hours into the Fieldhouse.
Ty had brought him the aldermen's vote at the side door at eight, going out.
Now it was Wednesday, and he had the key out before the truck was off.

The phone went in his coat at the side door.
The email was the athletic director's, in the shape all of his had.
No header. No signature. Two names in the address line.
His was first. Hers was under it.

*Effective this morning Dr. Cole is suspended from her duties as team
physician, pending an independent review of the December medical
determination. The review will report its findings within fourteen
days. Until it reports, this office will make the department's only
statement, at ten this morning, and no member of the football staff
will make another. Dr. Cole has been asked to be out of the annex by
noon today.*

He read it in the rain.
The fourteenth day from today was the third of February, a Wednesday,
and it was signing day, the day the class put its names on paper.
He read it twice.
The rain ran out of his hair onto the phone,
and he wiped the glass with his thumb and read her name again.
Her name under his on a page from that office did something in his chest.
It was not a hard thing. It was low, and it stayed there.

Nobody else knew yet.
Two people in the county had this email, and they were the two in the picture.
The review was the paper.
In February the trustees could hand her job to the group
with the review as the reason,
and her name would not have to come up at that table.
If the school could prove the picture, it did not need a review.
It could fire a coach and a physician on the same morning, and it would.
He turned the key and went in, and he did not read it a third time.

The lawyer and the press woman were in his office door before eight.
Dan was at his desk with the January roster,
and the rain was on the window.
The athletic director's office had sent them up.
The lawyer was the university's, a man in a gray suit with no folder.
The press woman was the department's, with a tablet against her chest.
The lawyer shut the door.
Every door in the Fieldhouse stood open by Dan's rule, and Dan let him shut it.

The lawyer wanted nothing said, by anybody, ever.
The press woman wanted the athletic director's words to be the only words.
Neither of them was wrong, and Dan knew it.

"You've read it," the lawyer said.

"I've read it."

"Then you know the statement goes out at ten,
and it's his words and nobody else's.
Between now and the findings you don't speak to the review.
Not to the board, not to the radio, not to a parent in a parking lot.
Not to her."

"She's my team physician."

"She's the subject of an independent review, Coach,
and you're the man in the picture with her."
The lawyer said it flat, and it was not unkind.
"Anything you say about Dr. Cole makes it worse for her.
A coach standing up for the doctor is the caption on that picture.
It makes the review about the two of you
instead of about a number in December.
I'm not telling you that for the school. I'm telling you because it's true."

It was true. That was the trap in it.
Dan had had a sentence in his head since the side door,
and the front half of it was out of his mouth before he knew it would be.

"In December she looked at a number on a screen and said no," Dan said.
"She was right."
He had the rest of it in his mouth, the half with his name in it.

"And the next half is your name beside hers," the lawyer said.
"That's the sentence they're waiting on, Coach.
Say it on the radio at ten,
and every trustee reads the findings on the third as a favor to you.
It's the picture again, in words, and you put it there."

Dan shut his mouth on the back half of the sentence,
and both of them saw him do it.

"It's four sentences," the press woman said.
"It doesn't say suspended. It doesn't say her name, and neither do you.
Not today, not on the third, not after."

His hands were on the desk.
He took them off it and put them in his coat pockets,
because a man with his hands in his pockets was not reaching for anything.

He was in the Fieldhouse fourteen hours today,
and fourteen tomorrow, and every day after, eleven months of the year.
That was the job, and it was also what he was.
A sentence with her name in it cost the job today.
He had been sat down once already in his life, and a coach did not get a second one.
He was choosing what he was. He knew it while he did it.

"All right," Dan said.

The lawyer nodded once and stood and opened the door,
and the corridor came back in, a cart going by, the rain.
Twelve minutes, and he had agreed to it.


At ten the statement went out.
It went up on the department's page under the athletic director's name,
and 1340 read it on the air at a quarter past.
Dan was at his desk with the statement open and the parents' board beside it,
and by eleven he had the town room open too.
He read a board for the football hidden in the ordinary talk,
because a board had known what he was once before anybody said it to his face.
A head job at this level was the dream job,
and a man fought for it every day of the year.
Somebody was always trying to pull him down,
and a board was where they started.

Rhonda's post from Monday was still up, the picture and the time under it.
The town room was for lost dogs and road work,
and it had never had the doctor in it.
By eleven it did. DeeAnn Prewitt had carried the picture over.
DeeAnn was local, a real name,
in her car at practice every afternoon in season with the heat on.
She wanted to be first to know a thing and first to tell it.

> **DeeAnn Prewitt** For everybody on here who doesn't do the parents'
> board. This is the team doctor they put on review this morning. Look
> at the date under it. That's the game the boy sat out. I'm just
> putting it where people can see it.
>
> **PottsRoadWatch** This is the lost dog room, DeeAnn. Take it back
> where you got it.
>
> **JanetOnElm** Some of us are on here for the recycling schedule. Is
> it Thursday this week or not.

Eleven replies by eleven, and not one about the doctor.
The town stepped around DeeAnn's post to get to the recycling schedule.
The county could hate the doctor fine in the rooms built for it.
This room was for lost dogs,
and it turned on DeeAnn for tracking the doctor in on her shoes.
Its verdict on the doctor was a shrug.
A shrug was worse than a fight.
It got into him on her account, cold at the back of the neck.
The town room could read her name at eleven and go back to its recycling.
At eleven she was across the lot with one box to fill,
and he did not know if she had the same post up on the tablet.
He wanted her not reading it,
or reading it with him on the other side of her desk.

Four posts down was the vote, one line, *Millrow rezoning passed*,
and three replies under it about where to park.
Tuesday night the aldermen had rezoned the town's side of Millrow
so Rex Boyd's glass building could cross the street.
The money wanted her rooms for the building
and her job for the group that rode in with the pledge,
and the trustees voted on both in February.
The ground under her clinic was the university's,
and no alderman could touch it.
Nobody in the town room had put the street and the doctor in one sentence.

He shut the town room and left the board up.

At noon Dan was at the film-room window, the one on the lot, on purpose.
The rain was still coming straight down and light,
and the lot had water standing in the ruts.
The survey flags from Monday stood in the water at the fence line, orange.
The RAV4 was at the annex's warm end with the exhaust going white behind it.
Aisha was carrying one box to it.
She was in the parka with the hood up.
The box was against her hip, the tablet on top
and a mug on its side in one corner.
That was the day's box. The office was not in it.
The trainer stood in the warm-end door behind her, hands in his pockets,
in the door and not on the step.
At the fence a staff car sat with its wipers going and somebody behind them.
The trainer and whoever the department sent were the clinic for the fortnight.

He went down the corridor and out the side door into the rain.
The lot was water and not glass,
and his boots went through it to the ankle in the ruts.
The rain came down on his bare head and ran in under his collar.
He crossed the lot in daylight, at noon,
with the trainer in the door and a car at the fence,
after a morning of being told not to.

She had the box on the passenger seat and both hands on the wheel,
with the engine running and the heat on.
He could see the vent's air moving her curls inside the hood.
He put one knuckle to the glass, once.
The other hand went flat against her door, below the glass, and stayed there.

The window came down four inches and stopped.

Four inches of her face. That was all of it, and it went through him.
His breath went out in the rain and did not come back on its own.
His heart came up hard under the wet coat,
and he felt it in the hand on her door.
The warm came out of the four inches, the car's heat and hers.
It hit his mouth and his chin
and went down his throat like a swallow of something.
His stomach dropped.
His right hand wanted the door handle, and he knew it wanted it.
He put it in his coat pocket and shut it there on the truck keys.
His face was wet and hot at the same time, and she could read it.
He wanted her.
He had the sentence in his head again, whole, with two people watching.
*In December Aisha Cole looked at a number on a screen and said no.
She was right. I'll put my name beside hers on it, in front of anybody.*
He was not going to say it, and he knew that too, standing there.

Her eyes came up to his and went over his face once, all of it, like a chart.
They stopped at his mouth and came back up.
She was waiting to see what he would say, and he watched her wait.

"Film's at four," Dan said.

That was the whole line.
It was the one thing a coach could say in a parking lot
with the lawyer still in his ear.
Film was at four, and he would be at work, and she would not.

Something went across her face,
the start of a smile that did not get anywhere,
and then her eyes were steady again.

"I know," she said.

Then neither of them had the next thing.
His hand was flat on her door and both of hers were on the wheel,
and it was like being fourteen.
Her mouth opened on something and shut on it, and her eyes went bright.
She blinked it off, once,
and looked past his shoulder at the flags in the water.

"You're wet," she said.

"It's raining."

"I can see that it's raining." She looked at him then, straight.
"Dan."
She had let his name out and put the rest away.
He could not tell if she was sparing him or shutting him out.
He decided it was both, and that he had earned the second one.

She did not ask him to say anything else. He saw her not ask.
It was on her mouth, the thing a person says, *say something, then.*
She did not say it, and it cost her, and he watched what it cost.
Nobody had ever spent anything on her,
and she was not going to start by requesting it in a parking lot at noon.
He knew that about her.
Her hand came off the wheel and went to the switch.

The window went up. It went up slow,
and her face went behind the rain on the glass,
and then it was glass with rain on it and the shape of her.
She backed out of the warm end and took Millrow down at noon, in daylight,
past the car at the fence and the trainer's door.
He stood in the water and watched the RAV4 down to the first curve.
The staff car had seen a coach cross a lot in the rain to a suspended doctor
and say nothing worth hearing.
He walked back across it with both hands in his coat.
The trainer was not in the door when he looked.


Film was at four.
The coordinator was on the clicker with the spring cutups,
two calls out on his own name on Dan's say-so, and still here.
Ty Beaumont was in the second row with a legal pad.
Ty was the receivers coach on Dan's staff, thirty-four, with a truck payment.
Dan came in late and stayed on his feet at the back.

"Coach," Ty said.

Ty said the rest low, under the clicker, to Dan and not the room.
"She had it right in December. You said so on the radio.
If anybody ever asks the second row, the second row says the same."

"Nobody's asking the second row," Dan said. "Keep it that way."

Ty nodded once and went back to the pad.
Ty had spent something to say it, and Dan had told him to put it away.
That was the coach Dan was today.
Forty minutes of a slot receiver on a hitch route.
He saw four inches of her face on the screen over every rep,
the hood up and the vent moving her curls.
He watched every rep and saw none of them.

Film ended at twenty to five, and he took the hill down in the last of the light.
At six Dan was on the stool nearest the register at the Checkerboard,
with a glass of water and the rain on the square's glass behind him.
It was the same rain since morning, steady, no louder.
Earlene Tatum had the pot and was walking it.
Tunk Ferrell, who sat the window table most mornings
and wanted every story in town first, had it at six at night too,
cup out for the pot.
The cook had his back to the room.
The cook set the Coach's Slice down in front of Dan
and went back to the griddle.

"It's a morning item," the cook said. "I made one for the ice. This is the other one."

"I'm eating it," Dan said, and did.

The stool next to his was hers in the mornings, after he was up the hill.
Nobody had moved it, and he did not look at it long.

"Zoned it for the college," Tunk said, to the window.
"Man I had it off said the whole thing took eleven minutes."

"Eleven minutes," Earlene said. She filled his cup.
"That's about what it was worth."

"They spent longer than that on the Christmas lights," Tunk said.
"And the doctor this morning. Same week."

"Nobody fired anybody," Earlene said.
"A school doesn't fire a doctor in January with a class coming in.
It hasn't got the stomach.
It puts a word like review on a piece of paper
and hands the paper to the county.
The county's been wanting the job since October, and now it's been asked.
That's all a review is. It's an invitation."
She looked at the window, at the rain on it.
"And the zoning went the same week.
I'm not saying who wanted the zoning.
I can count to two, and so can anybody with a calendar. I'll leave it there."

Tunk had the name and did not say it.
The cook had it and did not turn around.

"Town room's been on that street since last night," the cook said,
to the griddle. "Forty of them on the whole thread since last night.
Man's going to put up a glass building
and every one of them wants to know where to leave the truck."

Tunk laughed at that, once, out loud, and Earlene did not.
It was funny, and Dan did not have the laugh in him.
He put money on the counter, and Earlene did not look at Dan going out.


At half past six it was dark, and Dan drove the truck up Millrow to the annex
instead of home, the hill running with rain in both gutters.
The clinic at Aisha's end was still lit.
The rain was lighter now, on the truck roof and on the annex roof
when he got out, a sound like a thumb run down a screen door.
A man he did not know was covering her clinic,
at the near table in the whirlpool room with the tank off.
The warm-end door was unlocked because the clinic was open,
and nobody stopped him.

"Help you?" the man said, from the desk.

"No," Dan said, and went on down the corridor.

Her office door stood open at the end of the corridor,
and the corridor smelled of the tank and the tape.
He stood in her door and did not go through it.
The office was still hers.
Nothing had gone down the hill but the day's box,
and the file drawer was shut with her files in it.
The hook on the door was empty because the parka was on her.

He had the sentence whole one more time.
*In December Aisha Cole looked at a number on a screen and said no.
She was right. I'll put my name beside hers on it, in front of anybody.*
He could have said it at eight to a lawyer.
Or at noon, through four inches of glass.
He had not, and he was not going to say it tomorrow either.
He knew that about himself now, and he had not known it this morning.
He was afraid he would keep the job and she would go down that hill for good.
He would know it every day, fourteen hours a day.

Her chair sat where she had pushed it in, square.
The clean square on the desk was the size of her tablet, and the lamp was on it.
He looked at it as long as he had looked at the RAV4 going down the hill,
and then longer.
He wanted her in that chair with the tablet up,
not looking up when he came to the door.
She never looked up first. He had to say her name to get her face.
His right side, where the bench had put her in the storm,
was cold from the shoulder down.
His hands were empty in his coat,
and the low thing from the morning was still in his chest with nowhere to go.
The door at the warm end stayed unlocked behind him.
Dan stood in her door with the building open around him, and he did not go in.
