Sonny Dillard had the point of a trowel down in the crack
across the office step at the Magnolia Court,
working the loose grit out of it by the light of the neon,
and the bag of quick-set sat on the gravel by his knee
with the top still stitched shut.
The freeze had split the step.
It was seven on a Monday, dark enough yet
for the pink script over the office to be lit,
and under the script the word VACANCY was out.
Aisha had seen it out on every home Saturday since September.
She had not seen it out on a Monday.
Ashford played at home in five days,
the first home game this town had ever had in December,
and at seven forty-five this morning
she was going to put Trey Gault on a bike in a quiet room
for twenty minutes with his heart rate capped
and find out what his brain thought of the idea.
If it held, tomorrow was the next rung
and the county could go on counting.
If it did not, the clock went back to the start,
and the Saturday Verna had already sold every room to
would get played without Trey in it.

One RV sat up on the grass by the ice machine,
a plate from two states over,
and the rest of the lot was the lot.
Verna was in the storm door with the fur collar up
and the office phone at her ear.

"Through Saturday," she was saying. "No. Through Saturday.
I've been full through Saturday
since the Sunday they said our name on the television."
She hung up and held the door with her hip.
"Coffee's what's left of it."

Aisha took the cup.

"Seven Saturdays is my year, my winter doctor.
This is an eighth, and it's in December,
and I haven't got a word yet for what that is.
You're in the back room Friday and Saturday both.
And the other thing.
Earlene counted the doors on that truck. Four.
The Table's done with it, and so is anybody who eats there.
That's so you know before you walk in there."

Aisha had read the post herself at six, in room nine,
before she had her boots on,
and had not said a word about it to anybody,
and was not going to start on Verna's step.
Verna looked at the cuffs, pinned flat at both wrists,
her own pins in a coat cut for a man,
and at the block letters over the patch,
and said nothing about either,
which from Verna was a paragraph.

Sonny sat back on his heels and looked at the crack.

"Bag stays shut, Doc," he said.
"It wants forty degrees and the sun on the step,
and it'll get that about one o'clock if it gets it at all.
Pour it now and it sets up pretty
and comes apart in your hand in March.
You don't rush concrete."

"I had a man once who'd have poured it at seven
and sworn at it in March," Verna said.

Sonny went back to the crack.

Concrete cured on its own clock and so did a brain,
and Sonny's step would tell him at one o'clock,
and Trey's brain would tell her at seven forty-five.

Her phone went in the parka pocket at seven.
Missy Gault's page, a photograph of the notebook.
Slept eight hours. Headache zero, fourth morning.
Read twenty minutes and stopped on his own.
Seven nights held.
She sent back the two words she sent every morning.
*Good record.*
Then, because it was Monday, she heard him ask for the room.
*Let me be in the room.* Saturday, at her desk.
She had said not you,
and the quiet in that room at seven forty-five
was going to be a quiet she had made, by keeping him out of it.

She left the cup on Verna's sill and took the tablet to the RAV4.

***

The number had sat at one thirty-one for two minutes
when she asked him the first time.

The bike was in the empty rehab room next to the whirlpool room,
the annex's own bike, and a chair, and nothing else.
It was the quietest room in the building.
Trey Gault sat the bike in a gray hoodie with the hood up,
his back to her, the chest strap under the hoodie
talking to the tablet in her hand.
His coach had asked for the hood two weeks ago at her board,
for a different room, and here it was, on a bike.

"One-forty," she had told the trainer at the door at seven forty-five,
once. "Seventy percent of what August measured.
It goes over, you say so, and I'll have seen it before you do."
Missy was in the hall on a chair from the office
with the notebook on her knee, level.
Denny was at work, and that was Missy's doing.

Twenty minutes.
She asked at four, and at eight, and at twelve,
and she did not ask the hood.
She came around to where he could see her ask,
because a question to the back of a head got the room's answer.
The number sat between one twenty-eight and one thirty-four
and the cadence held, and his answers came back short and the same,
and she wrote 4 and 8 and 12 on the tape on her left wrist.
His own coach had told her in a hospital lounge
that Trey would not tell her the truth about a headache.
Aisha Cole had not built a protocol that needed him to.

At fourteen the cadence dropped.
Not much. The number stayed where it was and the pedals did not,
and his right hand came off the bar and went up under the hood,
and she was asking before the hand got there,
and he had said it before she was done.

"That's it. Feet down. Stay on the seat."
She took the number off the screen with her thumb,
wrote 14 on the tape and 7:59 under it,
and typed it standing, before she said a word to anybody.
Symptom recurrence at step two, minute fourteen.
Heart rate one thirty-two, under the cap.
Step down. Clock restarts.
A thing said before it was written
was a thing somebody could say differently later.

"He's done," she told the trainer. "Walk him to his mother."

Missy stood up in the hall with the notebook.

"What do I write."

"Seven fifty-nine, headache, on the bike, stopped.
Then whatever he tells you tonight, and when."

"And tomorrow."

"Tomorrow he's a student again.
It has to hold overnight with nothing in it.
Then the bike again Wednesday, at the earliest."

Missy wrote Wednesday and nothing after it.
The hood went down the corridor ahead of the trainer,
and Missy looked at it go,
and her face did not do one thing Aisha could have charted,
and she went after her son.

The room was quiet with the bike stopped.
Dan had asked for this room on Saturday,
and she had said not you, and she had been right,
and standing in it now
she would have liked Dan to have seen the fourteen.
Not the headache. The catch.
She would have liked to watch him take it, too.
He took a right call better than any man she had ever argued with,
a nod and no grudge in it,
and she had argued with most of them.
Dan. She had said it once in a bar and not since,
and it came easier in an empty room than his last name did.

At her desk she put it in the chart the long way,
and the board stood behind her, level on his folded card,
the circle at the top in her red.
She did the count from the chair without turning around.
It did not reach.
The sheet at five fifteen would say so in two words,
and Dan was going to hear it from her in more than two,
so at three she was going across the lot.

***

"—up on the video board after the game, is what they're asking for,"
the coordinator was saying, with his back to the open door
and Aisha in the hall behind him.
"The rendering. And the suite list for the nineteenth by Wednesday.
Boyd's people want it while the stands are still full."

"If," Merritt said.

"That's what I said. They said it like it wasn't a word."

The coordinator turned, saw her, and went by her with a nod.
She knew the drawing.
It was taped to the wall beside the depth chart with one corner curled,
and she had looked in it twice for her rooms and not found them,
and now she knew it went up on the video board Saturday night
if they won.
That was the football side of the lot's business, and she went in.

Dan Merritt was at the table with the week's script
and the projector dark,
and the film room had a window she had never stood at.
Through it, from his side, her building sat across the lot
under the light poles, the roof the color of the sky,
and the RAV4 was small by its door.

"Doc."

"I came over instead of waiting for five fifteen."
She stayed by the window.
"Symptom recurrence at step two. Minute fourteen.
Headache, on the bike, at one thirty-two, under the cap.
He's stepped down.
The clock's over. It starts again tomorrow, from the bottom."

He did not say anything, and she went on.

"Likely out. Likely, because out is Friday's word
and I don't say it till the day I'd have to.
But here's the count.
Tomorrow he's a student again, and it holds overnight.
Wednesday is two, at the earliest. Thursday three. Friday four.
Saturday is five. Saturday is the game."

She had Sunday ready.
He had asked for Sunday in her office two days ago,
and the answer to it was in her mouth with all its clauses,
and she waited for him to ask.
He did not.
He looked at his own whiteboard instead,
the top corner above the week's script,
where DEC 19 stood in his block letters, circled once.
She had guessed those letters two weeks ago. Here they were.
He read the date and looked back at her,
and kept looking a moment longer than the question needed,
before he asked it.

"Walk me through Tuesday, medically."

She had been asked for Sunday, and the room, and the week, and a form.
She had not been asked for Tuesday.

"Tuesday," she said.

"Tuesday. What's his brain doing tomorrow that it can't do on a bike."

She brought the tablet up to reading distance and opened the imaging,
and he came around the table to read it,
and reading distance for two people was about a foot.

"That's the twenty-eighth," she said.
"Saturday night, at the hospital. Clean.
That's the third, six-forty in the morning,
everything August took and two sequences August didn't. Also clean."

"Clean's good."

"Clean was never going to clear him.
A scan sees bleeding, swelling, bone.
It doesn't see a concussion.
I didn't order the second one to find something.
I ordered it so nobody in January could say we didn't look."

"So where is it."

"The answer? Not in the picture.
The picture's the building. The hit's in the wiring,
and the only way I can see the wiring
is to ask it to do a job and watch.
The bike's a small job. He answered at fourteen."

"Fourteen," he said, low,
and his breath came against her cheek when he said it,
warm, at the distance a screen gets read from.
That was new, the air off him,
and she swiped to the next image.

"The headache is the answer.
A healed brain doesn't get one at one thirty-two
in a quiet room on a bike. His did.
That's not a setback, Coach.
It's the first true thing that brain has told anybody in two weeks."

"Why a student day. Why not the bike tomorrow at ten minutes."

"Because the bike lit it,
and anything I ask him tomorrow, the headache's still in the room.
Twenty-four hours with nothing is the floor, not the test.
Then it holds overnight. Then I ask again."

"And if Wednesday's fourteen minutes."

"Then Wednesday's a student day too,
and I don't say a number past that, because I don't have one."

The window put a glare across the screen.
He put two fingers on the edge of the tablet
and tipped it out of the light,
and his knuckle lay along hers for the second that took,
and the hand did not come away when the glare did.
She noticed that it stayed.
Nobody said a word about it,
because the glare had needed fixing.
From a foot off she could see the side of his head,
the dark hair cut close over the ear,
and she had not looked at his hair before as a thing a man had.
He asked the next one, and the next,
what the overnight was for,
what Missy's page told her that a bike could not,
and by the fourth question she knew what the fighting had been for.
Every Friday since June this man had pushed on a sheet and she had held it,
and every one of those had been the two of them
learning how the other one thought,
and it had been practice for this,
one screen and a foot of air and him asking.

A wheel came down the Fieldhouse hall with a flat spot in it,
a thump on every turn,
and a man with a cart went past the open door and looked in.

"Coach."

He went on by, and the thump went with him.
Merritt straightened up out of the screen's light.
The door stood open because he kept every door in this building open,
and gave the man in the hall
a coach and a doctor and a tablet to see.
She stayed where she was, with the picture up.
She had been reading it.

"I've had the meeting room held at seven for three weeks," he said.
"For his twenty minutes. Projector's still unplugged."

"I know."

"I'll give it back."

"Wednesday, at the earliest, you might want it."

"Wednesday." He looked at the circle. "At the earliest."

She put the tablet under her arm.
On the way out she passed the drawing with the curled corner
and did not look for her rooms in it. She had looked twice.

***

Five fifteen, the practice turf, the lights on over the field
and the team in drills behind him down the field,
and the one printed sheet of paper she had carried across the lot
from the annex printer was in Merritt's hand
before the wind could get under it,
and the trainer stood close enough to hear a coach, cutting tape.
One page. Monday's date. Two names.
Under thirty-four, the word it had said all week.
Under Trey Gault's, where Held had stood seven nights in her typing,
two words.
Stepped down.

"Thirty-four," she said. "Sleeve stays on this week."

"He'll ask."

"He can ask."

"And Trey."

"It's on the sheet."

"I read it."

Behind him a whistle went and the drill turned over,
and the cold came across the turf with it.
He had had every word of it at three o'clock,
and he read the two on the turf anyway, in front of the trainer,
because the sheet was the part the trainer could hear.
Then he folded it once and put it inside his jacket,
where none of the seven before it had gone,
and went back to the near hash,
and she went to thirty-four with her own hands.

***

She was back in her office at the warm end of the annex,
the door shut, the heater going, the chart open in front of her,
four lines into it, the long way,
when the heater clanked and quit and she looked up at the board.
It stood level on his folded card, the circle at the top in her red,
and under the circle, on the desk,
the tablet lay open to tonight's line in the chart,
because two words like that went in a record.
His date, her red, her two words under it.

She let it come the whole way, as she had once before at this desk,
and it came in a different shape.
She had wanted his hands, and the heat off him, and his voice.
She had that list.
Tonight it was plainer than a list.
She wanted Dan a foot off with the next question in his mouth,
and no cart in the hall, and an hour.

It was a good thing to want.
She had not had one of those in a while.
She put it where she could reach it without looking
and finished the line she was on.
Tuesday. Student. Holds overnight.
Missy's page came at seven, and she would be up for it.
