The coat rack outside the trustees' boardroom was going over
under the weight of half the county's coats,
and Aisha Cole caught it by the top hook
and stood it back up against the wall.
Nobody was taking a coat back out on this Monday, the first of February.
They had come to watch the independent review sit,
and on Wednesday it would say in writing
whether she had made the right call
when she held Trey Gault from a playoff game.
If it said she was wrong, that went on her record,
and her record went with her to every job she would ever apply for,
the one out west included.

Her palm was still cold from the rack's top hook.
Verna had driven her in at eight in her own car.
The RAV4 stayed in Verna's gravel with the boxes in it,
because a loaded car in the trustees' lot
would have been read by the whole county
before the review had even sat down.
"I'll hear it all by noon," Verna had said at the curb,
and she had not come in.

The wool coat was back at the Court.
The parka lay folded on the top box in the back of the RAV4.
It was the team's coat, and she was not wearing the team's coat into this.
She had on the one good jacket she had come to town with.
It had hung in the closet in nine all season.
It was too thin for the hallway, and she was glad of the cold on her skin.

Through the double doors the boardroom was full.
It seated forty, and all forty seats were taken,
and people stood along the back wall with their coats over their arms.
Half of them had signed something or liked something
on the parents' board against her.
The other half had come to make sure she got a fair hearing,
and she could not have sorted one half from the other by their coats.

Missy Gault sat on the aisle in the third row
with her purse in her lap and her notebook on top of it.
Missy wanted her son safe, and she wanted him on the field,
and Aisha had watched her try to hold both of those in the same two hands
since the night Trey went down in November.
Denny sat beside her with his coat buttoned to the collar, indoors.
Marisol Pruett had the end seat of the second row
and a folder on her knees.
At a table on the right sat the athletic director,
and beside him the university's lawyer,
a man in a gray suit with no folder.
The athletic director wanted a quiet February.
The lawyer looked like a man who wanted nothing said by anybody, ever.

Earlene Tatum was on the bench under the hallway window
in her church coat, with her pocketbook square on her knees.
She had got to the boardroom before anybody else
to save the front row with her scarf,
and then she had come back out into the cold hall to wait on the doctor.
Earlene wanted the county to answer one question this morning,
and she had been asking it all week at the Checkerboard
to anybody holding a cup.

"All you ever did in this county was the job they hired you for,"
Earlene said.
"This morning we see if the county lets a person get away with that."

"I'd like to know that myself," Aisha said.

Earlene looked past her at the front doors.
"Your coach isn't here."

"He isn't on the review's list."

"Lists," Earlene said, as if she had found one on the bottom of her shoe.

Aisha did not look at the doors.
*Your coach*, in Earlene's dry voice, put her heart up hard,
and it went into her chest and stayed there.
She kept her eyes on the coat rack.
She wanted Dan to come through those front doors
so badly that the palm of each hand had gone damp.
She would not have said so to a living soul.
She wanted him to stay away just as badly,
because at her door on Thursday she had told him what it would cost him.
The terms held until Wednesday.
Nothing had been settled on Thursday night
except that she had sent him home, and he had gone.
Her breath came short, and she let it.

At nine the reviewer sat down at the front table of the boardroom
and read the rules off one typed sheet.
Aisha had a small table of her own at the left,
with the chart printed and tabbed at her elbow,
and no lawyer, because she had not thought she needed one
to tell the truth about Trey's brain.

"This review sits in an open room," the reviewer said.
"It will hear anyone it chooses to, at my discretion, and nobody else.
Everything said goes in the transcript.
The NCAA's own rules call a team physician's authority over return to play
unchallengeable and autonomous. That rule is Independent Medical Care.
Did Dr. Cole use it well? That's the question."

The reviewer looked down the rows.
"I'm told the coffee in the front hall is better than it looks."

The reviewer wanted the true answer and not a speech,
and Aisha could tell it from how short the sheet was.

"Dr. Cole. You may sit to answer, if you like."

She stood.
Her knees wanted to lock, and she unlocked them.
She put one palm flat on the stack of paper so it would keep still.

"The checklist lets a step go in a day," the reviewer said.
"It lets a player ride out a mild headache on the bike.
At the student's first headache you stopped him and started him over.
Most programs would have had him back for that game.
Why did yours run slower?"

It was the fair question.
It was the one a man at the Checkerboard counter
had put to the man beside him over eggs last Tuesday,
and she was glad to hear it from somebody who meant to write her answer down.
Her breath came easier for being asked it straight.

"A day is the least a step takes," Aisha said.
"The checklist never says it's the most.
It lets a headache go if it's mild and brief.
At step two, sixteen days out, fourteen minutes into the bike,
the patient's hand went up to his head.
That was more than the checklist lets a physician ride through.
So the checklist stopped him, not me.
I stepped him down, and his clock started over.
Past two weeks it wants a physician to look again.
I did, in January, twenty minutes at the cap. He held all twenty.
His family took him to Birmingham for a second opinion.
They booked it. I sent the whole chart there on my own.
What Birmingham told them is theirs to say."

She said *the patient* every time.
She had said Trey to his face every day of the season,
to his mother in the hall, and at the top of every page in his chart,
because he was a person and not a number.
Here there was a transcript,
and a transcript was a thing a scout could ask to read some spring,
so Trey's name stayed out of it.
Nobody in the forty noticed.
It cost her the one warm word she had in that boardroom,
and it ached going, low in her chest.
She let it go and kept on.

"Walk the review through the chart."

"August's baseline is first," Aisha said.
"Then the scans from the twenty-eighth of November and the third of December.
Every order has its reason typed under it and my name at the bottom.
The tape from the bike says fourteen, with 7:59 under it.
His mother's seven o'clock pages are all there, dated."

Every rung had a date on it.
She had built it that way in November for a morning exactly like this one,
and she had hoped the whole time she would never have to stand up
in front of a county and need it.
Her voice held. Her heart did not, quite.

"And the annex's records?"

"Forty-one boxes," Aisha said.
"I packed and numbered them in December.
They've been locked at Mt. Nebo Missionary Baptist since,
and my count matches the church's.
Mrs. Fountain-Mabry keeps the church's count on a legal pad."

"The review would like to see that pad."

"It doesn't leave the church," Aisha said.
"She says the review is welcome to drive over and ask her for it."

A few people along the back wall laughed,
and one of them said *Delores* as if that settled it.
The reviewer's mouth moved at one corner, and the pen kept going.
Aisha's chest eased a notch.

A trustee at the end of the second row lifted a hand,
and the reviewer allowed one question from the seat.

"I'd like one question in the transcript," the trustee said.
"If she'd cleared that boy and he'd gone down on that field,
what would all of us be saying about her this morning?"

Nobody answered it.
The trustee had wanted it on the record more than answered, and now it was.
Two rows back, a man in a feed-store cap looked at the floor between his boots.

Aisha sat down.
Dan had argued her Friday sheet all fall and lost on the merits every time.
He was the only coach who had ever argued it to her face,
and the only one who took her no as if it were worth having.
She thought of him at her board with the sheet in his hand,
and her chest went warm and sore at once,
and she kept her hand flat on the chart until it passed.

At half past ten Marisol Pruett stood up at the end of the second row
and asked to be heard,
and the reviewer looked at her for a long moment
and said the review would hear her.

Marisol moderated the Ashford parents' board,
and the eleven families had put their letter together on it.
Her own son, Wes, was a walk-on,
and the letter's questions about the roster were questions about boys like him.
She had left every word of that thread up anyway,
because it belonged to the families and not to her.
She wanted the forty to see where the letter's words had come from.
Aisha could see it in how she carried the folder up the aisle in both hands.

She laid two sheets side by side on the reviewer's table, under the lamp.

"This is the families' letter," Marisol said.
"It was organized on the board I moderate, so I have their own copy.
This is a page of the consultant's printed materials.
The trustees put them in the February agenda packet, for the public."
She put one finger on each sheet.
"Eleven families signed this, and I know every one of them.
They're good people, and they signed what they believed.
But the middle of it says *proactive risk posture*,
and I've never heard one of them say that at a ballgame.
It's in these materials word for word.
The materials came to town with the consultant the pledge brought.
I can't prove who put his words in our letter.
I can show you they're his."

The sound that went through the rows was not loud.
A woman in the fourth row who had signed covered her mouth
and kept it covered.
The athletic director looked at the table in front of him.
The lawyer wrote nothing down.
From her table Aisha could see the two sheets under the lamp,
and the same three words sat in the middle of both
like a phone number copied off one page onto another.
Her stomach turned at it.
She pressed her palm to the table and held it there.

That letter had come to Dan with her name under his on the routing line.
He had read it the same week she had,
and neither of them had said one word about it to the other,
because it was about her calls,
and her calls went to him across a sheet with the trainer listening
or not at all.
It was her rule.
He had kept it better than she had kept anything that winter.
Her throat closed, and her heart went over once, hard.

Marisol had not sat all the way down
when the door at the back of the boardroom opened.
Aisha turned in her chair, and so did the forty.

Dan Merritt stood in the doorway in a suit.
Every day she had known him he had worn the sideline jacket,
to practice and to the radio and to the Checkerboard counter.
He hated a suit, and anybody could see it from how he stood in this one,
with his shoulders set against the cloth
as if it were a hit he had seen coming all week.
The haircut from Thursday was still sharp along the back of his neck.
His jaw was set hard, as it set when he was counting something.
He did not look at her.
The cold went down the backs of her knees.

The lawyer was half out of his chair with one hand up.
He stayed like that, half standing,
as if he could hold the door shut from across the room.

"Coach Merritt," the reviewer said. "You aren't on this review's list."

"No," Dan said. "I'm asking to be heard."

The reviewer looked at him, and at the lawyer, and down at the typed sheet.
"This review said it would hear anyone it chose to. Go ahead, Coach."

The lawyer sat back down slowly.
He did not look at the athletic director,
and the athletic director did not look back.

Dan came up the aisle and stopped at the head of it,
an arm's length from the end of her table.
She could smell the cold of the lot on his suit.
He still did not look down at her.
He had not come to ask her anything,
and he was not going to look to her for leave before he said it.

"My quarterback got hurt on the twenty-eighth of November," Dan said,
"and Dr. Cole held him from that night on.
She signed her no-go for the nineteenth on Thursday the seventeenth of December.
That's eleven days before the families' letter got to the athletic director.
It's a month before anybody in this county had a picture to talk about."

His voice was low, and it carried to the back wall anyway.
It came from just past her shoulder,
and her whole back went tight, from her spine out to her arms.

She knew what he was doing by his second sentence.
Her hand came up off the chart.
She corrected people for a living.
She had corrected men twice his size in hospital hallways and on sidelines.
Every muscle she had was built to stand up now and say *Coach, that's enough*,
and then take the record back and say it better herself.
She had never let a man pay for her.
Not a dinner, not the rent, not one word on her behalf she had not asked for.
Her mouth opened on *Coach*.
She shut it, and she put the hand back down on the chart.

"I pushed her," Dan said.
"All season I argued her Friday sheet at the medical check,
and I lost on the merits every time.
When my quarterback got hurt, I went around her to the athletic director
to get the boy into a team meeting with film on.
The athletic director told me no in writing.
Everywhere I pushed, it broke on her and not on the boy. She held."

Heat went up her chest into her face.
Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in the palm on the chart.
In the third row Missy Gault's notebook had shut on her thumb.
Missy's other hand had gone to the clasp of her purse, and it stayed there.
Denny kept his eyes on the back of Dan's head and did not move.

"My staff did what I told them," Dan said.
"Whatever came at Dr. Cole from my side of the lot came from me first.
Put it on me, and leave them out of it."

He looked down at the carpet for a second, then back up at the reviewer.

"She cost me my starting quarterback for a playoff game.
If he walked in hurt tomorrow, she'd cost me him again,
and I'd lose that argument too."

The laugh went through the forty all at once, surprised out of them.
In the front row even Earlene's shoulders went.
Aisha did not laugh.
Her eyes had gone hot,
and she kept them on the edge of the table so they would not blink.

"I told this review last Monday that I'd have played him," Dan said.
"That much got on the radio. Here's the rest of it."

The boardroom went quiet enough to hear the radiator tick under the windows.
The lawyer's hand came up off his knee again and stopped in the air.
Aisha could not get a breath all the way down.

"I said I'd have played him. That's exactly why the rule says I don't decide."

He turned his head then and looked at her for the first time.
He said the rest to the reviewer with his eyes still on her.

"Dr. Aisha Cole decides.
She held my quarterback in December, and she was right."

On the fourteenth of January he had said *her protocol* on the radio
and left her name off it, and the county had heard the gap.
Last Monday he had said *I'd have played him* and stopped,
and by Tuesday the county had that too.
Now her whole name was in the transcript in his voice,
with the rest of his sentence in front of it.

Her breath went out of her and did not come back in.
Her chest had gone so tight it hurt,
and under the hurt it was warm,
and she could not tell one from the other.
The skin of her face burned.
Her hands were on the chart and shaking,
and she left them where anybody could see them shake.
She had not asked him.
Nobody had ever stood up in a room for her without being asked,
and she had spent her whole working life making sure
that nobody in any building she worked in would ever have to.
Under the table her knees would not keep still.

Everything in her wanted to stand up and tell him he had said too much.
She did not stand.
She sat still in the good jacket with her hands on the chart,
and she let him pay it in front of the whole county,
and it was the hardest thing she had done all winter.
She let it count.

She knew what it cost him.
She had said it to him at her door on Thursday,
the job he had wanted his whole life and every man on his staff with it.
She was so proud of him it hurt, in her chest and down the inside of each wrist,
and she had never said that to a man in her life
and could not say it now to this one.

His face was pale under the new haircut.
His jaw was still set,
and his eyes on her were steady and did not ask her for anything.
Then he looked back at the reviewer.

"Thank you, Coach Merritt," the reviewer said.
"The review will take fifteen minutes."

Dan left first, before anybody in the forty had stood up.
He went out the back door,
and through the glass in it Aisha watched him turn left in the corridor,
toward the back of the building, where nobody in the forty had any reason to go.


When the recess was called the forty stood all at once
and went out the front doors toward the front hall and the coffee,
talking before they were through them.
The lawyer went with them, and the athletic director after him.
Nobody went out the back.
Aisha waited at her table until the lawyer was at the urn
with his back to the boardroom.
Then she went out the back door and turned left, the way Dan had gone.

If anybody saw a coach and his team physician alone together before Wednesday,
the university would fire them both the same day,
and there were forty people and a lawyer at a coffee urn
on the other side of two thin walls.

The back stairs were behind a fire door at the end of the corridor,
concrete, with a window of wired glass on the landing.
It was ten degrees colder there than in the hall.
Dan was on the landing four steps down,
with his back against the cinder block and his hands at his sides.
He looked up when the fire door opened.

She let it close behind her on its arm, slowly, so it would not bang.
Neither of them said anything.
There was nothing either of them could have said
that the other did not already know,
and a word would only have been something for the hallway to overhear.
She went down the four steps to the landing and stopped a foot from him.
His eyes came up to hers and stayed there.
Through two walls she could hear the coffee crowd,
a laugh, a spoon on a cup, a man calling across the hall to his wife.
His hand came up an inch from his side,
and hers came up at the same time,
and their knuckles knocked together,
and for a second neither of them knew what to do about that.
Then his fingers turned over and closed around her hand.

The warm of his palm went up the inside of her wrist and into her chest.
Her breath went out of her all at once and would not come back right.
His thumb moved once across the backs of her knuckles,
and her stomach turned over slowly and kept turning.
Her heart went hard and high under the good jacket,
and she was sure he could hear it.
Heat came up into her face, and she let it,
because there was nobody on those stairs to hide it from.
Her skin had gone awake everywhere at once.
Her knees had gone loose under her, and she locked them.

She wanted him, right there on a concrete landing,
with the lawyer and the county and a transcript
on the other side of two walls.
She was angry at him for what he had just thrown away,
and she was holding on to his hand with everything she had,
and she could not make those two things into one thing.
Her head was clear.
His was too, and she could see it in his eyes.

They held on for as long as it took her to count one, slowly.
She counted it.

Then his fingers opened, and hers did.
He went on down the stairs without a word,
and she heard him all the way to the bottom
and the outside door let him out and shut on its closer.
She stood on the landing with her hand still a little out from her side
until the heat went out of her face.
Then she went back up the four steps and through the fire door,
and the lawyer was still at the urn with his back to the boardroom.

The review sat again at a quarter past eleven and ran until three.
It heard two more people, and neither was anybody Aisha had to answer.
At three the reviewer adjourned until Wednesday morning,
when the review would sit once more and then give its findings.
Marisol drove her back to the Magnolia Court in her own car.
Marisol wanted to say something about the morning,
and twice Aisha watched her decide not to.

By five the light was going off Verna's gravel.
Aisha was on the office step Sonny Dillard had poured the week before,
still in the good jacket,
with an unopened can of sparkling water on the concrete beside her.
She had come down the lot to tell Verna how the morning went,
and she had sat down on the step instead of knocking, and stayed.
Her breath went white in front of her.
At the other end of the lot the RAV4 sat loaded in front of nine
with the parka folded on the top box,
where she could see it from the step without turning her head.
The acceptance was still typed under the chair's email on her phone, unsent.
Nothing today had moved Saturday the sixth.

Verna came out behind her in her cardigan,
with her reading glasses down her nose and her phone in her hand.

"They posted the trustees' February agenda over again this afternoon,"
Verna said. "I read it at four.
The pledge for the new building says *re-sequenced to a later sitting*.
Your coach's extension isn't on it anymore. They struck it."

Re-sequenced meant moved back, to a meeting with no date on it.
Struck meant gone.
The pledge was the money for the performance center on the annex's ground,
the building the survey flags had marked out on top of her clinic.
The extension was Dan's.
It was the job he had wanted his whole life,
and it was the work of every man on his staff,
and by the afternoon of the day he said her name it was off the page.

She had seen him pay it, and now she felt what it weighed.
It landed low in her chest and stayed there, heavy,
and her stomach went hollow under it.
Her hand closed on the cold can and did not open it.

"I know what it was," Aisha said. "I watched him spend it this morning."

Verna wanted her winter doctor to stay.
She stood on her own step in the cold without a coat,
longer than any piece of news took, and did not say it.

She had let him.
That was what she kept coming back to on the step.
He had paid in front of the whole county, and she had sat still and let him,
and she did not know yet what a person did after that.
She was afraid of what came next.
She was afraid of being the thing it had cost him,
and she was more afraid of how much she wanted him anyway,
tonight, with her own car loaded and waiting at the other end of the lot.
The ache of it sat under her breastbone, and her heart went slow and heavy.

Verna lowered herself onto the step beside her, one hand on the rail,
and did not ask her anything.
Aisha's right hand lay open in her lap, palm up,
the hand he had held on the back stairs,
and she left it open.
