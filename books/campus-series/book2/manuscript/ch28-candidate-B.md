# Chapter 28 — Signing Day

POV: Dan, third person limited, past tense.
(DRAFT — blind candidate B, card D3, per plots/brief-ch28.md.)

---

> **On Grapevine, the Ashford parents' board —**
>
> **NotAFootballMom:** Visiting my daughter, who had class, so I went to
> signing day at the high school gym. I don't understand football.
> Nobody signed anything. I clapped whenever the radio man said to.


The university's photographer walked backward down the high school's
front hallway with one hand up,
and Dan Merritt followed the hand until his shoulders touched the trophy case.
It was Wednesday the third of February, signing day,
and at nine WDSS 1340 would put three hours of it on the air
from the gym behind him for a class that had signed in December.
Across town at the same hour, the independent review would sit a last time
and hand down its finding on Aisha Cole.
If it found she had been wrong to hold his quarterback,
the finding stayed on a doctor's record wherever she went.
If it found she was right, she was clear.
Three signatures were still owed his class by the close of the day.
He was counting on one of them.
He had stopped counting on the other two.

The trophy glass was cold through his jacket,
and the cold went straight down his spine.

"There," the photographer said. "Hold that, Coach."

The photographer handed him a pen
and a clipboard with a blank sheet of letterhead on it.

"Look like you're signing somebody."

"They signed in December," Dan said.

"Then look like you remember it."

Dan looked like he remembered it,
and the flash went twice off the glass.
Odell Fountain, the barber on Fountain Row, had cut his hair last week
for these pictures,
and it still felt short at the back of his neck when he turned his head.
Aisha had laughed at that haircut in the dark of her room at the Magnolia Court,
one short sound behind her wrist,
the same night he told her he would pay whatever it cost.
He could hear it now as plain as the flash.
His breath went out slow, and he held still for the camera.

The athletic director came in the front doors at twenty-five past eight
with a paper cup of coffee and the cold still on his coat.
He was Dan's boss, and in January he had signed her leave.
On Monday he had sat at the university's table at the review
while Dan walked up the aisle past its lawyer
and said the rest of the sentence the lawyer had told him not to say.
This morning he had come for the first picture,
because the athletic director stood in the first picture on signing day.
By nine he would be back at that same table across town to hear the finding.

The photographer took the two of them shaking hands in front of the trophy case
and went on into the gym to find the light,
and then the hallway was empty except for the smell of floor wax.
Cold came in under the front doors and lay along the tile.

The athletic director turned the cup in his hand.
His face was gray, and he had nicked himself shaving.

"The trustees are going to ask me in February
how a pledge that size moved on my watch," he said.
"I'd like to have an answer before they ask."

He wanted to keep his job through February, and that was all of it.
A donor's money was an athletic director's to keep.
On Monday afternoon the trustees had moved the new building's pledge
back to a later sitting with no date beside it,
and a board remembered a thing like that a good deal longer
than it remembered a season.

"It didn't move on your watch," Dan said.
"It moved on my testimony.
You didn't lose the money. I did."

The athletic director looked at him a long time.
His thumb had pushed a dent into the plastic lid of the cup.
Dan's heart was knocking under his jacket, slow and heavy.
Nobody else was in the hallway to hear what he had said.
It was not a thing a man could pay back,
and there was nothing the athletic director could do with it
but carry it into the boardroom in February and give it as his answer.

"I signed what the lawyer put in front of me in January,"
the athletic director said.
"I'd likely sign it again.
I didn't like it. I'd like you to know that much."

He dropped the cup in the can by the trophy case and went out to his car.

Dan stood where the photographer had put him.
He would not be at the review this morning.
He had said his piece to it on Monday,
and on signing day the head coach stood in this gym.
Nothing between him and Aisha had been settled on those back stairs.
Until the class closed today the terms still held,
and a coach and his team physician caught together were both gone that day,
and he would not put her through that for a phone call.

He had been awake at four anyway, working out her morning.
The front table at nine.
The good jacket, too thin for February.
Her palm flat on the chart to keep it still.
He would not have told that to anybody.
He had hardly told it to himself until the hallway went quiet,
and now it went through his stomach warm and would not leave.


The gym had half the county in it by nine.
The station did the college's signing-day show from the high school
because the gym had bleachers and heat,
and in February the college's stadium had neither one.
Tick Moran hosted the Coach's Table on 1340 on Thursdays,
and this morning the station had given him the gym.
He wanted a fair show more than a loud one.
He had a card table set up on the center-court circle,
with his mixer on it and one headphone pushed back off his ear.
He also had a fax machine,
plugged into the floor outlet under the scoreboard for the look of the thing.

The Liars' Table had come up from the Checkerboard
and taken the first row of the bleachers behind him.
Tunk Ferrell sat on the end.
Tunk had the window table at the Checkerboard most mornings
and wanted every story in town before anybody else had it.
Earlene Tatum sat beside him in a knit hat.
She had an opinion out loud about most things
and a front-row seat for all of them,
and all winter she had wanted the doctor treated fair.
Peanut Kyzer, who poured at the Mule, sat with his cap on and his arms folded.
Peanut heard everything in this town and put none of it anywhere,
and what he wanted was for the town to leave people be.
Sonny Dillard's coat was saving the last seat.

At nine the station's tone went, and the bulb on Tick's table came on.

"Signing day, friends, from the high school gym," Tick said into the mic.
"We've got a gym full of people who know good and well
the class signed in December, and you came anyway.
We've got Coach Merritt. We've got the fax.
Letters of intent come in on a computer now, and I know it.
The fax doesn't know it, and I'm not going to be the one to tell it."

The bleachers laughed.

"It ever ring, Tick?" Tunk called down.

"Once," Tick said. "A man selling a boat."

"That was your cousin," Earlene said, "and it was a bad boat."

The laugh went up the bleachers to the top row,
and Tick let it run on the air, because it was good radio.

The photographer had the class printed on a poster board,
a face and a hometown for every kid who had signed in December.
Dan held it up at center court beside the fax machine for the flash.
Most of the faces on it were sitting in a high school class this morning
two or three states away,
and not one of them was thinking about this gym or the man holding them up.
The bleachers thought that was funny too.
After a second Dan found he did.

At the first break he went along the front row.

"Your doctor's across town this morning, Coach," Earlene said.

*Your doctor* went into him low and quick,
and his stomach turned over before he could stop it.
Peanut looked at the floor. Tunk looked right at him.
Dan kept his eyes on Earlene's and felt the heat come up his neck.

"She's the team's doctor," Dan said.

"Mm," Earlene said.

He took his phone out of his jacket and looked at it.
There was nothing on it.
He had known there would be nothing on it.
The last thing in their thread was his own, sent the night before the boxes,
eight boxes and a tape gun and a marker of his own
so she could keep hers for herself.
He had been pleased with himself about the marker.
He put the phone away,
and his palm stayed flat on the pocket a second longer than it needed to.
His heart was going as if he had climbed the bleachers.


At a quarter past ten Tick's phone lit on the card table.
Tick read it, and the radio went out of his face.
He looked across the circle at Dan before he leaned in to the mic.

"Friends, I'll read you what I've got,
because you'll have it off the board in five minutes anyway," Tick said.
"The school the transfer quarterback told yes to first
has posted it this morning.
He's signed with them.
That's the young man Coach Merritt brought in out of the portal in January,
and he's gone back the other way."

The gym went quiet from the top row down.
Somebody in the middle of the bleachers said *well*,
and nobody said anything after it.

"That's the one he flipped," Tunk said, not low enough.

"We know which one, Tunk," Earlene said.

"Coach?" Tick said, and held the mic out across the table.

Dan stepped in to it.
"He's a good player," he said. "He'll do well there. I wish him that."

Then he stood there.
He did not sit down, and he did not go out to the hallway.
He stood at center court with his hands behind his back
and let the county look at him.
His palm had gone wet in the other hand.
His spine had gone straight as a post, and he left it that way.
The cold of the floor came up through his shoes,
and his breath would not go all the way down.
Behind him the photographer took a picture,
because that was the photographer's job.

In January the quarterback's father had called Dan's desk phone
from two states off and asked whether the doctor sat kids out here as a rule,
or whether that had only been the one time.
Dan had told him she held kids out here and would do it again.
He had put the phone down knowing it might cost him a quarterback.
The county had watched him go get that kid while his own starter sat on a bike,
and this morning it had heard the kid go back the other way
in Tick's voice in a high school gym.
That was what one true sentence to a father had cost.
He would say it again.
He knew that, standing there, and it did nothing for his knees.

Sonny Dillard came up the bleachers at half past ten in half his gear.
He ran the counter at the feed store by day
and the pumper when the pager went,
and he wanted his deliveries done and the pager quiet.
He picked his coat up off the end seat and sat down.

"Survey crew's out on the annex ground," Sonny said.
"Three of them and the long tape."

"In this cold?" Tunk said.

"Frozen dirt measures the same as any other kind."

The annex was her clinic, across the lot from Dan's office.
On Monday the trustees had put the pledge off with no date on it,
and the crew was out on her ground anyway,
working toward the eleventh, the day her annex was supposed to be boxed.
Whoever paid those men had not told them to stop.
Dan's stomach went tight and stayed that way.

Tick went to the feed store's spot,
and then to a caller who wanted to know if the concession stand took cards.
At twenty past eleven he looked at the sheet in front of him
and did not smile.

"Two more lines on Coach's sheet were due today," Tick said.
"Two families who told him yes in the fall.
They've been quiet since January, and they're quiet this morning.
Nothing on the computer, nothing on the fax.
The office tells me not to look for it."

Dan stood there again.
Neither family had picked up a phone since January,
and he had known what that meant for a month.
Hearing Tick say it to the county was another thing.
It sat in his stomach, cold, like something he had swallowed whole.
The extension was already off the trustees' page.
Now his class was a quarterback and two families short
in front of everybody who had ever bought a ticket,
and every man on his staff would carry that class into his next interview.
He kept his hands behind his back so nobody could see what they were doing.
His knees had locked, and the ache of holding them went up into his hips.


At twenty-five to twelve Tick's phone lit again,
and this time he put it to his ear under the headphone.

"It's Marisol Pruett, friends," Tick said on the air. "She's at the review."

Marisol moderated the Ashford parents' board,
and her son, Wes, was a walk-on on Dan's roster.
She had stepped out into the hallway over there to call Tick,
because she wanted the county to hear this morning straight from the people
who were there before the board got hold of it and bent it.
Tick listened with his eyes shut.
Then he said it into the mic a sentence behind her.

"Marisol says Missy Gault asked to be heard before the findings,
and the reviewer let her."

Missy Gault was the boy's mother.
All winter she had sat in the hall outside Aisha's office with a notebook
in her lap,
wanting her son safe and wanting him on the field,
and she had never found a way to have both.

"She's reading off a paper out of her purse," Tick said. "Here's Missy."

His voice changed for it.
It went slower and plainer, the way a man read a thing he had not written.

"'We drove Trey to Birmingham on the twenty-third of December
to get a doctor there to say this one was wrong.
He read everything she sent, and he saw my son himself,
and he told us she was right, and he wrote it down.
I've had his report in my purse since that night.
I never showed it to anybody. I never showed my husband.'"

Tick stopped.
The gym had gone so quiet Dan could hear the heat ticking in the ducts.
The skin on his arms had gone tight under his sleeves.

"'In January she put him on a bike for twenty minutes
to see if his heart would hold, and it held.
She wrote down every day of him since he got hurt, with a date on every page,
so that when the scouts come in the spring
they can read for themselves that his brain is sound.
She built that for my son while this county was writing letters about her.
I wanted her gone. I was wrong.'"

Dan's hands had come apart behind his back.
He did not know where to put them.

Tick listened again.
"Marisol says Missy told the reviewer why she stood up today," he said.
"She stood up because of what Coach Merritt said on Monday."

Every head in the front row turned to Dan.
His throat shut.
He had walked up that aisle on Monday to finish one sentence,
and he had not thought past the sentence to who might be sitting
in the third row with a purse in her lap.
Missy had carried the proof for six weeks,
and his sentence was what got it out of the purse.
Heat went over his skin all at once, and his eyes stung.
He kept them on the fax machine,
because it was the one thing in the gym not looking at him.

"And Denny's standing up," Tick said.

Denny Gault was the boy's father.
All fall he had held court about his boy and the doctor on Delmar's porch,
and in January he had stood alone in the porch's corner
with a plate of food he never touched.
Tick listened a long time.

"Marisol says he didn't know about that report," Tick said.
"She says he looked at it in Missy's hand like it had hit him."

Then Tick read Denny, and he read him slow.

"'They brought me the families' letter to sign, and I told them no.
I wanted my boy on a field more than I ever wanted anything.
I didn't want him on it that bad.'"

Denny's name had not been on that letter.
On Delmar's porch in January, Dan had let the missing name lie
and had not asked.
Now the whole county had the why of it, in Tick's voice.
The loudest man on the porch had been the father who would not sign.
Dan's breath went out of him hard, as if somebody had hit him too.

"They're reading the finding," Tick said,
and he held one hand up to the gym without looking at it.
"Hold on, friends."

Nobody in the gym moved.
Dan's heart went hard and slow, and he could feel it at each wrist.
He had waited in a hundred tunnels on a hundred flags.
He had never waited on anything like this.

"The review affirms Dr. Cole's call," Tick said.
"The suspension's lifted.
And the reviewer called her documentation exemplary.
Marisol spelled it for me. Exemplary."

*Affirmed* meant the review said she had been right, in writing, for good.
The suspension was gone, and her record was clean.
Whatever hospital or school she walked into next,
that record would walk in beside her with nothing on it,
and nobody at any front desk would ever have to ask her about this county.
Dan guessed that was the line the offer out west had been waiting on.
Nobody had told him so.

*Dr. Cole* in Tick's voice went through the gym, and through him.
His breath went out of him all at once.
His knees went loose, and he locked them.
Heat came up his neck into his face, and this time he let it come.
Something in his chest that had been held since November let go.
He wanted her in this gym so badly his hands shook with it.
She was across town in the good jacket with her name back,
and he was so glad for her he could not keep still on the floor.


Peanut Kyzer took his cap off first.
He did it without a word,
the way he set a beer down in front of a man who had not ordered one,
and held it on his knee.
Tunk looked at him and took his off.
Sonny pulled his off by the bill.
Earlene took off the knit hat and held it in her lap with both hands.
At the card table Tick reached up under the headphones,
took off his own cap, and laid it on the mixer.
The whole Liars' Table sat bareheaded in the front row of a high school gym
for a doctor who was across town and would not see it,
and not one of them looked around to see who was watching.
Dan's breath caught at Earlene's hat in her lap, and it stayed caught.

The money for the new building had wanted her rooms and her job,
and it had wanted the county to hand them over.
Five of the county's own sat with their hats in their hands instead.

Then somebody near the top of the bleachers started to clap.
The rest of the gym came in under it,
and it went on long enough that Tick took his hand off the fader
and let the county hear it.
Dan stood at center court and did not trust his face.
His palm was pressed flat against his leg to keep it still.

When it was quiet, Tick leaned back in to the mic.

"Friends, I'm going off my sheet, and my sponsors can take it up with me.
That fax never rang this morning, and I'm glad of it.
Every name on that poster board went on paper before Christmas.
The one that counted this year went down on paper
on Thursday the seventeenth of December,
and it was a doctor writing no,
and every good thing that's come to that boy since came out of that no."

Tunk leaned down over the rail with his phone.
"Odell says forty-six days this morning, Tick. Since the boy sat.
He says somebody ought to say it on the radio."

Odell had wanted that number on the radio since the day he cut Dan's hair.

"Forty-six days, Odell," Tick said into the mic.
"He's on a bike, and he knows his own name. There. It's on the radio."

Earlene laughed out loud, and half the bleachers went with her.


The bulb on Tick's table went off at noon.
The gym came down out of the bleachers with its coats over its arms,
and a good part of it came across the floor to Dan.
A man he did not know shook his hand and said *Dr. Cole*,
as if Dan could carry it up the hill to her.
A woman in a church coat told him the whole gym had stood up.
It had not. He did not tell her so.

Earlene came along the front row with the knit hat still in her hand.

"You tell your doctor the Table took its hats off," Earlene said.
"She won't believe it off the radio."

"I'll let the radio tell her," Dan said.

"Mm." Earlene looked at him longer than he liked,
and then she put her hat back on.

He was glad for her all the way down.
It sat warm under his breastbone and would not go anywhere,
and he did not want it to.
His knees still did not feel like his own.
He had wanted her cleared more than he had wanted the class,
and the county had sat in the bleachers and watched him lose the class
on the same morning she got her name back,
and he would not have traded it back for any quarterback alive.
He would not have said that to anybody.
He would have paid it twice.

His notes for the show were on an index card in his jacket.
He took the card out to put it away and read the last line,
the one he had not read on the air.

*Pruett, W. — walk-on. Keeps his spot.*

He had decided it in December off the scout-team film, alone,
and he had told nobody.
Marisol had phoned the county this morning to tell it the truth about a doctor.
She did not know yet that her own boy was staying.
He put the card back in his jacket.

For the first time since the night in Verna Poteat's gravel
at the Magnolia Court,
he let himself think about the RAV4 in front of nine, loaded to the roof,
and about the boxes coming back out of it.
He did not let himself think about who would carry them.
His breath came easy for the first time all winter.

The photographer caught him one more time at the fax machine with Tick,
and in that one he was grinning like a fool.

"That's the only good one all morning," the photographer said.


By one Dan was back up the hill in his office at the Fieldhouse,
with every door in the building standing open, the way he kept them.
The class sheet lay on his desk with three lines empty at the bottom.
Across the lot the annex sat with its lights off.

Ty Beaumont put his head in the door at two.
Ty coached the receivers,
and his truck payment had ridden on the extension
the trustees struck off their page on Monday.
He wanted his job, and he wanted the doctor cleared,
and this morning he had got one of the two.
He was grinning anyway.

"Weight room's had the radio up since noon," Ty said.
"Every kid in there heard her name.
We lost a quarterback and two families this morning, Coach,
and I've never seen you look this good."

Dan laughed, and it surprised him how easy it came.

"Get out of my door," he said.

Ty went, still grinning.
Dan sat with his palm flat on the class sheet.
The class was done. Nobody else was going to sign.
The terms were done with it, today.
He had waited seven weeks,
and he had not got as far as what he would say to her.
He was not going to call her yet.


At ten past three the athletic director came up the corridor
with his coat still on.
He had sat at the review's table all morning and at his own desk since,
and Dan heard his shoes on the tile a long way off.
The athletic director stopped in Dan's open door.
Then he came in and shut it behind him.

Every door in the Fieldhouse stood open by Dan's rule,
and nobody had shut this one since the season ended.
Dan thought it was the job.
He had been waiting on it since the agenda went up again on Monday,
and he found he was ready for it.
He put each palm flat on the desk.

The athletic director did not sit.
He stood with his back against the door
and looked at the class sheet with its three empty lines,
longer than it took to read them.
His face was the one a recruiter wore in a parent's living room
when the kid had picked somewhere else.
It was not the face of a man firing anybody.
Dan's stomach went cold before he knew why.
The radiator ticked under the window.
The athletic director took a breath and let it go, and took another.

"The chair out west called me at half past two," the athletic director said.
"It wasn't a reference call this time.
He wanted a release date for her.
She wants to start early, and he needs us to let her go."

Dan did not say anything.
He could not get a breath in under the words.
The athletic director looked at him, and at the shut door, and back.
Out in the corridor somebody laughed at something,
and it came through the door small and far off.
Dan's palm had gone cold on the class sheet.
He knew what was coming, and he held still and let it come.

"She said yes, Dan."
The athletic director's voice had gone quiet on it.
"She sent it today."

It did not land all at once.
It came in low, a flat cold under his ribs, and then it spread.
His skin went cold all over, and then hot.
His heart went down and did not come back up.
The breath he had been holding went out of him and left nothing behind it.
His palm went wet on the paper, and the paper stuck to it.
Under the desk his knees had gone to water.
There was a sound in his ears like the gym going quiet from the top row down.
He sat still,
because anything he did with his hands would be the wrong thing.

"The chair said one more thing," the athletic director said.
"A donor here called him in January to speak well of her.
The man behind the new building's pledge.
The chair thought I'd like to know she had friends in town."

Dan knew the name without anybody saying it.
Rex Boyd had called out west in January
and put in a good word for the doctor his money wanted out of her rooms.
He was a man who cleaned up his messes before anybody saw them,
and she had been his mess.
His money had not been able to move her clinic.
It had moved her.
Dan's stomach turned over slowly and stayed turned.

The athletic director had his hand on the knob behind him.
"It's a better job. I said so last week.
I'm sorry it came to you through me."

He opened the door and went out, and he left it open.

Dan sat with the corridor's noise coming in through the open door,
a plate set down on a rack in the weight room
and the radio still going under it with somebody on it saying her name.
The terms had lifted today with the class.
A coach and a doctor seen together on the square after signing day
would be a storm of its own for the county,
and he had not let himself get as far as that.
He had not got as far as asking her.
For ten years he had read the board every night before he slept
so that nothing could ever come at him from the side,
and this had come in through his own door in his boss's coat.

He was afraid of one thing now, and it was not the job.
He was afraid she would drive out on Saturday morning
believing he had paid on Monday to be square with her,
with nothing owed on either side, so she could go and not look back.
His breath came short and shallow at the thought.
He had spent his name in front of the county so she could have hers back clean,
and she had it back now,
and it was going west with her on Saturday morning
in the car she had loaded last week in Verna's gravel.
Nobody had asked her to stay.
He had not asked her.

He put his hand to the back of his neck,
where Odell had cut it short for the pictures.
It was the haircut she had laughed at in the dark of nine,
the last time he had made her laugh.
The ache went down from under his hand into his chest and stayed there,
and he kept his hand where it was.
