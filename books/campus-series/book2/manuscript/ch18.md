Dan Merritt's truck keys went into the pocket of the sideline jacket
in the Fieldhouse lot at nine on Sunday night.
The F-150 stayed where it was, nose to the fence,
the only thing parked on that side of the hill.
He started down on foot with his hands in the jacket,
the same as the night before.
At the bottom of the hill the Magnolia Court was a row of dark doors
and one lit window.
Room nine had the lamp on and the curtain open,
and Aisha was somewhere behind it.

The truck stayed up the hill because of his own terms.
He had said them in her office with the door shut,
nothing anybody could see until the class closed
on the first Wednesday in February.
A coach's two-door pickup in Verna Poteat's lot at night
was a thing anybody could see.
The town was gone till Friday.
Friday the boys flew back, the staff came into the building,
and the county came home behind them.
Every night after that was a night the two of them could not have.
So it was on foot, in the cold, every night this week.

He knocked, because he was not going to walk in on a woman,
and Aisha opened the door in her socks
with a can of seltzer in one hand.
She stood in the doorway and looked at him before she moved.
Her curls were down.
Every time he had seen her across a sheet they were tied back hard.
They were down now, everywhere,
and she had not done one thing to them for him.
That was the first thing he saw.

"You walked it again," she said.

"I'd have walked it twice."

"You'll walk it five times.
I counted on the run this morning.
Five nights till they're home, and this is one of them, so come in."

He came in and she shut the door with her back.
Then she put her free hand into his jacket pocket without asking
and came out with the truck keys,
and set them on the windowsill beside the can.

"So you'll know where they are at five."

He took the jacket off and hung it on the closet rod himself this time,
next to her parka.
When he turned around she was smiling at him with her mouth shut,
the brown eyes straight on him and not blinking.
There was the bed and there was the one chair,
and neither of them took the chair.
Verna's office light was on across the gravel until after eleven,
and Verna never said a word about it.

The square at ten past five on Monday morning had one lit window on it,

"You don't watch the ball. You haven't looked at it once."

"The ball's where the feet already went."

"You look like a man reading. Your mouth goes."

"Is that the one you were talking to last week?"

"That's him. He's got a lot to say tonight. He's not used to company."

She laughed the whole laugh at that, too loud for an empty building,
and clapped her hand over her mouth.
Then she took it away again,
because there was nobody in the building to hear her but him.
He ran the rep a fifth time for no reason but to keep her in the chair.

Tuesday night she fell asleep first, in room nine, with the lamp on,
and he turned it off at midnight when her breathing had gone even.
She said "Thank you" without waking up, to the dark.

Tuesday she had sat in his chair against the film-room wall
and clapped her hand over her mouth when the ice machine let go.
"Is that the one you were talking to last week?"
Wednesday thirty-four had been on her stool with her tape on his hand,
and she had never said one word about it.
He still had that, and kept it.
The same afternoon Odell had given him his jaw back,
and that night she had put her hand on it inside the door of room nine.
"I liked the beard. Come here anyway."
And the last night she had set a bottle of wine on his counter
with a knock, and drunk from his one glass with her feet on his chair.
"Wine, because we're in a house, not a room."
That was the week, all of it, and he had had every night of it.

The county was home.
Monday the building would have the staff in it and the boys.
Any room he saw her in would have a third person in it,
and she would be Doc and he would be Coach,
because those were his terms.
He was afraid of a plain thing.
He was afraid that a month of Doc and Coach would take the week back,
one room at a time.
By February the two of them could be as far apart
as the town thought they were.
He was afraid of the table too, the loss on his file and the word on it.
A man without a program had nothing to offer a woman with two suitcases.
That was the one under all the others, and he let himself have it once,
standing in the kitchen with the beer going warm in his hand.
He was more afraid of her car than of the table.

The phone lit on the counter and buzzed once against the wood.
He picked it up.
Her name was on it.
Both numbers were on the staff list, hers on his phone and his on hers,
and the list was all they had ever been.
This was the first word either of them had sent the other,
and it came the night the town got home.

*9:12. Lamp's on. Curtain's shut. It's still yours.*

It read like one of her chart entries, timed and short and finished.
He read it twice.
Then he typed longer, because that was him.

*Keys are home, on the hook. Leave the lamp on. I'll know it's there.*

He stood there with the phone lit in his hand
and thought about her at the window of room nine.
The curtain was shut on a lamp she had turned on for him anyway,
and nobody was meant to see it but him.
The week was over, and it had been the best one he had ever had,
and the ache of that was as real as the week was.
He would take both.
He left the light over the stove on and went down the hall.
The bed was made, tight, the way she had made it before light.
He sat on the edge of it with the phone in his hand and the lamp on,
and let her be the last thing in the day.
