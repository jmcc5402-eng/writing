The envelope in Dan Merritt's box had come across campus
from the athletic director's office,
and he opened it standing in his own office at the Fieldhouse
on Monday the twenty-eighth,
with his jacket still on and nobody else in the building,
and took out one printed page.
Eleven families had written the athletic director.
They wanted to know how a doctor's decisions got made
and how a roster got built,
and the office had sent the coach a copy on paper,
as it sent him everything,
and the portal opened Saturday.
Until then the roster was gone
and the hall outside his door had every light on for no one.
The routing line at the top was the office's typing.
His name, on paper.
Under it, by email, hers. Dr. A. Cole.
So she had it too, across the lot, on the tablet,
and had had it since whatever hour that office sent email.

He read it standing.

*We write as the parents of eleven current Ashford State football
players, and we have signed together so that no one family carries
this alone. We are not asking that any person be named or that any
decision be undone. We are asking the department for clarity on
medical decision-making and roster priorities as the program enters
the January window, so that families can understand how a player's
availability is determined and how a roster spot is kept or lost.
We believe a proactive risk posture on the department's part would
go a long way toward that understanding. We are grateful for this
program and for the people who run it.*

Then eleven names, typed, in a column,
with a state after each one and not one of them his state,
parents he could not have picked out of a lot in the fall.
Denny Gault's name was not among them.
It was the letter Denny had held up on Ray's porch
with all of Denny's words taken out of it
and somebody else's put in,
politer, and worse for it,
and one phrase near the end he had read somewhere
and could not put a room to.
He knew what *roster priorities* meant.
It meant Marisol Pruett's boy,
a junior who had run other people's offenses at the first defense
all fall for nothing, and had a walk-on's spot
that the portal was going to want on Saturday.
He knew what *medical decision-making* meant.
It meant Trey, and it meant her.
Her name was on the routing line at the top, where the office put it,
and nowhere in the letter under it,
not in the paragraph and not in the column,
and it was on every line of it anyway.

Her rule covered her calls.
She had made it in her office with her palm flat on the door,
that no call of hers got talked about anywhere
but across a sheet at five fifteen with the trainer in earshot,
and a letter about her calls was not a call.
He took it for one, standing there,
and that reading was his, and she had not asked him for it.
He did not post. He did not walk across the lot.
He did not call the athletic director
to ask what a coach was supposed to do with a page like this,
because he knew what the office wanted him to do with it,
which was have it.
He put it in the top drawer of the desk, face down,
under the depth chart from the nineteenth,
and shut the drawer,
and went down the hall to see if the ice machine had anything.

Dan was in the kitchen at nine that night with the phone in his hand
and a beer he had not opened on the counter, alone,
the rest of the house dark,
and the phone lit with Aisha Cole's name on it.
Her number had been in his phone with the rest of the staff's
since June, and his in hers,
and neither of them had used it for one word before tonight.

*9:05. Wind's gone north. Furnace is behind it.*

She texted like she charted, the time, the thing, done,
and it was about a furnace,
and he could see her sending it,
on the end of the bed in room nine with the parka still on,
the phone close, because she read everything close.
He typed longer than that, because he did.

*Every door up at the Fieldhouse was open all day and the heat went
straight out them. I'd have boxed you up some if it traveled.*

Then he stood there with his thumb over the keyboard
and typed the other thing.
*Same page in my box this morning as the one in your email,
about you and the roster.*
He took the last five words off it, plain,
and sent what was left.
That was all he was going to say about it,
and he had known that before he typed the part he cut.

*9:20. Mine came at nine. Read it once.*

Nothing after that from either of them about what was on it.
He looked at her three lines
and thought that this was the rule from her side of the lot,
a woman with a letter about herself on a tablet
telling him about the wind,
and he had told her about a heater,
and neither of them had lied.
He opened the beer.
The phone did not light again,
and he had not expected it to, and stood there a while with it.

The film room at the Fieldhouse had the projector fan going
at eight on Tuesday night
and the scout-team film from the week before the nineteenth
up on the screen,
and Dan had the clicker in one hand
and the January roster flat on the marker tray under the other,
one printed page, and the building to himself.
Every door on the hall stood open, his rule,
and the heat ran down the hall to the open doors
with the building empty behind them,
and the rendering with the curled corner leaned against the wall
where it had been all month.

He watched feet.
Marisol Pruett's boy took the snap for the scout team
in the third period of a Wednesday practice in December,
running the visitors' package at the first defense
for the fourth day running,
and his feet knew where the ball was going before it went.
That was all a quarterback's feet could tell a man,
and it was enough.
On the third rep of the cutup a pressure came off the edge
that the first defense had been told to bring,
and the ball was out and on a hip before it got there,
and the defense had been made to work by a walk-on
who was never going to take a snap for this program on a Saturday.

"Hell," Dan said, to the room.

He ran it back and watched the feet again.
Then he picked the January roster up off the tray.
The scholarships ran down the page above a line,
and the walk-ons ran down it below the line in a block,
and the portal opened Saturday,
and every name that came in through it needed a number,
and a coach who wanted a number
drew a line through one below the block and initialed it,
and that was how a spot got made.
He read the block to the bottom.

"Wes Pruett," he said, out loud,
and in this building the name had not been said whole before.
In here he had been Marisol Pruett's boy.

He initialed the top of the page and dated it,
and drew a line through nothing,
and put it back on the tray for Saturday.
He told nobody, because to tell it he would have had to say why,
and why was a walk-on's feet on a Wednesday in December,
and you did not hand a room that for a reason.
Eleven families were going to read the January roster too,
the day it posted, with the block whole.

He went to the window.
Her building was dark across the lot, every window of it,
the RAV4 gone from the warm end,
the poles over the practice field off till the semester came back,
and there was nothing out there to stand in.
Not the poles, not a stadium, not a lamp with a curtain open.
He stood in the window a while, with the corner of the rendering
curled at his shoulder,
and what he saw was Saturday night from the gravel,
her in the window of room nine with the lamp behind her,
a can in her hand, in her socks,
not stepping back from the glass when she saw who it was.
He took the phone out.

*Poles are off till the semester. Standing in my window anyway,
for nobody.*

It looked wrong as soon as it went,
a man asking for a light he had been told the rule about.
The answer came before he had put the phone down.

*8:50. Nobody's a bad reason. Go home.*

It was not cold.
It was the voice she used on thirty-four's hamstring,
the one that had never been wrong about a hamstring yet,
and he killed the projector.
On his way out past the cage
the ball on the counter still had a name on it under the light,
and he went home.

The staff room at ten on Wednesday had the offensive staff
back in it for the portal, on the two couches and the counter,
the coordinator on the clicker with the red-zone install
on the clipboard on his knee,
Ty Beaumont on the end of the near couch,
and Dan in the door with his coffee and a marker
he had picked up off the tray without deciding to.
The room had been going a while before he got there.
He could hear that from the hall.

"Earlene count your doors for you, Ty?"

"She counted them on the board," said a man on the far couch.
"B. Hollis typed it. Four."

"Still got four on it, or did you take two off Sunday?"

if anybody wants to count that."

"Park under a streetlight next time. Save the woman the trouble."

and went to the board,

Odell Fountain had Dan in the first chair at Fountain's,
on Fountain Row, at four on Wednesday,
the cape up to his chin and his hands under it,
his phone in the jacket on the hook by the door,
and nobody on the bench.
The clippers were going.
The shop smelled of clipper oil and bay rum,
and the beard, eleven days of it, dark,
was coming off Dan's face onto the cape a stroke at a time.

"You grew that on purpose," Odell said,
"or the razor take the week off with the rest of the county?"

"Take it off."

"I'm taking it off. I asked was it on purpose."

"It was a week nobody had a camera."

"Hm. Looked like a man hiding from a bank."

Odell worked. He was sixty-eight and particular
and he did not talk while he was on the jaw,
and when he was off it he did.

"Wind's north," he said. "Nothing's biting till it turns."

Dan said nothing to that. There was nothing in it to answer.

Odell tipped the chair back a notch and came at the neck.

"Eleven families, they're telling me."

"They're telling you right."

"Eleven." The clippers went off, and Odell wiped them on the towel
and looked at Dan in the mirror and not at him.
"That board's got twelve thousand people on it, Coach.
I don't read it, I don't have to, I've got a bench.
Twelve thousand on it, and eleven of them wrote a letter.
A man who counts one of those numbers and not the other one
isn't counting. He's worrying.
Count both. Then tell me which one's bigger."

Dan looked at the man in the mirror with half a face.

"Yes sir."

"All right then."

The clippers went back on.
What Dan thought about, with his chin up and Odell at his throat,
was her in the one chair in room nine on Saturday night
with her feet tucked up under her in her socks,
looking at this beard while he talked,
the look she gave a thing she was deciding about,
and she had not said what she decided.
The next time she saw him it would be a jaw,
the one she had put her hand past to get to the back of his neck,
and he wanted her to see it, and could not have said why,
except that she had seen the beard first,
the face he had grown with no camera to shave for,
and he wanted her to have both.

Odell finished, and did the hot towel,
and slapped the bay rum on without asking,
and took the cape off him with one hand,
and Dan paid and put his jacket on
and took the phone out of it on the sidewalk.

*Shaved. Odell said the beard made me look like a man hiding
from a bank.*

*4:40. I liked the beard. Noted.*

He stood on Fountain Row with that in his hand, the wind north
and the bay rum going cold on his neck,
and she had liked the beard,
and had made a joke about her own charting to say so,
the first joke she had sent him,
and he walked back to the truck slower than the cold called for.

The light over the stove was the only one on in the house
at midnight on New Year's Eve,
and Dan stood under it at the kitchen counter, alone,
with the phone in his hand and the board open on it,
because the board was the last thing before lights out
and had been for ten years.
Nobody on it was in the state.

> **PieBeforeKickoff:** Midnight here and I'm up. Boys fly back
> tomorrow and the portal opens Saturday and nobody in this house is
> sleeping either.

He closed it, and the phone lit in his hand before he set it down.

*Midnight. Nothing rang.*

It was the holiday line she meant,
the one that reached one doctor in a motel room,
and it had not rung on her night,
and she had told him that first, at midnight, before anybody.

He stood there and thought about Aisha,
because the kitchen was empty and he could.
He thought about the word *medical* on a page in a drawer
at the Fieldhouse, with her name on the routing line
and nowhere in the letter, and the letter about her from its first word.
He thought about what the page was.
Eleven families had written down what the board had only typed,
and a thing written down went in a file,
and a file went to a table,
and the table in February already had a word for him,
and now it had a page about her to lay beside the word.
He could live through a table.
He had been sat down a year once and had lived,
and he had said so to himself with a ball in his hand
and it had been true.
This was the other thing.
She had built a rule so that no call of hers
would ever need him to be honest about it,
and the rule was holding, and what it was holding out was him.
He could not tell her what the page did to him.
She could not tell him what it did to her.
They were going to text about a furnace and a heater
and a beard till the first Wednesday in February,
and the page would sit in the drawer the whole time,
and he was afraid that the furnace was all there would be room for,
that a rule built to keep a call clean
would keep the two of them that clean too,
all the way to February and a table at the end of it.
That was the thing.
He said it to the kitchen so it would be said once.

"I can't tell her."

Then he looked at the phone again,
at what she had sent,
and it was not a furnace.
It was a woman in a motel room
with a page of her own on a tablet and a rule she had built herself,
telling him at midnight that the line had been quiet,
the one thing on her night that was hers to tell,
and she had told him first.
He typed back more than she had, because that was him.

*Nothing rang here either. I went home Tuesday when you said and I've
been going home since. Happy new year from a kitchen you haven't seen
yet.*

*12:06. I plan to see it. Leave the light on when I do.*

He read it twice.
Then he turned the stove light off
and stood in the dark kitchen with the phone lit in his hand,
the one thing on in the house.
The letter was in a drawer at the Fieldhouse
and the portal opened Saturday,
and neither of those was in this kitchen.
What was in it was a counter and a stove and a man with a phone,
and she planned to see it,
and he looked around it once as a thing she was going to see.
