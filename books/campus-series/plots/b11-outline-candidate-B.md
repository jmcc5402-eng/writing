## Snowflake spine

### One sentence

When booster money redraws Ashford with a lawn where Hartwell House
stands, the woman who runs the town's twelve-thousand-parent board
must save the House before the trustees' November vote — and the one
signature that can doom it belongs to the widower she's falling for,
whose whole crew's winter is what that money buys.

### One paragraph

Fall in Ashford: move-in chaos, charcoal Saturdays, and a master-plan
rendering that shows "future green space" where the town's living
room stands. Marisol pins the save-the-House petition; Walt gets the
order to certify the structural report that would condemn it — the
report Rex Boyd's expansion needs, funded by the same gift that keeps
Walt's crew off the winter layoff list. **Disaster 1:** the campaign
discovers its true target is not a plan but a pen — one man's
signature, and Marisol has met the man. **Disaster 2 (midpoint):**
Boyd's sock accounts coup the board and strip Marisol's admin seat
the same night Walt's name surfaces on the preliminary assessment —
she loses her platform, he loses his name, and at midnight in the
motor pool they strike a secret alliance the town would read as
treason on both sides. **Disaster 3 (their own progress backfires):**
the unsigned draft leaks cropped to look signed and final — the
secrecy the alliance chose makes the truth untellable, the false
outrage becomes her campaign's best fundraising day, and his crew
rings the bell for jobs he never bought them. Resolution, found not
wished: her receipts break the astroturf and clear his name at cost
to her own surge; his equal-standing restorability report — priced,
drawn, and staffed by his own crew as the winter's work — gives the
trustees an honest third option, and the House wins its stay with
the payroll intact. Boyd is not beaten; he smiles and fast-tracks
the demolition survey. The town has a fight worth having.

### The four quarters (built middle-first; Q2–Q3 at highest
### resolution per card A5)

**Q1 — The pen has a name (ch 1–8).** Move-in week: Marisol
moderates a twelve-thousand-parent meltdown from Delmar's porch and
follows unexplained survey chalk to Hartwell House's curb, where a
big irritated man is pulling the stakes out of the boxwoods. The
kickoff rendering drops — the House replaced by lawn, "pending
structural review and trustee approval in November" — and her
petition is born beside Delmar's cash register the same week Walt is
told the review is his to certify, and that the gift funding it is
also funding the deferred-maintenance hole that otherwise becomes
twelve winter layoffs he'd have to name himself. A migrated seating
card puts them at the same catfish plate; sparks, wrongly aimed. By
quarter's end the power dynamic is said out loud on Delmar's porch:
her petition is twelve thousand people aiming at one man's pen, and
his pen is aimed at the town's living room. Nobody's wrong. Nobody
yields.

**Q2 — Two fights become one secret (ch 9–16).** Working proximity:
she gets inside the assessment as Friends of Hartwell chair, he
keeps tending the House off the clock, and each catches the other
caring. The plants go in: Sonny Dillard's fire-lieutenant verdict —
good bones, bad wiring; the dated glass tell-tales across the east
crack in a dead woman's handwriting; the scope memo that pre-decides
the finding by grading a 1911 house against current code; Marisol's
Sunday ritual of exporting the mod logs; thirty-one sock accounts
that all type "ya'll." The squeeze tightens on both — the neutrality
bloc rules-lawyers her pin while Boyd's draft arrives with RECOMMEND
CONDEMNATION pre-written above Walt's pre-typed name — until coup
night: at 9:41 p.m. a procedural vote strips her seat, and by
midnight a screenshot of the draft's cover has welded his name to
the verdict in the town's mind. She drives to the only lit door on
campus. Ratchet the shop dog defects to her boot; Walt opens the
drawer and shows her DRAFT — UNSIGNED and the rigged scope; and the
book's engine turns over: two people, fully honest with each other —
and still on opposite sides of a payroll. The alliance is struck,
the first kiss lands, and the secrecy that protects them both is
cocked like a gun over the second half.

**Q3 — On the record (ch 17–24).** The gym town hall, box fans and
WDSS live: the new team physician refuses to lend medicine to real
estate and torpedoes Boyd's safety framing in a blazing public
argument with the head coach — so Boyd hijacks Walt's silence
instead, thanking "our facilities director's partnership." Marisol
takes the mic and asks the question on the record: *will you sign
it?* Eleven seconds of dead air on live radio. He can't answer —
the honest answer burns his inside play and her campaign both — and
now she is publicly the woman who asked, and he is publicly the man
who didn't. She goes analog: swap-shop radio, clipboards, the
Fifth-Sunday circuit, and the found answer said out loud for the
first time — the House needs exactly the trades Boyd's decks
promised; what if the restoration IS the crew's winter? He goes
technical: nights measuring the House for an equal-standing
restorability report, her handwriting joining the ledger where his
late wife's stops. Homecoming peaks the campaign; a fire-marshal
inspection lands the truth Sonny always said — the wiring really is
dangerous — and Marisol publishes it herself, rewiring first on the
pledge list. Then the trap they built springs: the draft leaks
cropped to read signed and final. FACILITIES CONDEMNS HARTWELL
HOUSE by lunch, the bell rung at the Blind Mule for jobs he never
bought, Boyd's Friday deadline on the table — and the lie is her
best fundraising day. All is lost in the exact shape of their
secret.

**Q4 — Found, not wished (ch 25–30).** Her essence act: she burns
her own surge to publish the truth — watermarked pages, metadata
naming Boyd's paid sock vendor, the "ya'll" fingerprint, Tunk
Ferrell cleared by his own church-usher timestamps — and the
co-mods un-coup her by emergency vote when the receipts land. His
essence act: the Blind Mule chalkboard, the winter math written in
front of the crew, and Boyd's deadline allowed to expire unsigned.
At the trustees' public session in rivalry week he signs the
restorability finding at the podium, says in one sentence what the
House is to him, and Marisol delivers the funded plan — pledges,
the restored board's match, the crew's labor math. The Millrow
phase is tabled; restoration is authorized; the crew is contracted
through winter. Boyd smiles, proceeds with phases one and two, and
moves the demolition survey up. Scaffolding rises, the clipboard
retires to the House mantel, the board live-blogs the new couple
wildly wrong, and Walt glues a fresh glass tell-tale over the old
crack and dates it in his own hand.

## The two journeys (Hauge)

**Outer goal (shared finish line, pictured):** the trustees' November
vote ends with the House standing and no condemnation on the record —
Marisol's win — without the crew's winter paying for it — Walt's.
The image achieved: scaffolding on the House, his crew on it.

**Marisol — inner journey.** Wound: the divorce and the emptying
house. Identity: the fixer-admin — if she organizes hard enough,
nothing she loves gets torn down and nobody leaves; the moderator
seat is her worth made visible. Essence: a leader who values true
over winning and people over the win. The climax is unwinnable from
inside the identity: the fixer would ride the false outrage; only
essence publishes the correction that deflates her own surge.

**Walt — inner journey.** Wound: his wife's death; the House is her
memorial and he has never once said so out loud. Identity: the
caretaker-in-silence — maintenance as grief management, love
expressed only in boiler rounds and dated tell-tales. Essence: a man
who claims what he loves in public, by name, with his signature. The
climax requires exactly that: an honest finding signed at a podium
and one sentence about why.

Both journeys turn together: coup night strips both armors at once
(midpoint); the town-hall question forces identity's last stand (his
silence, her weaponized mic); the leak makes identity's strategy
(secrecy, momentum) the trap; the climax is two essence acts in one
room.

**Ticking clock, seen early:** the rendering's caption in ch 3 —
"pending structural review and trustee approval at the November
board meeting" — restated on Walt's work order in ch 4 and by WDSS
throughout. **Superior position:** from ch 6 the reader has seen the
sock vendor's credit line (and its telltale typo) in the gala
program; from ch 16 the reader and both leads know the draft is
unsigned and scope-rigged while the whole town doesn't — the gap
that powers ch 17–25. **Foreshadow/echo pairs:** the tell-tale slide
in her handwriting → a new slide dated in his (ch 12 → 30); "good
bones, bad wiring" for the House → for the man (ch 10 → 29); the
seating card from table nine → pinned over his workbench (ch 6 →
30); the petition that hunts his signature → the last signature
shown is his, on the restoration finding (ch 3 → 28).

## Chapter-by-chapter (30 chapters, ~2k words each, ≈60k)

POV alternates strictly: Marisol odd, Walt even.

### Ch 1 — Move-In Day (Marisol)
Marisol wants one flawless move-in weekend — water stations placed,
towing intel pinned, a four-hundred-comment mattress-topper war
talked off the ledge from Delmar's porch — while her son's dot
crawls across campus and his texts say *mom. stop.* What blocks her
is a survey crew nobody ordered, chalking arrows down Millrow toward
the one porch in town that belongs to everybody. She follows the
chalk to Hartwell House and catches a big, irritated man yanking
survey stakes out of the boxwoods — and unloads on the only person
in Ashford already on her side, though neither of them knows it yet.
MEM: She flames the stake-puller who turns out to be pulling them
  out, not in.
SPINE: The survey chalk is the expansion's first physical touch on
  the House; the leads collide over it.

### Ch 2 — The Motor Pool (Walt)
Walt wants a quiet season: mowers running, dorms watertight, WDSS on
the shop radio, Ratchet asleep on the parking spot stenciled with
her name. What blocks him is a calendar invite he never accepted —
Boyd's office scheduling a "condition survey of Millrow structures"
— and what changes is his stomach when he sees which structure. His
evening round at the House (boiler, gutters, a fellows' photo in the
hall he doesn't look at directly) tells the reader what no one in
town knows: he tends this building off the clock, and not for the
university.
MEM: A shop dog with her own stenciled parking space.
SPINE: The assessment enters Walt's world; his secret tending of the
  House is established.

### Ch 3 — Future Green Space (Marisol)
Kickoff week: Boyd's master-plan rendering posts to the board — the
south end-zone tower, two decks, a fan plaza, and a tidy lawn where
Hartwell House stands, captioned "pending structural review and
trustee approval at the November board meeting." Marisol wants the
board mobilized tonight; what blocks her is her own group's
keep-it-nice culture and a first wave of brand-new accounts cheering
the plan in suspicious unison. She pins the save-the-House petition
anyway, and by Friday the clipboard sits beside Delmar's register —
the town's second guest book. Ray slides brisket across the counter
and signs it without being asked.
MEM: The rendering's lawn — the House erased by landscaping.
SPINE: Opportunity (~10%): petition born, clock on the page, socks'
  first ripple.

### Ch 4 — Deferred Maintenance With a Porch (Walt)
Boyd comes to the motor pool himself, charming, reasonable, and
armed with the budget: the expansion gift plugs the
deferred-maintenance hole that otherwise becomes a winter

Boyd's exit line names the House "deferred maintenance with a
porch."
MEM: Boyd's line — and the twelve-name winter list under it.
SPINE: The signature requirement and the payroll stakes lock in; the
  clock restated on the work order.

### Ch 5 — The Clipboard and the Zinnias (Marisol)
Curb Market Saturday: petition table between the boiled peanuts and
Ivy Tran's flower truck. Marisol wants signatures; what blocks her

noon — and a rumor arriving phone to phone that the House is "a
firetrap anyway," posted by accounts nobody's ever met. Ivy starts
bundling a zinnia with every signature; Deke Wallace, in town for
the press-box flower order, buys three bundles and signs with grass
stains on his hands. What changes: Marisol counts the socks and
realizes signatures won't outvote a structural finding — she needs
the report itself to be honest.
MEM: A zinnia with every signature.
SPINE: The campaign meets its real obstacle (the report, not
  opinion); side romance opens inside the petition scene; socks
  escalate.

### Ch 6 — Table Nine (Walt)
The booster kickoff dinner. Walt wants to shake three hands and

### Ch 8 — Named on the Page (Walt)
