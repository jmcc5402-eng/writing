## Contrarian comp (variance card A4)

**The comp that says this book shouldn't work: the Save-the-Gazebo
romance** — the shelf's hundred bright small-town books where a
developer threatens a landmark, the couple flirts *beside* the fight,
the second act is a montage of meetings, and the climax is a hearing
won by a bake sale and the developer's change of heart. Its four
failure modes, and this outline's answer to each:

1. *The civic fight runs parallel to the romance.* Here it runs
   THROUGH it: her petition targets his signature by name. There is
   no petition scene that is not a couple scene.
2. *Procedure reads dry.* No docket ever carries a chapter. The
   structural report is tracked as a PHYSICAL OBJECT — a folder
   riding shotgun in a truck, red ink on a bar top, a pristine PDF
   that proves a lie — and votes happen in sweat, not gavels.
3. *The landmark is wallpaper.* The House is a grave with a payroll
   attached: his late wife's memorial, and the answer to whether
   twelve families eat in January.
4. *The money repents.* Boyd never softens and never blinks. The stay
   is won by documents, church suppers, and priced labor — and Boyd
   walks out of the climax already aiming at Millrow. The rescue is
   work; the villain survives it.

The card shifts emphasis only; no conflict with canon, the brief, or
the output format was found.

## Snowflake spine

### One sentence

Marisol [TK surname] can save Ashford's Hartwell House with twelve
thousand signatures — unless Walt [TK surname] gives booster Rex
Boyd the only one that counts: his own, on the structural report
that would condemn his late wife's memorial and keep his whole
crew in winter paychecks.

### One paragraph

Move-in Saturday doubles the town, and Rex Boyd picks that weekend
to unveil stadium renderings that reduce Hartwell House to
"structure 41, pending assessment" — pending, the Liars' Table
explains, one signature: the facilities director's. Marisol, admin
of the 12,000-member parents' board, launches the petition; Walt,
the widower who keeps the House at dawn and a crew of twelve by
day, won't sign anything — including her clipboard. **Disaster 1:**
she learns why honesty won't move him — Boyd's expansion funds the
facilities budget; her petition is aimed at a man whose refusal
costs his own people Christmas — so they strike a wary bargain: an
honest inspection, for the record, whichever way it cuts.
**Disaster 2:** the record is stolen out from under them — sock
accounts Marisol herself waved in on move-in day seize her board in
a midnight "neutrality" coup the same week Walt's name surfaces,
pre-typed, on a condemnation he never wrote, and when she asks him
at the town hall, on the record, whether he'll sign, he can't
answer. **Disaster 3:** their own weapon backfires — the draft
assessment leaks as if it were the condemnation itself, the town
takes the House for dead, his crew toasts the jobs he hasn't
bought, and she spends one bad night believing his name on it.
Resolution shape: cleared by red ink that isn't there, rebuilt by
spreadsheet forensics, church-supper phone trees, and payroll math
chalked on a bar mirror — Walt refuses on the record, files his own
restorable verdict, and the trustees table demolition and buy the
stabilization his crew will spend the winter earning. The House
gets a stay, the crew gets its payroll, the couple gets its porch —
and Boyd, unbeaten, turns to face Millrow.

### The four quarters

No locked quarter text exists for 1.1 (the ratified spine is the
plot-sweep §3 entry); the quarters below are PROPOSED and map the
sweep's fixed beats to chapters.

**Q1 — The clipboard and the pen (ch 1–8).** The town doubles, the
renderings drop, and the save-the-House petition finds its true
target: not Boyd, but the one honest signature Boyd cannot buy and
cannot skip. She besieges; he declines; and then she learns what
his silence is protecting — twelve families' winter — and asks him
for something harder than his name: the truth about the building,
in writing. *Locks the goal at ch 8 (~25%).*

**Q2 — The record and the coup (ch 9–16).** The inspection becomes
the battlefield: his crew falls for the enemy building, fresh
damage appears suspiciously on schedule, and a folder with his name
pre-typed rides shotgun in his truck, unsigned. Boyd stops arguing
and starts capturing — the board falls to ghosts in one night, and
at the town hall Marisol stakes the whole campaign on one on-record
question Walt cannot make himself answer. *Coup at ch 14 (~47%),
PONR at ch 16 (~53%).*

**Q3 — The long gray offline (ch 17–23).** Locked out of her own
board, she takes the petition where sock accounts can't follow —
the Curb Market, the fifth-Sunday suppers, the swap-shop hour —
while he builds the counter-case alone, in red ink, as penance for
his silence. Then the draft leaks as gospel: HOUSE CONDEMNED, his
name visible, the town in mourning, his crew buying rounds — all
is lost, procedurally and personally, at ch 23 (~77%).

**Q4 — Red ink and roll call (ch 24–30).** One midnight of
forensics clears him; one weekend of work arms them: photo logs,
geotech dates, a stabilization scope priced under one video
scoreboard, and the winter payroll chalked where the crew can read
it. At the trustees' meeting she yields her three minutes to him,
and the silent keeper speaks: refuses, files, and prices the
alternative. Stay granted; work assigned to his crew; Boyd pivots
to Millrow on the record; the porch, the clock, and the HEA.
*Climax ch 27–28 (~90%).*

## Chapter-by-chapter (30 chapters, ~2,000 words each)

### Ch 1 — Twelve Thousand Parents, One Porch (POV: Marisol)
Move-in Saturday, and Marisol runs it like an air-traffic
controller in sandals: the board's carpool threads, the lost-mattress
crisis, her walk-on son's dot crossing campus for the first time —
and, between fires, a queue of three hundred "new parent" join
requests she approves in batches without looking twice. The day
ends at the board's one flesh-and-blood ritual, the lemonade social
on the Hartwell House porch, where a scowling man in a facilities
shirt is unscrewing her balloon arch from his fresh-painted rail.
They argue about tape. Neither wins.
MEM: 12,000 parents online, one porch in the flesh — and the
balloon-arch standoff.
SPINE: introduces the House, both leads, and the board; the
approved ghosts are the coup's future votes (superior position).

### Ch 2 — Structure 41 (POV: Walt)
Walt's dawn runs on rounds: motor pool at five, Dozer asleep on the
seat of truck two, work orders triaged before the town wakes — and
an off-book stop at the House, where he replaces a sash cord and
winds nothing, says nothing, tells no one. At Boyd's booster
unveiling that afternoon, renderings drop: south end-zone tower,
two decks, a fan plaza — and the House reduced to a footnote,
"structure 41, pending assessment." The phase board reads SITE
CLEARANCE: DECEMBER; the package goes to the trustees rivalry week.
Boyd claps his shoulder: he'll want Walt's "good eyes" on the
report. Walt has seen this play before. It has never needed his
eyes. It needs his name.
MEM: a house he keeps at dawn, renamed "structure 41" by lunch.
SPINE: clock on the page; the signature chokepoint begins; the
memorial established without a word of grief spoken.
