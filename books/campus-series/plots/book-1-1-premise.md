## The hand-sell line (twelve words)

**She needs one signature. Signing it would raze his late wife's
memorial.**

## The one sentence

A divorced parents'-group admin with twelve thousand signatures sets
out to save the oldest house in a football town — and learns that the
one signature that can condemn it belongs to the widowed facilities
director who has spent four years quietly keeping the place standing
for his late wife.

## The one paragraph

Marisol runs the twelve-thousand-member parents' group of a Southeastern
football school, which makes her the most powerful woman in town who
holds no office at all — and when a booster's stadium expansion comes
for Hartwell House, the town's oldest building, she writes the petition
that will stop it. It takes her four days to learn what everyone at the
BBQ counter already knows. The expansion cannot touch the House until
one man signs a structural report calling it unsafe, and that man is
Walt, the university's facilities director, a widower whose late wife
was a Hartwell fellow, who has been patching that roof on his own time
since the funeral, and whose entire crew is paid out of the budget the
expansion funds. Her petition names his office. His signature ends her
fight. Refusing it ends eleven jobs, and every one of those people
knows his truck. They spend a season on the same porch anyway, arguing
about sill rot and joint custody and whether a building is worth what
it costs to keep, while the booster buys the parents' board out from
under her with accounts that don't exist and a report written to ask a
leading question. By homecoming, both of them are holding a document
that can destroy the other. What saves them is not the report and not
the vote — it is a maintenance log in a dead woman's handwriting, and a
woman who has moderated everyone else's opinions for six years finally
posting one of her own, under her own name.

## The four quarters

### Quarter one — the smoke gets there before the sun does

The season opens the way it opens every year. Ray's cooker has been
running since four in the morning, the tailgate ground is a village of
tents by seven, and Marisol works the lot like a precinct captain,
clipboard on her hip, because twelve thousand people trust her and she
has learned that trust arrives in person or not at all. She is here for
signatures. Her son is on the field somewhere in a walk-on's number and
has not answered a text since Tuesday. Her house has two bedrooms in it
that nobody sleeps in.

She gets her collision at the far end of the lot, where a man in a
county-surplus truck is pulling orange survey stakes out of a garden bed
and stacking them in his bed like he intends to keep them. He is not
apologetic, he is not charming, and he knows exactly how many coats of
paint are under her hand where she is leaning on the porch rail. He also
knows the answer to the question she has been asking all morning, which
is why the House cannot simply be protected, and he does not want to
tell her. The answer is that the expansion needs a building declared
unsafe, that only one office in the county can declare it, and that she
is standing on that office's porch, arguing with it.

By the end of the quarter she has walked the House with him twice, seen
what a hundred years of deferred maintenance does to a stair sill, and
written a petition clause that names his office by name. She does not
tell him the sentence is hers. That is the only thing in this book that
is not structural, and it costs exactly what it should.

### Quarter two — the leading question

They become the strangest working pair in town, which everybody notices,
because a college town of this size is a room with the door open. He
walks her through the building on Tuesday evenings because the alternative
is her walking through it alone with a contractor she found online. She
reads the state's assessment scope aloud on his porch and hears a
question that is not a question — the report is commissioned to inspect
the two elevations facing the expansion, and nothing else, which is how
you order a building's death without asking anyone to lie.

Meanwhile the parents' board turns strange. New members arrive who post
only on weekdays, only between nine and five, and only to say that the
group should stay neutral. Marisol has run that room for six years and
she can feel the floor moving. She does what she is excellent at, which
is administration — approval logs, membership queues, timestamps — and
what she finds is not proof of a villain but proof of a shift pattern,
which is worse, because a shift pattern means somebody is being paid.

The quarter ends with a vote to make her group neutral in the fight that
is eating her town, called and won by people who do not exist, on the
same night the preliminary assessment surfaces with Walt's name printed
in the signature block. Nobody has signed anything. It only looks that
way. It is going to keep looking that way for a while.

### Quarter three — on the record

The town meeting is the middle of the book and the middle of both their
lives. She stands up in a room with a microphone and asks him, in front
of his crew, in front of the paper, in front of two hundred people, the
only question that matters, which is whether he is going to sign. He
cannot answer it. Not won't — cannot, because the honest answer is a
number, and the number is eleven households, and saying it out loud in
that room ends those eleven jobs a week earlier than the report would.
A coach and a team physician have a furious, entirely professional
argument in the third row about something else entirely, and nobody
watching learns anything except that the town is out of patience.

After that it gets worse in the way that fall does. A lightning delay
empties a stadium into every covered porch within four blocks, and one
of those porches is the House's, and for forty minutes there is nowhere
for either of them to go and nothing left to be careful about. What she
learns there is what he has been doing with university labor for four
years and whose handwriting is in the maintenance log. What he learns is
that she wrote the sentence with his office in it.

Then the preliminary assessment leaks — scope page removed, cover page
intact — and the town reads a survey of two walls as a condemnation of a
building. Her board, which she no longer controls, carries it in ninety
minutes. His crew reads it as their last month of work. He has been
demolished with his own name, and the woman who taught this town how to
share things is the reason it moved that fast.

### Quarter four — what a signature is for

He is not rescued and neither is she. What happens instead is that two
competent people do their jobs in public, at cost.

He signs the report. He signs it with the full scope of work attached,
the two-elevation limit circled, and four years of his own maintenance
log appended as an exhibit — every board he replaced on his own time,
every invoice he never filed, in his late wife's handwriting where she
kept the list before him and in his after. It is a confession that will
follow him through every audit he ever sits for, and it converts the
document from a death warrant into a public record of a repairable
deficiency, which is a thing an institution cannot leave standing in a
file drawer.

She takes the other half. She stops moderating and starts speaking —
posts under her own name to a group she may lose, with the shift-pattern
evidence, and asks twelve thousand people to change what they are
demanding. Not save this house. Fix this house. A petition to stop a
demolition is a wish. A petition demanding a landlord repair a building
it holds in trust is an obligation with signatures attached, and she has
more signatures than the town has voters.

The House gets a restoration contract, which is a multi-year payroll,
which is eleven households unhooked from a booster's money — the answer
found, not wished, and found in a log that was sitting in a drawer the
whole book. It is a reprieve, not a victory; the expansion is still
coming and the council fight is a year out. But the crew's jobs no
longer live in his signature, which means the two of them are finally
allowed to be two people instead of two offices, and the last chapter is
the two of them on a stair the whole town now owns a piece of, with a
photograph being taken on it, and a survey crew scheduled for the annex
in the morning.

## A3 SENSORY INVENTORY — the material every later chapter draws from

Sourced from `BRAINSTORM.md` §8 World 1 and extended. No stock autumn.

### A fall Saturday, 7 a.m.

**Smell.** Oak and hickory, already grey over the lot, because Ray's
cooker went on at four and at this hour the smoke is still sweet — much
closer to burnt sugar than to meat; the meat smell does not arrive until
ten. Under it, generator diesel, cut grass gone wet overnight, and the
sun-warmed dust off asphalt that has not cooled since August.

**Sound.** Aluminum tent poles ringing on pavement like a boatyard.
Compressors. One leaf blower two streets over doing the work of an
argument. The PA saying *check, check* into ninety thousand empty seats
and the syllable coming back off the concrete a half-beat late. Ice —
this town's actual morning sound — bags of it going into coolers, one
after another, all over the lot, like distant applause.

**Feel.** Dew through canvas shoes by the second row of tents. Tent legs
still cold enough to hurt your palm. Humidity you wear rather than feel.
The specific damp of a folding chair that sat out all night.

### Kickoff

**Smell.** Sunscreen and hairspray, which is a fall smell here because
people dress up; bourbon-and-Coke in wax cups; fryer oil; hot polyester
from a band that has been in uniform for two hours.

**Sound.** The roar arrives at the House four seconds late and comes
through the walls, not the windows. The drumline puts the sashes in
their frames. A hundred thousand people exhaling at once is audible
blocks away and does not sound like cheering at that distance; it sounds
like weather. Between plays, an unnerving lull in which you can hear a
single cicada in a magnolia and the mower running on the practice field.

**Feel.** Concrete carrying the stomp up through your heels. No cell
signal anywhere within a half mile, which is why the parents' board goes
quiet for three hours every Saturday and detonates at the final whistle.

### Midnight

**Smell.** Smoke gone cold and sour. Wet cardboard. A hundred bags of
melted ice running out of the gutters, which smells like a creek. Fresh
turf paint from the crew re-striping under headlights.

**Sound.** Tent city coming down under the midnight teardown rule —
poles again, but tired. A trash truck's hydraulics. The chapel bell if
they won, still going at one in the morning, rung by strangers `[TK:
Ashford's name for the bell ritual]`. Sprinkler heads ticking on. The
mower.

**Feel.** The first genuinely cool air of the day, arriving four hours
after anybody wanted it. Everything sticky. The lot empty enough that
your own footsteps have an echo, which they did not have at seven.

### Walt's job site — inside Hartwell House

The temperature drops six degrees inside the front door and the smell
changes completely — hot attic dust, old varnish, mouse, and the chalky
ammonia of hundred-year-old plaster; in the crawlspace, cold clay, which
is the one smell in the book that is genuinely pleasant and that he
cannot explain to anybody. Longleaf pine sawdust is resinous and sweet,
nothing like modern lumber, and it is the smell of anything going right.
The House talks: sash weights knocking inside the walls when a door
shuts, and a floor that tells him which room he is in with his eyes shut.
The stair rail is worn to a soft edge that his hand knows. The porch
compressor cycles on and off all afternoon. The AM station. The dog's
tags. Outside, orange flagging tape on the survey stakes ticking against
themselves in the wind, which is the sound of the whole plot.

### The board room when it turns

**The parents' board, when a thread turns.** For Marisol this is
physical, not visual — the phone face-down on the nightstand going off
like popcorn in another room; the specific timing where the notifications
stop for eleven seconds and then come faster; the pause with three dots
in it. The silence after she deletes a comment is louder than the
comment. The 2 a.m. thread has a different quiet altogether — one post
every forty minutes, from women who cannot sleep, and no reactions.

**The physical room, when the vote turns.** Folding chairs on tile.
An HVAC unit that everyone stops hearing until it cycles off mid-motion
and the room learns how quiet it actually is. Mic feedback that makes two
hundred people flinch in the same quarter second. Then the sound that
signals it is over — chairs scraping backward in ones and twos while
somebody is still talking, and a room full of neighbors who have stopped
looking at each other.

## HAUGE FRAME (`studio/craft/hauge.md`)

**Outer goal (visible finish line).** Stop Hartwell House from being
declared unsafe before the report is due. Visible, dated, and either
happens or does not.

**Marisol — inner journey.** Identity: the admin. The woman who holds
twelve thousand opinions and never posts one, because being needed by
strangers is a way of not being alone in a house nobody comes home to.
Wound: the divorce, and two empty bedrooms, and a son who reads texts
without answering them. Essence: speaking as herself, with her name on
it, to a room that may turn on her.

**Walt — inner journey.** Identity: the custodian. The man who keeps
everything standing and asks for nothing, who converted grief into
maintenance because a building can still be taken care of. Wound:
widowhood, four years old and worn smooth, the way a rail is. Essence:
letting the House be repaired — changed — instead of preserved, and
letting himself be wanted in the present tense.

**Five turning points.** Percentages against a proposed 32-chapter
rung-2 build (~2,000 words each).

- **~10% (ch. 3) — Opportunity.** She learns the fight has exactly one
  gatekeeper, and walks onto his porch. Inner: she asks a stranger for
  something as herself, off the clipboard.
- **~25% (ch. 8) — Change of Plans.** The petition clause naming his
  office goes out under the committee's name, not hers, and he agrees
  to walk her through the building anyway. Both journeys turn: her
  first concealment, his first unpaid hour spent on someone living.
- **~50% (ch. 16) — Point of No Return.** The neutrality vote carries
  and his name appears in the signature block the same night; then the
  town meeting, where she asks him on the record and he cannot answer.
  Public, irreversible, and both of them are now inside it.
- **~75% (ch. 24) — Major Setback.** The assessment leaks without its
  scope page. He is destroyed with his own name; she learns her sentence
  did it; his crew reads their layoff in the paper.
- **~90% (ch. 29) — Climax.** He signs, with the scope and the log
  attached. She posts under her own name and turns the petition from a
  refusal into a demand. Each spends the exact armor their identity was
  made of.

## REGISTER BLOCK — register 4, and the widowhood waiver

**Declared register.** 4/10, bright and banter-forward, per the
quartet cadence (`ENGINE-CHECKLIST.md`, emotional register). The
book's dominant mode is a public fight between two competent people in
rooms full of neighbors.

**The flag.** `notes/plot-sweep-2026-08-08.md` §2 raises Walt's
widowhood inside a bright register-4 book as a premise-vs-register
manage flag needing a waiver signed at premise time.

**WAIVER — SIGNED (proposed; author ratifies).** Grief with weight is
retained. The tonal-ambush risk is managed by eight mechanics, all
checkable at outline and draft time.

1. **Disclosed, never sprung.** The widowhood is in the hand-sell line,
   the blurb, and on page one. The reader knows inside 400 words. The
   checklist says ambush earns the one-stars, not darkness; this book
   has no dark surprise to spring.
2. **Grief renders as maintenance, not weeping.** It appears as verbs
   and inventory — which sash he stripped, whose handwriting is in the
   log, the fact that he knows what the House needs down to the
   fastener. That register is active, competent, and compatible with 4.
3. **Amplitude cap.** Four grief-forward beats in the book, exactly one
   at full amplitude — the log in her handwriting, immediately after
   the leak, one scene, ~78%. Never inside the final 10%.
4. **Armor within the chapter.** Every grief beat is followed inside
   the same chapter by work or banter. No two consecutive grief-forward
   chapters.
5. **The comedy is never about her.** Quirky-widower comedy is banned
   outright (STANDARDS 14 — grief handled with weight, never as quirky
   setup). The crew, the board, and the dog carry the jokes.
6. **She is a person, not a shrine.** The late wife gets a name `[TK]`,
   a job (Hartwell fellow — canon), and opinions that are still
   inconvenient. Marisol is never in competition with her, and no scene
   asks her to be.
7. **The loaded gun is retired.** The dog is not the late wife's dog.
   See the strand budget.
8. **Packaging.** The blurb whispers *widower*; it does not whisper
   *tissues*. The tissues promise belongs to Book 1.2, per the X.2
   packaging rhythm.

**The alternative, stated honestly.** If the author declines the
waiver, the only clean fix is recasting Walt from widowed to
divorced-and-done, which costs the memorial motive that makes the House
personal to him rather than professional, costs the grid one of its
five widowed leads, and weakens the maintenance log — the object the
resolution is found in. Recommendation is to sign.

## POWER BRUSH — where the petition-targets-his-signature is named

The sweep flags this must be named on the page, not finessed. It is
named four times, escalating, and it is named in both directions.

1. **~10% (ch. 3), by him.** Walt tells her flatly what her petition
   actually is: a document asking one man to end eleven jobs, and he
   names the number. Plain, early, unromantic.
2. **~25% (ch. 8), by the page.** The clause is quoted verbatim — it
   names the Office of Facilities. The reader watches her write it and
   watches her let the committee's name go on it.
3. **~50% (ch. 16), by her, on the record.** The PONR question at the
   town meeting, into a microphone, in front of his crew. This is the
   power brush at full strength and the book does not soften the room's
   reaction to it.
4. **~78%, by her, about herself.** After the leak, with no one to
   perform for. *I wrote a sentence with a man's job in it and signed
   somebody else's name to it.*

**The other direction, named once (~30%).** He holds a pen that can end
her fight and she cannot appeal it to anyone. She says so; he does not
deny it. The asymmetry runs both ways and the book says so out loud,
which is what keeps it a load-bearing obstacle and not a misunderstanding
(SC4).

## STRAND BUDGET (STANDARDS 24)

**Main story (A).** The signature — will Walt sign, and what does the
town become on either side of that.

**Side romance — one.** The flower-truck owner × the stadium
groundskeeper `[TK: two names]`, carried from the sweep's 1.1 roster.
Both beats-in-main only, never in a detour scene.
*Tether.* Their courtship happens entirely on the expansion's footprint
— his crew is on Walt's payroll, her route and her stall are on the

chapters, the House-garden chapters, and the town meeting.
*It pays into A.* At ~72% the groundskeeper mentions, unromantically,
that he was told to mow around survey stakes weeks before the assessment

**Subplot — one.** The admin war (thread S11). Boyd's sock accounts
capture the parents' board and force a neutrality vote.
*Tether.* Boyd does not need the board to like him; he needs it silent,
because a signature that twelve thousand people are watching is a
different signature. When Marisol loses the seat at the midpoint she
loses the only lever she has that is not Walt, which is what makes the
last quarter cost her something to win.

**Red herrings — two.** Declared in the fair-play table below with the
honest signal that clears each.

**Running totem — one.** The facilities motor-pool shop dog `[TK:
name]` — rides shotgun in the truck, on every site, in no scene's peril
at any point (register wall: never the dog). The standing joke is that
he will not use the House's front step; he goes around to the ramp,
every time, and has for years. At the climax the deficiency list opens
with the front sill.
*Tether, and a boundary.* The dog is a wink, not evidence. The proof is
the maintenance log; the dog was right, and nobody in the book gets to
solve anything with him.

**The late wife's dog — formally out.** The sweep flags it as a loaded
gun against the never-the-dog wall. This premise rules it out of
existence in 1.1 rather than leaving it unwritten, so that no later
drafter reaches for it. There is one dog in this book.

**RED HERRING 1 — the co-moderator `[TK: name]`.** The reader and
Marisol both come to suspect her longest-serving moderator is Boyd's
inside account. Signal that plants it: this person argues for neutrality
before the socks do, and posts at strange hours. **Honest signal that
clears her:** her hours track a real night shift, established at ~30% in
the 2 a.m. thread, and the socks post nine-to-five on weekdays. The
clearing signal is also the discovery — office hours mean a payroll, and
that is the thread that leads to Boyd. Misdirection planted, never
cheated.

**RED HERRING 2 — the House really is unsound.** The reader is invited
to suspect that Walt is suppressing a genuine structural failure to
protect his wife's memorial, which would make him the villain of his own
book. Signals that plant it: he will not let anyone in the crawlspace,
he keeps the log private, and the sill is visibly bad. **Honest signal
that clears it:** the scope-of-work page at ~28%. The building is not
unsound; it is expensively repairable, and it was surveyed with a
question designed to blur those two things. This herring is what makes
the resolution honest — the House needs the work the contract pays for.

**No coincidences.** Nothing in the last quarter is discovered by luck.
The clue chain runs groundskeeper → stakes → timeline; log → scope →
public record; approval logs → shift pattern → payroll. Every link is
someone doing their own job well.
