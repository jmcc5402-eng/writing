The motor pool kept one light on after midnight,
the long fluorescent over the workbench,
and Cal was standing in it when she let herself in the side door.

The drawer stood open, and the drawer stood empty.
Everything he had kept locked in it all season
lay on the bench in rows, squared to the edge of the wood.
Both envelopes, flaps up. Every page, dated.
And on every page the watermark ran corner to corner,
gray as rain coming three fields off: DRAFT — UNSIGNED.

Ratchet crossed the concrete, toenails ticking,
put her chin on Marisol's boot, and left it there,
the whole weight of her head, settled in for the duration.

She had driven over with one question.
She had rehearsed it at the light by the square,
and again in the lot with the engine off,
and now, standing at the edge of his light
with a dog anchoring her to the floor,
she could not get it out of her mouth.
It was a big question.
It asked a careful man to hand over his whole life
and trust her to hold it right side up.

"Cal."

"I know what you drove over to ask," he said.

"I didn't ask it."

"You'd have stood there till Thursday."
He put one hand flat on the stack
and slid the whole of it across the bench to her side.
"Publish it," he said. "All of it. Me too."

She looked at the pages. She looked at him.

"You know what they'll do with the rest of it," she said.

"Same thing they did with the last crop," he said.
"Be wrong loud."

"Yours," he added, and took his hand off the stack.

Her throat closed.
Forty-three years, one marriage, two kids,
a divorce she'd survived mostly out of stubbornness,
and nobody in any of it had ever pushed
the whole of himself across a table at her
and called it hers.
The pages could clear him.
Everything else on that bench could bury him just as easily,
the drafts, the long season of silence
the county had already read wrong once,
and he knew that better than she did,
and he had slid it over anyway, in a shop apron,
at one in the morning, with the same two motions
he'd have used on a brake job.
The nerve of it never showed.
It went into making those two motions look easy.

Here was the part she would not be saying at any war room,
not to Earlene, not to her own bathroom mirror.
She would have taken his word with no pages at all.
She already had, for weeks, against the whole county,
on nothing but him.

Because crying on the evidence helps nobody,
she photographed every sheet where it lay.
He gave his consent out loud, and she made him say it twice,
once for her and once for the record.
Dates plain. Watermark plain, corner to corner, every frame,
the scarred bench wood showing at the margins,
because an honest photograph keeps its edges.

She should have gone home then.
The photos were in her phone.
The pages went back in their envelopes,
the envelopes back in the drawer, the drawer locked.
Nothing in that shop needed her anymore.
She leaned on the bench anyway.

"You should sleep," she said.

"I know it."

The compressor kicked on, ran a while, quit.
Ratchet gave up on her boot,
lay down across both their feet, and sighed.

"The drawings are still behind the seat," Cal said.

"I know it," she said,
and heard whose words she'd borrowed, and kept them.

"The jar had its best day ever yesterday," she said.
"On the lie. Every dollar of it."

"Price it out."

"Already did," she said. "To the dollar."

"Course you did," he said,
and the shop went quiet again, the good kind.

She learned his hands in that light.
Brown at the wrist, nicked at two knuckles,
flat on the bench and still.
The clock over the parts cage said ten past two.
Then it said twenty past,
and neither of them had spoken,
and neither of them had minded.

"It's past two," she said.

"It is."

"I'm not leaving yet."

"I know it," he said.

He shifted until his shoulder rested against hers,
warm through the sleeve, and they stood there
while the shop got cold around the one light,
past tired, past sense,
past every reasonable version of good night,
until the fluorescent stopped being shop light
and turned into a lamp in a room
where the two of them lived.
Nobody published anything. Nobody moved.

---

The Checkerboard turned its lights on at six,
and Earlene Tatum was in the back booth by four past,
which meant the verdict was in.

Marisol slid in across from her.
Eggs arrived that she had not ordered,
over medium, toast on the side,
because the Phams fed you first at the Checkerboard
and took your opinion after.

"Eat," Earlene said. "Verdicts sit better on eggs."

Marisol ate. Earlene salted her own, tried them,
and delivered.

"You can win crooked. You just can't keep it."

She set the salt down and arranged her silverware
into surgical order beside her plate.

"We had astroturf before the internet, honey.
We called it the Chamber of Commerce."

At the Liars' Table somebody's mug went up like a toast.
Verna Poteat, a Tuesday regular and a Sunday empress,
sat among the commoners and let the counter run itself.
Marisol finished her eggs under the verdict
and agreed with every word of it,
which at six in the morning on no sleep
was the nearest thing to church she was going to get.

By half past, her booth was a war room.
Aisha Cole came straight off a night shift
with two phones and triage in her thumbs,
dealing screenshots across the table like gin hands.

"Give me the ugly ones first," Marisol said.

"They're all ugly," Aisha said.
"Some are just better organized.
Fake, fake, real but always like that, fake, fake."

Birdie Pham skipped the counter entirely
and ran her own pot, booth to booth to their booth.

"Drink it or wear it," Birdie said, pouring.

And then the fourth chair, filled at last.

Kat. Her realtor. Her friend from long before Ashford,
the voice from the old life showing up in the flesh,
already mid-story as she slid onto the bench,
as though she'd started it out in the parking lot
for efficiency.

"—so it's his third margarita, and he looks me
dead in the eye and tells me his mother—"

"New batch," Aisha said, and turned a phone around.

Every wine night owed the room one interrupted story.
This one got paid at six-forty in the morning, cold sober.

"Hold that thought till Friday," Kat said,
and put her reading glasses on, and joined the war.
She kept her bag on the bench beside her all morning
and never opened it.
She ordered the pecan waffle
and never got a bite of that either.

Marisol Pruett was a self-employed bookkeeper.
Ten years of strangers' shoeboxes,
other people's invoices, other people's timestamps,
every receipt guilty until dated.
A fake grassroots campaign
is only somebody else's books, kept badly.

She built the post the same as a year-end.
Artifacts in order.

The originals went first, photographed at the shop
with Cal's consent and Cal's name on the consent,
every sheet gray corner to corner, every sheet dated,
DRAFT — UNSIGNED, no crop, all four edges showing.

"Tell me you kept them boring," Kat said.

"Every edge in frame," Marisol said.

"Good. Juries hate art."

Then the leak, read as a document, because it was one.
It had been photographed off a screen,
and there was a glare bar to prove it.
On the wide frames the watermark washed down
to a copier smudge you could talk yourself past.
On the last frame the crop came in so tight
the gray fell outside it at both ends.

"Who taught them to crop?" Kat said.
"There's a glare bar. I've pulled listings
for better photography than this.
When a photo's cropped that hard,
there's a water stain just out of frame. Every time."

"You don't trim a thing that fits."
Verna had said it again by eight,
and by nine the county was saying it with her.
Ronnie down in bay three had been calling the margins
a warranty crop since the first screenshot went around,
so the county owed Ronnie an apology on top of everything.

"A crop that tight got measured," Marisol said.
"You measure what you mean."

The leaked file had never even been renamed.
Kudzu Media LLC sat right there in the file name,
and Kudzu Media LLC sat in eight-point type
on the back page of the gala program,
under the words "digital engagement."
Around nine thirty the county finished reading
its own program.
Who wrote Kudzu its checks, the file did not say,
and Marisol did not say either.
The county could count its own dots.
She had been waiting weeks on the county
to turn that page.
It came with no drums at all, just neighbors reading,
which suited her fine.

The account histories under the screenshots were hers:
Sunday exports, logged with the coffee,
rain or shine or nothing to report,
by a woman who kept receipts on her own life now.
Aisha was dealing off the top of that stack.

"Thirty-one," Aisha said, dealing the last screenshot.
"Thirty-one of the loudest accounts,
and every last one of them spells it ya'll.
Apostrophe on the wrong side. Identically.
I've seen rashes with a more natural distribution."

"Y, apostrophe, all," Marisol said.
"It's a contraction, not a garnish."
And every last one of them had been
quoting the Courier's bound volumes back at a town
that had said all of it first, two mayors ago.

"And Tunk?" Birdie said, pot in hand.

"Cleared by twenty years of Sunday-usher timestamps,"
Marisol said.
"The loudest 'neighbors' posted straight through
every service he ever worked."

At the Liars' Table, Tunk Ferrell took his acquittal
without looking up from his hash browns.
Peanut shook his hand anyway, formally,
like a parole board.

"Somebody should tell Tunk he's famous," Kat said.

"He knows," Earlene said. "He's already insufferable."

Tick raised his mug. "Never trusted a phone," he said.

Birdie's pot came around again,
trailed by toast nobody had ordered.
"Eat," Birdie said.

"Now watch the clock on them," Marisol said,
and turned her own phone to face the table.
Every reply from every one of the accounts,
inside of four minutes, at every hour there was,
two a.m., Sunday morning, suppertime.

"Inside of four," Aisha said.
"I've paged cardiologists with worse response times."

"That's not a neighborhood," Marisol said.
"That's a schedule."

Scrolling for the last of the shop photos,
she passed an old video of her own.
Cal, weeks back, fixing somebody's hinge
in the middle of an argument, screwdriver going.
Her thumb stopped over it.
It would have played beautifully.
She moved on past.
That one wasn't evidence. That one was hers.

"Post it," Aisha said.

"Posted," said Marisol.

Kat took her glasses off.
Aisha put her head back against the booth
and closed her eyes, the night shift catching up.

It went up at ten past ten, artifacts in order,
her name at the top.
She read it through once more and allowed herself
the professional opinion. Airtight.

---

Grapevine gave her the moderator badge back before five,
restored in a notification exactly as small
as the coup had been loud.
She'd expected to feel something when it happened.
She felt about what you feel when a stapler turns up.
She left the group a three-word pinned post.
Evening, my loves.

The pledge jar's best day ever deflated on schedule.
She had priced the lie at the shop in the dark,
and Tuesday paid it back out on her numbers.
The jar kept the part the truth had earned.
She could stand to look at the jar again.

Two allies quit loudly, long posts,
doors shut where the whole county could hear them.
Nobody went after them.

Cal called at suppertime.
She took it out on the porch, and for ten minutes
neither of them mentioned the county at all.

"My table, Thursday," she said. "Bring the dog."

By Friday, Ashford had retold the whole week as a romance.
In the versions that reached her,
the shop light was candlelight,
the workbench was laid like a supper table,
and there was a kiss on the square
that had not happened there yet.
Nobody in any version stayed up past one
photographing watermarks with cold hands.
She corrected nobody.
It was a romance.
