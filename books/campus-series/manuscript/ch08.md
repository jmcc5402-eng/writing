> **On Grapevine, Ashford —**
>
> **Marv Kessler:** Delmar's was out of chopped by one o'clock again. I
> have written to them about this. I will be writing to them again.

Cal wanted barbecue.

He wanted a chopped plate, extra pickle, and forty minutes
where nobody said the word House.
He wanted it at Delmar's, on a Wednesday.
That was like wanting dry socks in a creek.

The crew's trucks were already in the gravel.
Ratchet rode with her head out the window,
reading the smoke the whole last block.
Hickory, vinegar, black pepper, butcher paper.
Three generations of it, soaked into the cinderblock.
Out back the frozen-margarita machine ran off a generator
next to the smoker,
which said everything about the year Delmar's was having.
Cal parked, cracked the windows,
and told Ratchet to guard the truck.
She lay down on the seat and did not.

He saw Marisol from the lot.

She was up on the screen porch with her spiral notebook,
working a table of strangers like she had been born on Millrow.
Cal stood at the tailgate for one breath
and considered sending somebody in for his plate.
He could eat in the cab. Men had done it.

He crossed the lot and took the steps.
He had known she was up there. He took them anyway.

"Afternoon," he said, to the porch in general,
and went inside to the register,
where Ray had watched the whole approach
and charged him nothing for it.

The register had a QR code taped where the menu used to be,
and a hand-lettered card that said OAT MILK — YES, FINE,
UNDER PROTEST, the protest in very small print.
And it had the clipboard.
It sat by the till where the mint bowl used to live.
A plain clipboard. A pen tied on with string.
Pages deep. Cal did not flip them.
He could read a stack from four feet, and it was a thick stack.

"Chopped plate. Extra pickle."

"Heard." Ray cut the ticket. He looked at Cal.
Then at the clipboard. Then back. "You want the pen?"

"I want the pickle."

"Some men want both."

The porch door opened behind him and Marisol walked a stranger in,
a dad in a golf shirt, and set the clipboard in front of him.
Square to the counter.
Pen laid across at an angle, handle toward the man.
Offered, not pushed.
Her hands did all of it without her watching them do it.
Cal made himself study the QR code instead.
It held no new information.

The dad signed. Marisol thanked him by name.
She had known him for roughly ninety seconds.
The thank-you came with a smile, straight on, dead level.
Every smile Cal had ever gotten off that woman
had gone on past his ear,
and he had not counted that a loss until right now.
"And the laundry duffel rides home with your boy at Thanksgiving,"
she told him, "not ahead of him."

"Mr. Sutter," she said, without turning around.
"You're blocking the guest book."

"That what we're calling it."

"That's what it is." Now she turned.
"The first one lives at the House.
This one comes to the people."

"Clever," he said, and meant it, which annoyed him.

She pushed the screen door open with her hip and held it.
"Your table's out here, I believe."

The porch had a dozen chairs, and most of them were safe.
Cal turned one around and sat where he could see up Millrow.
He always sat where he could see up Millrow.
Nobody had ever asked him why, and nobody asked now.

Earlene Tatum had the rail seat, a lemonade,
and a pair of binoculars she referred to as her reading glasses.
She had them trained on a doorway across the street.
"Reconnaissance," she said, to nobody who had asked her anything.

Verna Poteat held the shady table like a deed to it.
Over the register the little radio ran 1340,
Tick Moran reading the noon stock report like a bedtime story.
And behind the screen door, at the inside tables,
Cal's whole crew sat over their plates and did not chew.

Marisol stayed standing. She set the notebook on the rail.
"Since I have you," she said,
at a volume that was not for him.
It was for the porch. Fine. He could do porch volume.

"You don't have me. I'm waiting on a plate."

"Since the town has you, then. Here's my whole case.
Everything on Millrow runs through your office.
When the university wants to know what a building is worth,
they ask one man. They ask you.
So I am asking you, in front of witnesses,
to know what that one is worth."
She did not point at the House. She did not have to.
Everybody on that porch had been born knowing where it stood.

"Pages of names," she said,
with a tip of her head at the register.
"Pages. And more coming."

"That clipboard's got one name on it, ma'am. Mine."
Cal tipped his chair onto its back legs.
"You can tie a pen to it and fill a hundred pages.
It's still addressed to me."

"Then read your mail."

It got him. The laugh was out before he could vote on it.
Short. Real. One.
The corner of his mouth went somewhere it hadn't been invited,
and the porch saw it go.
Her face caught the laugh and kept it,
bright for exactly the length of the sound and not a second more,
which was somehow worse.
Behind the screen door, the forks went quiet.

That was the thing about the inside tables.
Twelve people ate lunch back there,
and their checks came through his office,
and to them her guest book was not flowers.
It was a list, and lists have a bottom.
His second was watching him through the screen
with her tea stopped halfway up.
Cal put his chair back on four legs.

"Now my case," he said.
"I keep buildings standing for a living. Old ones too.
I doubt them for a living. That's the job.
That porch down there's got a dozen good years in it, minded right.
Love is not a line item. Payroll is.
I've got a budget that has to make winter,
and it doesn't care how anybody feels. Me included."

There was more he was not saying, and he did not say it.

"It's not a building file," she said,
and laid her hand flat on the notebook.
"I started writing down the people.
If we fight about joists, I lose.
I can't shim anything.
But that building is where this town sits down.
It's the living room.
Your pen is aimed at the town's living room,
and you know it, and you would rather talk about pickles."

"Living rooms fall on people.
Somebody has to measure them.
You want the measuring done by somebody who eats here,
or somebody who doesn't?"

That one landed. He watched it land.
The porch made the low sound a crowd makes at a good return.
Sonny Dillard came up the steps for a to-go order
and took the rail instead of the register.
"Don't stop on my account," Sonny said. "This beats the radio."

"You made that exact speech at table nine," Marisol said.
"It was better with the zinnias. I beat it then, too."

"You called it a draw at the time."

"I was being a lady at the time."

She was enjoying this. That was the trouble.
She argued with her whole reach, hands open, chin up,
having the time of her life at his expense.
Her hair was coming out of its knot a strand at a time,
and she hadn't spared one hand to fix it,
and her mouth was already amused at things she hadn't said yet.
He had opinions about that,
and he kept them off his face with real effort,
and he was not sure it was working.

The screen door banged. It had been banging all lunch,
loose in the top hinge, screw backed half out.
Cal stood up in the middle of somebody's sentence,
possibly his own, opened the knife off his belt,
and ran the screw home.
He sat back down still talking.
The door hung quiet. The porch pretended not to notice.
Marisol's phone came out of her pocket on reflex
and caught it, the door, the porch, him, probably.
She saw him see her do it.
She put the phone away, unrepentant.

Verna spoke from the shade.
"That is not a fight," she said, to her iced tea.
"I have watched fights on this porch for sixty years.
That is a dance where they're both too proud to pick the song."
She smoothed her napkin.
"Also. Somebody has been leaving late tomatoes
on my step at Magnolia Court.
I have not decided to notice yet."

"Notice," said Earlene, not lowering the binoculars.

"Say the loud part plain," Marisol said, quieter,
which on that porch meant everybody leaned in.
"November. Things get decided in November.
After that it's all condolences."

"I know what month it is."

"And I know whose pen it is."

"Then we understand each other," he said,
and the porch went away for a second.
She held his eyes and he held hers,
and it ran one beat past the place
where either of them could have called it an accident.
Her glance dropped to his hands, quick,
and broke quicker, eyes down,
one loose strand of hair sent back behind her ear,
and a debt neither of them had settled sat in the air,
and neither of them claimed it.

The register bell rang like a fire drill.
"Sutter!" Ray called through the screen. "Chopped plate.
Been up so long I'm fixing to charge you rent on it."

The porch broke up. Cal went and got his plate.

When he came back out she had taken the chair
across from his, not asking,
the notebook squared in front of her.

"Rules," she said.
"Since we are clearly going to keep doing this.
I won't make it personal.
I will fight your pen with everything I've got,
in every room in this county.
The man holding it, I leave alone."

Cal set the plate down and took his time.

"Then here's mine. I won't pretend it isn't personal.
It's people's jobs and it's people's porch.
There's no version of this where nobody bleeds,
and I won't stand on this porch and call it paperwork.
Not to you. Not to them."
He nodded at the screen door, and at the street,
and let it cover everybody either way.

"Deal," she said, and put her hand out.

He shook it. It was a working grip, warm and certain of itself.
He let go on time, and it cost him something.

She stood, collected the notebook,
and gathered the porch back up with one look,
a hostess to the bone.
"Enjoy the plate, Mr. Sutter. Chew everything.
Come November I want you at full strength."

That made twice. He heard himself do it,
and behind the screen door the crew could count.

She went down the steps and along Millrow.
Earlene finally lowered the binoculars.
"Well," she said, to the street at large.

Ray came as far as the doorway with a rag over his shoulder
and considered what was left of his porch.
"Talk's smoke," he said. "Anybody want dessert?"

Nobody had won. Cal ran the whole thing back while he ate
and could not find a yielded inch anywhere on that porch,
hers or his.
Her case was true. So was his.
That was the entire trouble,
and it was going to stay the trouble clean through November.

The plate had gone cold somewhere in the second round.
He ate every bite of it anyway, facing up Millrow,
the crew quiet at his back and the door hanging true,
and he would have told you it was the pickle
that made it the best lunch he'd had in a year.
He had come in wanting no conversation.
He was leaving with a standing fight and a shook hand,
and he'd have paid real money to keep the loud going another hour.
