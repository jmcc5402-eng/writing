Marisol took the long way home on purpose,
up Millrow with the square at her back,
because it was Sunday, and the street was quiet,
and the House was on it.
That was the whole itinerary.
She had decided not to examine it.

Her phone went off twice in her pocket on the way up the hill,
the parents' group getting an early start on Monday,
and she left it there both times,
which was its own small Sunday miracle.

The sound reached her at the two oaks.
A long, even shearing, wood giving itself up in ribbons,
then a pause, then the stroke again,
patient as somebody rowing across a small lake.
Under the cicadas it was the only human sound on the street,
and it was coming from inside Hartwell House.

The front door stood open.
On the bottom step the mum sat where it had landed
two Saturdays back, big as a dryer drum,
claimed by nobody and known to everybody.

She had no business following that sound, and she knew it.
What she wanted was small and true and hers.
One minute standing inside the thing
the whole town was suddenly carrying around in its phones.
She went up the walk between the oaks and in.

The hall was cooler than the street.
It smelled of fresh shavings laid over old floor wax,
and Cal Sutter was kneeling on the third step of the staircase
with a wood plane in both hands.

Nobody had turned a light on.
The day came in through the open door instead
and lay down the length of the floorboards,
and dust moved through it, slow, like it paid rent.

He had set himself sideways on the step,
angled so the open door stayed in front of him,
which she put down to craft or habit.
He didn't look up.
The plane traveled the tread and lifted a curl of pale wood
thin enough to read a letter through,
and his forearms did all the work of it,
brown from the summer, unhurried,
the green polo staying out of the way like it had been trained to.
She gave herself one second to notice and spent three.

"The door was open," Marisol said.

"Door's been open a hundred years," Cal said, to the step.
"That's most of what's wrong with this town."

"You're the one making nineteenth-century noises on a Sunday."

"Wood doesn't know it's Sunday."

She came the rest of the way in,
and that was when she saw the wall.

Photographs, all the way up the staircase.
The same photograph, she understood after a moment,
taken a hundred times across a hundred years.
Different frames, different dresses, different wars,
and in every one of them a couple standing on the stairs,
and in every one of them the same stair.
Third from the bottom.
The one under his knees.

"Tell me who they are," she said.

"That's Verna and her Mr. Poteat," Cal said, not looking up,
the plane making its patient run.
"Bottom row. Magnolia Court sign was so new
you can about see the paint shine off it in the picture.
She gives the full history. She charges."

Marisol leaned in.
A girl with a grip on her young man's arm like a title deed,
and behind the two of them this same hall, younger.
"Verna," she said. "Verna at the motel."

"Verna at the motel.
Two frames up, that's the Phams.
Diner keys still on his belt. You can just make them out."

She could.
A crowded key ring at a dark-suited hip,
two faces trying to be solemn for the camera and losing.

"Keep going," she said.

"Soldier, there, above the landing."
The plane never broke rhythm.
"Army gave him two weeks.
The two of them took every one of them."

Her eyes went on up the wall ahead of his voice.
Near the top hung a frame like all the others.
Same hall, same step, two faces she couldn't make out from below.

He kept naming, a Pham cousin, a Poteat by marriage,
the soldier's people again, on up the wall.

"And that's the end of the tour," Cal said, right past it,
no hitch in the words at all.

The plane's stroke ran a half inch longer than the others.
That was all.
She looked at the back of his neck, and she did not ask.

"I'd pay," she said instead. "What's Verna charge?"

"Depends how good the story is.
The good ones run you a pie."

"Who hangs them all? Who takes the picture now?"

"They get hung." The plane came back to the tread.
"Wall's got room for another forty, fifty,
if folks keep the frames sensible."

She looked up the staircase at all that room.
She did not say the month, and neither did he.

She came to the foot of the stairs.
This close, she could see what he was kneeling on.
The third tread was worn pale at its center,
one soft-edged hollow the color of scrubbed pine
in a staircase gone dark everywhere else with age and wax.

"Why's this one pale?" she said,
though she was already counting couples on the wall
and arriving at the answer.

"Hundred years of knees."
Cal sat back on his heels and looked at the tread instead of her.
"Third step's the kneeling step. Always has been.
Second's too low. Fourth's showing off."

The laugh got out of her before she could dress it up.
The hall took it and handed it back off all that old plaster,
doubled, like the house had been waiting on one.
"And you're up here planing it."

"Keeping it level."

"If you plane it," she said, "don't you plane them off?
The knees. The hundred years."

"A sixteenth of an inch of splinters is all I took."
He fit the plane back into its stroke.
"The hollow stays. That deep, it's not wear anymore.
It's the shape of the step."

He set the plane on the tread above,
squared so the blade hung over nothing,
and ran his thumb slow along the fresh edge of the wood.
"Tread's got another hundred years in it, minded right.
Banister, forty. Porch boards, ten."
The thumb went back along the edge the other way.
"Fifteen if somebody loved them."

"Job security," he added, before she could ask,
which answered the question and didn't,
and they both knew both things.

He looked up at her then, the first full look since the welcome tent,
and it went one beat past friendly,
and neither of them filed the paperwork on it.
His hands, for once, were doing nothing at all.
Heat arrived at the back of her neck and stayed,
on no schedule she had approved.

Laughter came up the walk through the open door,
and he didn't have to turn toward it,
because he'd knelt himself facing that door all along.

Out past the oaks, a man of the town went by on the sidewalk
carrying a casserole dish with a checked towel over it,
and a woman of the town walked beside him,
making no move at all to take it back.
At the corner of the House's fence she reached over
and straightened the towel, which did not need straightening,
and he stood and let the towel be straightened,
holding that dish level like it had a bubble in it,
and whatever she said next bent him in the middle laughing.

"Potluck let out," Cal said.

"Do we wave?" Marisol said.

"They'd have to look away from each other first.
Give them a week."

The two of them went on up Millrow, slow,
like the whole afternoon had been canceled in their favor.

He swept the shavings off the tread with the flat of his hand,
went down the steps collecting curls off each one,
and fed the handful into an empty coffee can
that was on the floor there for exactly that.
The plane got wiped with a rag before it went in the toolbox.
The rag got folded in thirds before it went in after.
She watched his hands put the room away
and understood she was watching a liturgy.

"Somebody ought to thank you," Marisol said.

"It's a step," Cal said. "It was getting out of level.
Now it's less out of level."

"On behalf of everybody about to trip on it less, then,"
she said, smiling at a point somewhere past his ear.

He stood and took the toolbox up in one hand,
and the shoulders under the green polo
briefly reorganized her opinion of Sundays.
She let the opinion stand.

"Pull the door to when you go," Cal said,
already headed down the back hall.

"I thought the door stays open."

"It does." His voice came back around a corner.
"Pull it to anyway. House likes to know somebody minded."

Somewhere at the back of the building a door met its frame,
and then it was her and the hall
and a hundred years of couples looking down the stairs at her.

She meant to go.
The hall table stood by the door,
and on it lay the House guest book,
fat with itself, pages ruffled tall from all the hands.
She tipped the cover back with one finger,
meaning it as the last idle thing before leaving,
turned through a page or two of strangers,
and stopped,
because the handwriting halfway down one page was her own.

Her name, in her own hand, in this hall.
The line beside it said her son's drop-off day.

She held the page flat and let that day come back in pieces.
Bins in the truck. The duffel.
A hundred signatures before noon, forms and waivers,
her own name over and over until it stopped looking like a word.
And somewhere in the middle of all of it, apparently,
a cool hallway, a table, a pen on offer,
and she had signed and walked out
without ever once looking up.

She had stood in this house.
She had been ten feet from that wall.

Her thumb had the phone out and the camera up
before the rest of her had voted on it.
She took the picture of the page.
She sent it to nobody, which, for her, was practically a vow.

The hall stayed quiet.
Somewhere out back, metal was being put away, unhurried.
She crossed to the staircase and went up.
One step, two,
and she set both feet in the pale place on the third
and stood where all of them had knelt.

The new wood was silk underfoot, and level,
dead level, the kind you could set a marble on
and the marble would have to make up its own mind.
She stood where a hundred years of the town had stood
at the exact moment their lives folded open,
and she gave it a minute.
A real one, sixty full seconds, on purpose,
with the couples looking down at her,
and the cicadas going in the oaks,
and her own name ten feet away in a book she'd never meant to sign.

Then she stepped down and let herself out,
and pulled the door to behind her with both hands,
easing the latch over soft, like he'd asked.

Twelve thousand members heard from her before the day was out,
about parking, and bedding sizes, and the eleven p.m. Venmo economy.
Not one of them heard about the step.
