> **On Grapevine, Ashford —**
>
> **Marv Kessler:** Went by the stadium at six this morning. Lights on,
> nobody in it. Somebody in this town is already at work while the rest of
> us sleep.

The stadium on a Friday dawn was the biggest room Cal had a key to,
and the only one that was ever honestly empty.

Work lights stood over the field on their yellow masts,
and under them the turf lay mowed in checkerboard,
every square sharp as a pressed collar,
ninety thousand seats holding their breath around it.
Seven Saturdays a year this was the third-largest city in the state.
At six on a Friday it was a church nobody had unlocked yet,
and Cal had come for the north tunnel gate,
whose roller had been dragging since the last home Saturday
and was going to roll right or be replaced
before anybody had to ask him twice.

He had the old roller out and the new one seated
when an engine geared down out in the lot,
one he knew from the curb market, and quit.
Two doors. One of them took two tries.

He ran the gate down its track to test it, and it went quiet,
and while he had it open Eli Fountain came up the tunnel
out of the dark with Birdie Pham beside him,
carrying one cup of coffee level in front of him
with the concentration most men save for open flame.

Cal stood where he was and held the gate.

Eli nodded.
Cal nodded back.
Both parties considered the matter fully discussed.

Birdie stopped where the concrete quit and the grass began,
boot toes on the last inch of tunnel,
the same as if the field were water
and nobody had said the temperature.

"Eli," she said. "People get run off this grass.
I have personally watched you run off a dean."

"The dean had golf spikes on."

"And me?"

Eli looked down at her boots for a serious second.

"Those'll do," he said.

She stepped on, careful, then less careful.
They went out side by side under the work lights, not touching,
Eli steering her wide of the numbers.

"What's the checkerboard for?" she said. "Bragging?"

"Light. The blades lean two ways. Catches the sun two ways."

"So, bragging."

A pause, the length of an honest man checking his answer.

"Some," Eli said.

At the fifty he stopped and handed the coffee over,
and she took it in both hands and turned all the way around once,
just looking, the empty bowl of the place standing up
on every side of her.

"How often do you mow it?" she said.
An empty stadium keeps nothing to itself;
her voice came all the way back up the tunnel.

"Every other day, in season."

"The whole thing?"

"Crew of four."

"And the fifty?"

"The fifty I do," Eli said.

"You know what this is?" Birdie said.
"This is the biggest flower bed in the state,
and one man grows the whole thing."

"It's rye," Eli said. "Mostly."

"Take the compliment."

"It's a cool-season grass," Eli told her,
which, from Eli, was the compliment being taken.

She handed him back the cup.
Then she put her arms straight out and turned a slow circle
in the middle of the fifty, head back, boots planted on the paint,
turning under the work lights with the whole checkerboard
running out flat and perfect around her,
and Eli Fountain stood holding her coffee in both hands
and watched her do it,
and never once checked the turf for her bootprints.

"If you cry," Birdie called, mid-turn, "I'm picking some."

"There's nothing out here to pick."

"I'd find something."

She came to a stop facing him, a little unsteady from the turning,
and laughed at her own feet, and he put out a hand
and she didn't need it and held onto it anyway.

"All right," she said, to the sky as much as anybody.
"Now I've been somewhere."

"Grass'll be here," Eli said.

The gate should have been shut already.
Cal knew it; shutting it was his own errand.
He stood another minute anyway, one hand on the chain-link,
past the place where watching still had an excuse,
and then a minute after that,
because a town only does certain things once,
and somebody ought to see them done.

The cup was the thing that followed him out.
Eli had carried it level across the whole dark lot
and the length of the tunnel and had not drunk from it,
and would not, because Eli Fountain did not drink coffee
and never had.
The cup was hers. It had been hers the entire way.

Cal eased the gate along its new roller until it kissed the post,
left it off the latch, and went up the concourse the way he'd come.
Half a lifetime of unlocking buildings for other people's mornings,
and it had never cost him one thing before breakfast until today.

---

The strangers were on Millrow by nine.

Three of them, clean trucks, cleaner boots,
tripods ringed around the east end of Hartwell House
where the old crack came down through the brick,
little glass instruments on yellow legs
staring at that wall from three directions at once.
The appointment had come down through his own office
with the paperwork already stapled to it,
and Cal had read the paperwork twice
and put it back in its envelope both times.

He stayed the morning. Nobody had asked him to.
There were reasons enough on a house that age.
The porch rail wanted a screw.
The downspout at the northeast corner had a clot in it
he could hear from the walk,
and he went up the ladder and broke it loose one-handed,
the pull stopping at the elbow, the ladder none the wiser,
and ran the hose until the downspout had nothing further to say.
There was a coffee can of zinnias on the east porch rail,
and Cal topped the can up at the hose bib on his way past,
which he would not have called a habit,
though the zinnias could have.
The front door stood open to the daylight
the way it stood open every day of its life,
and Cal worked the House's small aches under the oaks
and kept the strangers in the corner of his eye,
and they never learned one thing about being minded.

Verna Poteat came up the sidewalk at ten,
on the long half of her walk from the Magnolia Court,
and settled at the fence like a woman taking a reserved seat.

"Those tripods," she said. "Television, or worse?"

"Measuring."

"Measuring what?"

"The east wall."

"Mm." Verna watched the man with the tablet call a number
to the man with the notebook.
"Insurance men," she decided. "They've got the shoes for it."

Cal let her keep it.
It was a harmless story, and it was hers,
and he was not in the business of handing out the other kind.

"How long have they been at it?"

"Since nine."

"There's two reasons a man measures a thing, Cal.
To mend it or to price it."

"They didn't say which."

"No," Verna said. "They never do."

He came down the ladder with the hose coiled over one shoulder,
and she watched the whole descent without apology.

"Mr. Poteat went up a ladder like that," she said,
to the street more than to him.
"At the Court, back when the neon was new.
I married him by Christmas.
You be careful who's watching you, Cal Sutter."

"Yes, ma'am."

"That wasn't a warning," she said,
and went on up Millrow with her hat brim down,
satisfied, her morning's errand complete.

He thought then, unbudgeted, of what Marisol would do
with three tripods and a Friday morning.
She would have the crew named, fed, and testifying by ten thirty.
He could see it without closing his eyes,
chin up, arguing with her whole reach,
hair coming out of its knot one strand at a time
while the county rearranged itself into the shape she wanted.
He put the picture away twice.
It did not stay put either time.

One of the crew came across the yard at eleven for the hose bib.

"Mind if we top off?"

"Help yourself."

The man filled his jug and stood a second looking up
at the whole tall face of the House.
"Old girl holds her lines," he said.
"Straighter than my house, and mine's got thirty years on me."

"She does," Cal said, and left it at that.

They cased their instruments at half past one.
The chief brought a bound packet across the yard,
spiral spine, his office's own letterhead on the cover.

"You the Sutter on the letterhead?"

"I'm Sutter."

"Field data goes to the engineer of record.
Methodology's the back section. I'm told you get the copy."
He looked at the House once more and got in the truck.
"We shoot what's there.
What they grade it against is somebody else's shop.
They send expectation sheets. Helps us pack the right gear.
Good luck with it."

They took their tripods off Millrow
and left the street to the oaks and the cicadas.

---

Cal went up the walk and in at the open front door.

The parlor held its early-afternoon cool.
The crack came down the inside of the east wall,
old plaster over older brick,
wandering the height of the room
with a river's disregard for straight lines.

And glued across it, every foot or so, rode the slides.

Microscope glass, set in little dabs of epoxy,
each one bridging the crack,
each one with a date inked small in the corner.
Glass was the honest material; that was the whole trick of it.
A wall can creep too slow for any eye to catch,
but glass won't stretch and glass won't lie,
and if the crack so much as breathed,
the glass would show a line.

The dates were in his wife's handwriting.

Small, square, patient, the ink gone tea-colored
up near the crown molding and darker toward the floor,
laid down season by season in the years she kept the watch.
The oldest date, at the top of the wall,
was nineteen years old.

Not one slide carried so much as a hairline.

Not the oldest, up where the plaster met the crown.
Not the newest, under his hand by the switch.
Nineteen years of dates in a hand nobody was adding to,
and the wall had not moved the width of a pencil line.
The crack was a scar, not a wound.
She had asked this house the honest question,
glued the answer up where anybody could read it,
and the house had spent every year since answering her.

He stood in front of that wall a long time.
Longer than checking takes.
Long enough that the light moved on the floorboards,
and he was still standing there when the carillon
let go of two o'clock up the hill, unhurried,
and the sound came down Millrow the same as it came every day,
and let him off.

He counted the slides. Eleven.
He photographed every one with the date plain in the frame,
top of the wall to the floor, in order,
and then did a thing he had not planned on doing,
and laid two fingers flat against the newest glass,
cool under them, her writing in the corner,
the same two fingers he'd hold on a supply line
to feel whether a boiler meant it.

The wall stood. It had been standing the whole time.

He went out through the open front door into the daylight
and left it standing open behind him, because it was day,
and that was the House's rule, and the House kept its rules.

---

His second raised him on the radio at five.

"North dorms are asking about heat. The chill's settled in to stay."

"Boilers go on Monday. Tell them that's what blankets are for."

"They'll quote you."

"They always do."

He fed Ratchet at six.
She ate her dinner and then came and lay down
by the truck's front tire, closer than usual,
which was as much as Ratchet ever said about anything.

He sat in the cab in the shop yard
with the dome light on and read the packet through.

The field data was numbers, and the numbers were honest.
He'd have been surprised by anything else;
the men had clean instruments and no reason to lie.
It was the back section that had been worth the whole day.
The ground rules.

Every structure on the Millrow list would be graded
against current code.
Not the code it was built under.
The code for a house going up this year.
Stair rise. Rail height. Wire gauge. Footing depth.
Pass or fail, line by line, and no credit anywhere for standing.

He read it twice, because he was thorough,
and the second reading was worse.

Graded on those rules, Hartwell House failed
before anybody climbed out of a truck.
So did the old opera house the town did its voting in.
So did the Magnolia Court, and the Checkerboard,
and every church on the Fifth-Sunday circuit,
and, if a man got honest about wire gauge, Delmar's.
It was not a test. A test can be passed.
It was a finding with the date left blank,
and the signature line under it, waiting, was his.

And against it he had eleven pieces of glass.
A wall that had not moved since the dates ran out,
and the dates in a hand nobody was ever going to add to.
Somebody had asked the honest question first
and left the answer glued where anyone could read it,
and the men with the clean boots had stood all day on the lawn
and never once gone inside to read it.

He thought about the porch at Delmar's, and read your mail,
and her laugh going low through the middle
with the break at the top,
and how she was the one person in the county
he'd most like to hand this packet to,
and the one person in the county he couldn't,
not while twelve paychecks stood
between the House and the winter.

There was a box of manila folders in the office,
and he went and got one
and did not turn on the shop lights to do it.
The packet went in.
The photographs would go in come morning,
printed off the office printer, dates and all, in order.
He wrote nothing on the tab.
Anybody who needed the tab explained
had no business opening the folder,
and nobody was going to open the folder but him.

He set it behind the bench seat,
in the milk crate with the House's parts,
cash-paid, off every book the campus kept,
which was the only filing system on earth he trusted with it.

Ratchet saw him to the gate and went back to her spot.

He drove home with the windows down and the radio off,
past porches with their flags out and their lights going down,
the House dark on its street where he'd left it,
front door long since pulled to, the side door minded,
the whole town sleeping easy on its own side of November.
Somebody always had to be first to see a thing for what it was.
His wife had been, about the wall.
Now he was, about the paper,
and between the glass and the ground rules,
the whole truth of that house was down to the two of them.
