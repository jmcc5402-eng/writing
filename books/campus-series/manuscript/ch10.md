> **On Grapevine, Ashford County —**
>
> **Junior Pettit:** Doss Feed has the fall roses in. Fair warning, they
> will be gone by noon Saturday. Same every year. Somebody always beats
> you to them.

The Saturday list ran six items in his second's handwriting,
and a seventh at the bottom in Cal's own,
added the same week every fall,
and he had gotten through this week without looking at it much.

On an away Saturday the crowd noise was four hours down the road,
and nobody needed him to attend one thing.

Doss Farm & Feed held down the east road out of town,
1946 on the lintel and alfalfa on the air,
pellets and leather and eighty years of creosote in the floorboards,
the one checkout line in the county
where town and county stood together and behaved.
The register had grown a QR code over the summer,
taped down hard at all four corners
by a man who wanted it understood the tape was not his idea.
Across the front, where the salt blocks used to winter,
ran a wall of coolers in colors no barn ever asked for.

"Sutter." Sonny Dillard had the counter,
navy work shirt gone soft at the name over the pocket,
his fire radio parked by the till where it lived.
He took the list, read it top to bottom once,
laid it face down, and came out from behind the counter
already walking.

Cal followed and mostly carried the basket.
Sonny pulled hose menders from the third bin without slowing down,
counted hinge screws out of the drawer by feel, two dozen even,
and stopped Cal's hand a foot short of the float valves.

"Not that one.
They quit making yours in April.
The new one's threaded a quarter-inch off."
He set the right valve in the basket
and laid the adapter next to it like a second opinion.
"Adapter's ninety cents. The drive back is not.
I've been giving this speech all week."

"How many takers?"

"Nine. You're the first one didn't argue the thread."

They came up front past the cooler wall,
and Cal nodded at it.
"Somebody in this county's paying four hundred dollars to keep ice."

"Paying it smiling. Can't keep the white ones in stock.
Daddy sold salt blocks off that wall for thirty years.
Now I sell coolers a man can leave in his will."

"When'd the register learn to read phones?"

"June. Boy from the bank said it'd bring in the young people."
Sonny rang the screws, the menders, the valve,
the ninety-cent adapter.
"Young people were already coming in.
Feed's feed. But the tape holds, so it stays."
He rang one ball of jute twine last.

"Jute rots."

"That's what I like about it."

Cal set cash on the counter beside the QR code,
which he did not remark on.

"Been a parade through here all week about that House,"
Sonny said, making change without looking at the drawer.
"Thursday a man wanted me to say tearing it down was a crime.
Friday another one wanted me to say keeping it was foolishness.
I sold them both washers."
He counted the change into Cal's palm, exact the first time.
"The House? Good bones, bad wiring. Same as the rest of us."

He bagged the twine.

"Burn ban's still on," Sonny said, by way of goodbye.

"I'm tying roses, Sonny. Not lighting them."

"That's what everybody says right up until the brush pile."

"Sonny," Cal said, taking the bag.

"Sutter. Mind your gutters. Fall's not asking."

***

Millrow at midmorning on a Saturday was flags and cut grass
and somebody's sprinkler arguing with the sidewalk,
and Hartwell House stood open for the daylight,
front door wide, same as a hundred years of Saturdays,
the mum still holding the bottom step,
bronze and enormous and unclaimed,
because nobody had moved it and nobody was going to.

The rosebush owned the east end of the porch.
A climber, trained up the lattice and past the rail,
canes run long and loose where summer had pushed them over the top,
budded heavy from the porch floor up,
every bud still shut.

He'd cut the deadwood out first thing
and piled it by the steps, a season of it, thorned and awkward.
Then he cut his lengths of twine and laid them over the rail,
pulled his gloves on,
and started in at the left, gathering, easing, tying.
Ten minutes, once a year, him and her roses.
He kept his head down and his hands moving.
That was the drill, and the drill held.

He was three ties in when footsteps came up the walk and stopped.
Then they started again, going the other way,
quiet about it, gravel giving her up one careful step at a time,
and in a pocket a phone buzzed, and buzzed,
twelve thousand people needing somebody one at a time.

He didn't turn around.
"Hand me that twine."

The footsteps stopped a second time.
"You didn't even look."

"Your pocket's been going off since the gate.
One phone in this county sounds like that."

"It could be anybody's phone."

"It buzzed nine times coming up one walk."

"Saturday is our busy day," Marisol said, with dignity,
and came up the steps.

She had a coffee can of zinnias riding one arm,
orange with no apologies in it anywhere.
She set the can on the rail,
picked the ball of twine off the post by the steps,
and looked at his hands, full of cane.

"Two arm's lengths," he said. "Snips are on the rail.
Then hold these where I put them and don't let them spring."

She cut the twine clean on the first try
and took the canes where he set them, both hands,
steady against the spring of them, thorn-close and unbothered,
and Cal tied in his knots a half beat slower
than any knot required.

"Birdie's?" he said, meaning the zinnias.

"Birdie's. She asked who they were for.
I said the rail.
She charged me the singing price anyway."

"So this is what facilities does with a Saturday."
She was watching the knots go in.
"There's no work order in this, is there."

"This isn't facilities."

"Then what's the twine for?"

"Wind. October wind gets organized up here.
Tied in, she rides it out.
Loose, she beats herself to pieces on her own lattice."

"*She.*" A strand of hair had come loose across her cheek,
and she left it there, both hands being spoken for.
"You named my tent, too."

"Tent was a she. This one came named."

"Who planted her?" Marisol said.

"My wife," Cal said, to the canes.
"Her fellowship year.
Whole street blooms in April and quits by the Fourth.
This one holds out till October and then does it all at once.
Out of spite, she said. Her word for it."

Marisol laughed, quick and complete, her whole face going into it,
and then she caught it,
one hand starting up from the canes and stopping,
and looked at him,
checking whether laughing was a thing this porch allowed.

His shoulders came down an inch.
He felt them go, and he let them stay down.
"It's allowed," he said. "She'd have counted it as applause."

"Is it going to bloom late this year?"

"Buds are set. October, if she holds to form."

"Then she's still winning," Marisol said.

"Every year."

The hand went back to the canes.
They finished the run of the bush without much talking,
cane by cane, hold and tie, the bees working the shut buds,
and it went easy in a manner Cal declined to examine.

"For the record," she said, at the last tie,
"I have not said one word about pens."

"Noted in the minutes."

"It's Saturday. Even the war takes Saturdays here.
I'm nearly sure it's in the bylaws."

He pulled his gloves off, gathered the deadwood pile
off the boards in one arm, carried it down the walk,
and swung it up into the truck bed,
and came back brushing his palm on his jeans.
She had watched the whole trip from the rail.

"You can't stand in the sun in front of roses looking like that,
you know," she said.
"Somebody on this street is going to call somebody."

"There's a number for that," he said. "It rings my office."

She laughed again, and the laugh settled,
and she looked at him and he looked back,
and neither of them looked away on schedule,
and it ran on past anything either one of them
could have blamed on the roses.

Up the hill the carillon let eleven o'clock go,
unhurried, one note at a time coming down over the roofs,
and the porch got its manners back.

Her phone went again, twice, then a third time, committed.
She squared the coffee can on the rail instead of answering,
turned it so the best of the orange faced the street.
"Those stay. The step's got its mum.
The rail gets better light.
She can keep an eye on them out here."

"They'll want water."

"Verna's expecting me at two," she said.
"For the notebook. I'm told the good stories run you a pie."

"Then it's a good thing the House keeps a man,"
she said, to a point just past his ear,
and went down the steps and up Millrow toward the square,
and her phone was out before the fence corner,
and she made even that look unhurried.

Cal checked the run of ties, cane by cane,
tight enough to hold, loose enough to grow.
Then he took the coffee can around the east corner to the spigot,
topped the water up till it nearly ran over,
which cut zinnias did not strictly require, and got anyway,
and set it back where she'd squared it,
best of the orange to the street.

Cut flowers want water. That was the whole of it,
and he told himself so at the spigot,
and once more on the walk back around.

The roses held every bud shut, saving it all up for October.
The zinnias sat lit in the eleven o'clock sun,
already acting like they'd grown there.
He picked his gloves up off the rail,
and what was left of the twine went into his back pocket
for next fall, the same pocket as always.
Ten minutes, once a year, him and her roses.
He'd been on that porch the better part of an hour,
and he didn't count one minute of it against the ten.

Water was drying on the boards
where he'd brought the can around from the spigot.
The deadwood lay in the truck bed.
