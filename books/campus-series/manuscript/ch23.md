Cal Sutter walked her home from Hartwell House
at a pace that would have embarrassed a funeral.

Behind them the band was packing up on the porch
and the lawn was emptying two by two into the dark,
and Earlene Tatum stood at the top of the steps
waving them off with her whole arm,
the send-off of a woman who intended to be quoted later.

Marisol carried her shoes in one hand.
His one good jacket rode her shoulders, warm from him,
and Millrow went by porch by porch,
every light on the street burning,
every window occupied by somebody working very hard
at not watching them go.
She had already given him the smile, back on the porch,
in front of the entire dressed-up town,
and she did not regret one watt of it.

"Everybody saw that," she said. "You understand. Everybody."

"That was the general idea."

"Earlene is drafting the announcement as we speak.
There will be a commemorative plate."

"Long as I'm on it," Cal said.

"You've been quiet for three porches," she said, later.

"Thinking."

"Take your time. It's a short street."

It was a short street.
She had walked it since she chose this town
and it had never once taken this long,
and she would have slowed it down further if she'd known how.

He took his time.
Twice he pulled in a breath with a shape to it,
some sentence assembling somewhere behind his teeth,
and twice he let it back out unused.
The cross street came up ahead of them,
the end of Millrow plainly in sight,
and his nerve gave out about four boards before the street did.

"Hartwell porch," he said instead. "You notice the floor?"

"I noticed the dancing."

"Quarter-sawn oak. Nobody mills it anymore.
You cut the log across the grain,
takes twice the wood and twice the labor,
and the board runs true for a hundred years.
Won't cup. Won't twist.
Whole town stood on that porch tonight
and it never said a thing."

"You're serious," she said. "Quarter-sawn."

"Dead serious. You could dance on that porch
every homecoming for a hundred years and it'd hold you."

Marisol stopped walking, right at the ragged end of Millrow,
and looked up at him.

"Cal Sutter," she said,
"I have never heard anything more romantic in my whole life."

He looked over to see if she was laughing at him.
She was not.
That was the terrible part.
He had said quarter-sawn oak to a divorced woman in the dark,
and it had worked all the way down,
and now she was going to have to live with that.

"I know some things about heart pine too," he said.

"Don't spend it all in one night."

They turned up her street,
which was darker and slower, live oaks closing overhead,
the music from the square thinning away behind them
until it was down to their footsteps
and the tail end of a song
somebody two blocks over refused to let die.
Somebody's after-party had shed plastic cups down a fence line,
and she collected them as they walked, six, seven, eight,
and Cal lifted the lid off a curbside can without being asked,
and neither of them said anything about that either.

Her gate sat where it always sat, under the live oak,
in a dark the streetlight didn't reach.
The latch had a trick to it, lift and then thumb,
and she did the trick without looking,
and then didn't push the gate open.

He took his cap off.
Not to fuss with it. Off.
Both hands on the brim, held against his chest
like a man in a movie made before either of them was born,
bareheaded under her oak, shirtsleeves pale in the dark.
His hair was pressed flat where the cap had been.
She looked at that longer than it deserved.
She was allowed to now.
She had seen him a hundred ways by now,
up ladders, under trucks, across a crowded porch,
but never yet holding his own cap like an offering,
and she stood there and let herself look.

"Ma'am," he said.

"Oh, we're doing ma'am."

"Seemed like the occasion."

She was laughing before his forehead ever touched hers,
and so was he, a low sound she felt more than heard,
and the kiss was slow and sure, with nothing left to prove.
They had done this before,
in a lit shop at midnight, weeks ago,
and had spent every day since pretending otherwise.
This was the same kiss with the lights on it.

Then nobody moved.
He stood holding his cap. She stood holding her shoes.
It went on long enough that a truck came up the street
behind them,
slowed — somebody getting their look — and rolled on,
and even then they let the quiet sit a while longer.

"You'll be wanting your jacket back," she said, against his jaw.

"Not especially," Cal said.

The gate got opened, eventually.
The yard took a while too.
At her door the key went in on the second try,
and she would have blamed the porch light, if anybody had asked.
The door shut behind them.
His one good jacket slipped from her shoulders
and landed on the hall floor.

---

Sunday came up bright and stayed that way.

In her kitchen she took down two mugs,
which was not the usual number.
On the wall by the pantry the kitchen calendar
hung the same as always, the one date in June circled.
She carried the mugs down the hall.

By noon the Checkerboard was doing its after-homecoming business,
the griddle roaring, every stool taken,
the tower ringing the hour out over the square,
and the Liars' Table in full session at the front window:
Tick Moran, Tunk Ferrell, Peanut Kyzer,
and Earlene Tatum, its only seated woman,
sitting wherever she pleased.
Verna Poteat had annexed the counter for the church rush,
the way she had every Sunday for years,
the Phams long past arguing,
and the price of pie was whatever Verna said it was.

They came in together, which was its own headline.
Conversation at the Liars' Table did not stop,
but all four heads swiveled at once
and tracked them to the back booth like a single instrument.

"Well," Earlene said.

Marisol slid into the back booth across from Cal,
phone already going off like a june bug in a jar.

"How bad," Cal said.

"Somebody got a photo. The last dance, shot from the lawn.
You're a little blurry. I look sensational."

"How many people in that group."

"Twelve thousand." She held up the phone.
"And they took my badge weeks ago,
so I am reading this to you as a private citizen."

He set down his coffee, put both forearms on the table,
and got still, hands quiet around the mug,
a man settling in to hear all of it.
It was, she had decided, one of the best things he did.
She was fully prepared to die in the booth
and be buried in it.

"These are terrible," she said. "I've read them nine times."

"Read them again."

She squared the phone in front of her.

"Leading theory. 'Y'all, that is the new strength coach.
I would know those shoulders anywhere.'
There are forty replies. Several are just fanning themselves."

"He's got the neck for it," said Tick Moran, from the window.

"Theory two. You are a country singer,
in town quiet ahead of a private show.
There's a faction. They are researching your discography."

"A faction," Cal said.

"A large one. Now, one woman says
you can't be the strength coach,
on account of the county's still got that warrant out on you.
She is being shouted down by people
who say that's just part of the singer's outlaw brand."

"He can't sing," said Peanut Kyzer, to the Table, with authority.

"You've never once heard him," said Tunk.

"I stand by it."

"Huh," Cal said.

"Somebody has asked when you're playing.
Somebody else has answered, and I want you to hear this,
'after the legal matter is resolved, I'd imagine.'
And one of them would like to know
if the shoulders are recent."

"Recent," said Cal.

"Her word. I'm only reporting what I read."

Cal turned his mug a slow half-turn on the tabletop.

"Keep going," he said. "I want the whole picture."

Her phone buzzed on top of everything, and she glanced down.
Kat. One line, warm, to the effect that margaritas
were now owed and details would be extracted.
Marisol turned the phone face down on the table. Later.
Then she turned it back over, because there was one more.

"And the bronze medal." She slid the phone across the booth,
screen up. "This one you have to read for yourself."

Cal picked it up, held it at arm's length, and read it twice.

"'The tall one from facilities,'" he read.
"'Asking for a friend.'"

Marisol leaned out of the booth
and pitched her voice to carry to the window.

"Anybody here know the tall one from facilities?
I'm asking for a friend."

The Liars' Table broke up.
Somebody's coffee got set down hard enough to slosh.
Earlene had both hands over her mouth
and was not using them to muffle a single thing.

And Cal Sutter's smile opened all the way.

Not the short one, not the flash she had been living on
since August.
The door swung wide onto the lit room and stayed open,
and he sat there in a diner booth on a Sunday
and let the entire Liars' Table look straight in.
Her breath went and came back.

"Ma'am," he said, not quietly.
"Would your friend like my phone number?"

The Liars' Table turned around, all of it, in a body,
which under Ashford law amounted to a press release.

"I had him for the singer," said Peanut Kyzer, to his eggs.

"You know what you've just done," she said.

"Yes, ma'am," Cal said. "Took me long enough to do it."

"Verna," Marisol called. "The buttermilk. A whole one.
One fork, and the fork's not mine."

The pie came the length of the counter on her flat palm,
whole, in its tin,
and the Liars' Table gave it the respect
of watching it travel the whole distance.
Verna named her price and Marisol paid it in cash,
bills laid flat on the table.

"About time," Verna said.

"Verna, I paid the minute the pie existed."

"Wasn't talking about the pie," said Verna,
and went back to the counter.

"For the record," Marisol announced,
to the booth, the counter, and the full senate at the window,
"some weeks back, on Delmar's porch, I lost a bet to this man.
Terms, one buttermilk pie. Witnesses available."

"Available," Earlene said. "Eager."

She turned the tin to face him
and set the fork on his napkin, handle toward him.

"Paid in full," she said. "I wanted witnesses. You'll do."

"What was the bet?" asked Tunk Ferrell.

"That," said Marisol, "stays on Delmar's porch."

"Witnessed," said Tunk,
and Tick said it after him, solemn as church.

She'd held on to that debt longer than she'd needed to.
For a while it had been the one sure thing between them,
the appointment he'd have to keep, come whatever came.
She watched him take the first forkful off the whole pie
and didn't need it anymore.

"Verdict," she said.

"It kept," Cal said.

"High praise."

"From me it is."

"The whole senate is watching you eat that."

"Let 'em."

Earlene appeared beside the booth with the coffee pot
she had no authority to be carrying,
topped off both cups, and looked Marisol over.

"Look at her," Earlene said, to the room at large. "Glowing."

"It's the humidity," Marisol said.

When the fork went down for the last time, Cal stood,
left something by his cup for Verna anyway,
and put his hand at the small of Marisol's back,
and walked her out the whole length of the Checkerboard,
past Tick and Tunk and Peanut, none of them even blinking,
past Verna at the register,
past Earlene, past every last grinning face at the counter,
and not one soul in the place pretended, even slightly,
to be looking at anything else.

They stopped on the sidewalk in the flat noon light.
He put his cap back on with one hand.
His other hand stayed at her back.
