The shop opened Wednesday on cold concrete and WD-40,
bay doors up into a blue morning with an edge on it,
the radio low on the parts shelf,
and by the time Cal had walked the bays with his coffee
the transcript was up in the break room.

Somebody had typed it.
That was the part he kept coming back around to.
Somebody in this county had sat down after Thursday night
with the broadcast and typed the whole exchange out fair,
her question, the room going still underneath it,
and where his answer belonged the typist had put [pause],
brackets and all, centered on its own line,
and now the sheet hung by the time clock,
still smelling faintly of toner,
between the safety poster and Boyd's pledge letter,
which still said pending,
and had said pending long enough
that the crew had quit reading it out loud for luck.

Nobody asked him about the sheet.
The crew had a grammar for a thing like that.
They read it, they looked at the stove instead of at him,
somebody ran the grinder two minutes earlier than usual,
and the matter was considered heard.

He read the sheet more times that week
than the coffee strictly required,
which was its own kind of phone call,
and didn't count against the terms, and didn't help.

He left it where it hung.
Taking it down was an answer too,
and answers, just now, were the one thing he wasn't giving out.
The real answer lay face down in the locked drawer,
the key in his shirt pocket next to the House key,
where it had ridden since the Monday the mail brought it.
The pause had never been a man deciding.
The pause was a man keeping.

The winter salt came at eight, a month ahead of itself,
sixty bags and a driver new to the route
who started rigging a two-bag dolly load at the tailgate.
Cal took a bag on each shoulder instead
and walked them back to the salt rack, breathing even,
four trips, while the driver quietly retired the dolly,
and his second signed the slip watching him go by
and added the morning to whatever running total she kept
on what the job had not yet managed to wear off of him.

Ronnie came off bay three at the ten o'clock lull
and stood at the corkboard awhile, wiping his hands,
reading the transcript like a box score.

"You've got a beer at the Mule," he said. "Going flat."

"Says who?"

"Peanut. Poured it last night and set it down on the house,
far end of the bar. Told the room it'll keep.
I have watched that man charge a widow for ice.
Peanut says it counts against the nothing you missed.
Whole town went. You'd have hated it fine."

Cal kept reading the sheet.

The Blind Mule did not pour beer on the house on speculation.
In a bar that voted with glassware,
a full one standing untouched at the rail all night
was Peanut Kyzer giving his whole speech,
and half the county had been there to hear it.
So there were two verdicts out now.
The town at large had convicted him by screenshot
in the first dark of October,
and the Mule had acquitted him by draft pour in October,
and both juries had ruled without the one fact that mattered,
which was gray, and said UNSIGNED, corner to corner,
and lay in a locked drawer thirty feet from the corkboard.

"You going down for it?" Ronnie said.

"Beer'll keep. Peanut said so."

Ronnie nodded and went back to bay three,
and the impact wrench started back up.

---

Boyd came up the hill himself after lunch.

No folder, no company, the good coat left in the car,
a man arriving at volume one and staying there.
He stopped in the gravel short of the first bay,
out in the open where anybody who cared to look
could see how pleasant it all was,
and he looked at the yard fence a moment with real approval,
which the fence had earned.

"I won't take ten minutes," Boyd said.
"Friday got past us. I've had them hold the packet.
The same pages will reach you fresh this week,
and the new line is the Friday before the trustees come up.
Thoroughness reads well in November. Signed, it reads better."

He gave that a little air, easy in his shoulders.

"Fine supper Thursday," he said. "Fine question, too.
A man who says nothing keeps every option he's got.
I've done business with worse negotiators than silence."

Cal said nothing,
and let the nothing run a good while past the place
where visiting men usually get relief from it.
Boyd stood in it without a flicker, gracious to the last,
touched his hat brim into the quiet,
and drove back down the hill at a neighborly speed.

"My brother-in-law's got that same coat," Ronnie said,
to the taillights.

Nobody took it up.
The grinder came back on, and the shop went back to work.

In the office the feed-store calendar hung where it always hung,
and Cal stood in front of it and counted.
The trustees came up the first Tuesday of November.
The paper Friday had died unsigned, quietly, offstage,
and Boyd, reading something in the pause worth waiting on,
had reset the line to the Friday before the trustees came.
Between Cal's boots and that Friday stood three more Fridays,
counting the one at the end of this week.
Boyd had buried the dead deadline standing up, with honors,
and the same two words were coming back in the mail
with a longer fuse under them.

He looked at the calendar until the looking was done,
and then went and found work for his hands,
and the afternoon gave him plenty.

---

At knocking-off time Thursday an engineer walked up
to the yard fence with an empty water jug,
clean boots a week past clean, a surveyor's squint,
a man waiting on a truck or on paperwork
in the one spot on campus where waiting felt like work,
and Cal ran him a hose from the shop spigot
and stood with him at the fence while it filled.

"Question," Cal said.
"Suppose a certifying official gets handed a finding
he doesn't hold with.
Can he answer it with a finding of his own?"

The engineer watched his jug fill.

"One of equal standing," he said.
"Real drawings, real numbers, work a stamp will stand behind.
Short of that it's a letter to the editor."

He capped the jug and gave the question's address no glance,
which was its own kind of manners.

"And a ladder," he allowed.
"Most bad findings got written by men who wouldn't climb."
He swung the jug over the fence rail and looked at Cal once.
"You strike me as a man with a ladder."

The laugh got out of Cal, one syllable, over the fence rail,
and startled a mockingbird off the wire.

"Obliged," Cal said.

The engineer raised two fingers off the jug for a goodbye
and walked back down to his truck.
Cal never did learn whose man he was,
and that was all the conversation there was,
and it was enough to build on.

That evening he stayed late in the office with a legal pad
and put down what equal standing weighed.
Measured drawings, every floor, every elevation.
Load paths a stamped man would initial.
Footing pits, dug and photographed.
Numbers, real ones, run against the code the House was built under
and the code it was being hanged by, side by side on one page.
The list ran to two pages before it quit,
and what it added up to was a season of honest work,
and out the window October was going along dry and blue,
in no hurry on anybody's behalf,
and he had three Fridays.

---

What he wanted, the whole week, was to call her.

The news itself traveled fine.
Boyd's new Friday went to her phone the same afternoon
in the plain flat letters the phone gave everybody,
and came back answered inside the hour,
because silent, with her, was a bill he'd paid once at that bench
and did not mean to owe twice.
But letters on a screen were not her voice,
and four times between Wednesday and Friday
he got exactly as far as her name.

Thursday it was at the fence line, coffee going cold on the post,
her name up under his thumb,
and he stood there long enough for the screen to dim twice,
wanting the laugh that went low through the middle
with the break at the top,
wanting the chin coming up, the mouth taking its time
over whether he'd earned the sharp thing or the kind one,
wanting the dark hair down out of its day knot by that hour,
wherever her Thursday had her.
Then the handheld on his hip went off,
his second asking after a part number for the flatbed,
and the county was back,
and her name went dark in his pocket.

Friday ran its own errands first.
The shop did Friday work, brake lines and blade grinding,
Tick Moran sold a gooseneck trailer over the radio,
sight unseen, before lunch,
and the back road's pecans let go a few more yellow leaves
with every good gust.
The mail came at a quarter past ten,
the same pages in a fresh envelope, gray on every page,
with a one-paragraph memo naming the Friday before the trustees.
It went into the locked drawer on top of the first one,
and the key went back in his shirt pocket, next to the House key,
and the drawer now held more paper aimed at that house
than the shop had held in twenty years.

The crew knocked off at five thirty,
and he stayed, working the legal pad under the office lamp
until the pencil quit making new lines.
Up the hill the carillon let go of ten o'clock,
and the notes came down the back road through the open bay
the same as they came every night.
On the end of the bench the two wrenches lay on the clean rag,
right size and wrong one, a matched set going nowhere,
exactly where her hands had left them,
and he looked at them awhile and let them be.

He cut the office lamp, then the bays, east to west,
and stood in the last dark doorway with his keys in his fist.

Out on the apron the yard light made its small gold room,
and Ratchet lay curled in the middle of it on her stencil,
nose to tail on her own painted name, dead asleep,
the best-guarded parking spot in the county.

He got the phone out.
Her name came up the same as it had all week,
plain letters, still not sufficient,
and this time his thumb didn't stop there.
He stood in the dark of the bay door and took one picture,
the sleeping dog in the gold light, her name under her,
and sent it.

No words under it.
There weren't any that kept to the terms.

The answer came back in nine seconds flat.

*Best-looking thing on that whole campus,* it said.
*Tell her I said so.*

Cal stood a long time in his dark shop.
The screen lit the near end of the bench,
the clean rag and the two wrenches lying on it,
and out on the apron the dog slept on her name.
