The phone started at ten, and Cal let it ring.

His second first, then two of the crew,
then numbers the phone didn't know,
and in between the ringing the screenshot came through
from four different senders, all of them kind, all of them horrified,
his own letterhead with his own name under it,
gone gray from being a picture of a picture,
riding a verdict he had never reached.
He read it twice, the way he read everything.
Then he set the phone face-down on the bench
and quit being reachable.

He was still there near midnight, where the whole day had been
pointed, alone in the shop with one bay lamp burning,
the bay door up in spite of the cold,
the canvas jacket on for the same reason,
and the drawer unlocked and open under his hand,
holding what it had held since Monday.

Down the hill the Blind Mule was letting out,
doors and engines and one last holler with no anger in it,
and under that, on the cold air through the open bay,
Delmar's banked pit kept its thin smoke minding itself over Millrow.
Everybody down there was going to bed
believing they knew his name.

Ratchet heard her before the gravel did.

One ear came up out of the sleeping dog,
and then the gravel started, far too fast for the back road,
somebody driving it like it owed her money.
Headlights swung the whole length of the bay and cut.
One car door, shut hard enough to carry.
Then Marisol stood in the doorway of his shop at midnight
with her keys still in her fist.

She'd been crying at some point and had driven straight through it;
what was left on her face in the bay light
was scrubbed down to the fight and nothing else.

"Twelve thousand phones," she said.
"Your office. Your letterhead. Your name.
I spent my evening up a ladder stringing lights
on the lawn your survey is supposed to bury,
and I found out with the rest of the county.
So I drove out here to hear it from the man himself."

Ratchet got up, crossed the concrete,
and put her head down on Marisol's boot, and stayed.

"Don't," Marisol told the dog. "I'm furious."

Ratchet stayed.
The shop's first vote was in, and it was not close.

Cal didn't offer her a chair or a defense.
He lifted the pages out of the open drawer,
set them on the bench under the lamp,
and stepped back out of her way.

"Top of every page," he said.

She came to the bench still armed.
She looked down for the length of a breath, and then longer,
and he watched her mouth take the word in
and refuse, at first, to trust it.
DRAFT — UNSIGNED, corner to corner, every page,
gray as a watermark and plainer than one.
She turned the pages as if one of them might quit saying it.
None of them quit saying it.

"You never signed."

"No."

"You never signed it."
The breath went out of her once, hard,
and relief rode out with it,
and what came back in was the other thing.
"How long?"

"Since Monday."

"Monday." She set the pages down.
"I had one rule, Cal. One.
The House is the fight, and the man holding it I leave alone.
I kept my half of that
in a county that convicted you by suppertime.
Tell me what you did with yours."

"I was going to bring it to you."

"When?"

"When I could walk in with the answer instead of the weight.
There's a list behind all this if Boyd's money doesn't land.
Twelve jobs out of this shop, and the pen that types it is mine.
I wanted the twelve solved first. Then you."

"That was not yours to decide."
She didn't get louder. She got quieter, which was worse.
"What I can carry.
You sat in this shop and rated my load limit
like I'm one of your trucks,
and then tonight came, and it turned out
the man deciding what I could handle
wasn't handling it either."

He stood and took that, and none of it missed.

She wasn't done.
"I lost my seat tonight being helpful.
I strung lights for a town that spent the same three hours
learning your name like a swear word.
And the worst minute wasn't either of those.
It was standing in the middle of the rig-up, lights half hung,
reading a screenshot about a man I —"
She traded the end of that sentence away.
"About a man I trusted.
You had a day and a phone, Cal. A day and a phone."

"I picked the phone up Monday night," he said. "Twice."

"And?"

"Put it down twice.
Once I said it out loud to you,
it was going to be true the whole way through,
and I wasn't ready to hand you a thing that heavy half-solved."
He looked at the drawer,
then made himself look at her instead.
"There hasn't been much in that drawer
I ever minded locking up alone.
I minded this. It scared me slow."

"And the rules it was graded on. When did you read those?"

He could have made it softer, and didn't. "Friday."

"Friday." She set the pages down flat
and took her hand off them.

"Slow I'd have forgiven by Thursday," she said.
"Silent, with me, you don't get to be again.
That's the price. Decide right now if you're paying it."

"Paid," Cal said.

She glared at him a moment longer and lost ground to it.
"And quit standing there looking like that
while I decide to forgive you," she said.
"It speeds things up.
I had planned on this taking all night."

"Take all night," he said. "Shop's open."

"Then let's have the whole accounting.
Everything in this building you've been carrying alone.
Tonight, on that bench, all of it."

"Some of it's in the truck."

"Then it's a good thing the truck's not far."

He went out through the bay into the cold for the milk crate.
The Mule's last engines were fading off down the hill,
and he stood a moment in the dark with the folder in his hands,
about to be a man with no secrets
in front of the one person it mattered to.
Then he carried it in.

He laid the folder on the bench beside the draft
and dealt it out in order.
First the ground-rules pages. He put them in her hands.

"They mailed the graders the answers
before they ever mailed the men," he said.
"Expectation sheets.
Every structure on the Millrow list
gets graded against this year's code.
Not the code it was built under. This year's,
and no credit anywhere for standing."

She read like a woman billing somebody for the minutes.
"Rail height." She flipped. "Egress. Cal.
The opera house fails this page.
The churches fail this page."

"Delmar's fails that page, if a man's honest about the wiring.
It was never a survey.
It's a finding with the date left blank,
and the blank they left for the date
sits right over the line they left for me."

Then he took out the photographs,
printed off the office printer, dates plain in every frame,
eleven pieces of microscope glass
riding a crack down an old plaster wall.

"East wall of the House," he said. "The famous crack.
Glass glued across it, every foot or so, year after year.
Glass is the honest material.
It won't stretch and it won't lie.
If that wall had moved the width of a pencil line,
one of these would show it.
Nineteen years, top of the wall to the floor. Not one hairline.
The House quit moving before anybody now shouting about it
ever read a survey."

She looked closer, at the small square writing in a corner,
and Cal watched her find the question before she asked it.

"Whose dates?"

"My wife's," he said. "She kept the watch on that wall.
The dates stop where she did."

Marisol didn't say she was sorry, which he was grateful for.
She reached out and straightened one photograph
so its date sat square with the others, and that was all,
and it was enough.

Up the hill the carillon struck midnight,
and the notes came down the back road the same as every night,
through the open bay, across the bench, into the quiet.

"So the House is fine," she said finally. "Provably."

"The House is honest. The paper's rigged.
And the only signature that can launder
the one into the other is mine."

"Which you haven't given."

"Which I'm not going to give."

She looked up from the photographs.
"Say that again. Slower."

"I'm not signing it.
I knew that Monday, in the truck, with the dome light on.
Everything since has been me figuring what refusing costs,
and who it costs it to,
and how to pay it without help.
That last part is the part I'm through with."

"Good," she said. "Because you're out of the without-help
business as of about eleven forty tonight."

"Then there's one more thing I've been sitting on," he said,
"and it's the oldest one."

He crossed to the parts wall
and took down the wrench that had hung there since August,
right size, right jaw,
four feet from her little crescent
where it lay squared on a clean rag at the end of the bench,
the one rag in a working shop nobody keeps clean by accident.

She looked from the wrench in his hand to the rag on the bench.

"You kept mine on a rag."

"Best rag I've got."

The laugh got out of her sideways, wet at the edges,
and it was the first good sound in that shop all night.

He held the wrench out across his open palm.

"I said I'd bring you the right one."

She looked at the steel, and then at him,
and put her hand out, palm up, the same as the first day.
He set it in her hand,
and her fingers closed around the wrench,
and his hand closed over hers,
palm to palm with the steel warm between them,
and neither hand was in any hurry about the transaction.

"Right size. Right jaw," he said.
"I've been sure since that first morning,
before I ever walked off with the wrong one."

"You've known the size since August."

"Knowing the size was never the holdup.
Handing it over meant the errand was done."

She looked down at their hands over the steel.

"Six weeks," she said.
"You kept a hostage for six weeks
so you'd owe me a visit."

"I was slow about that too. It's a pattern. You've met it."

She laughed, short and real, surprising them both,
and it took the last of the crying with it.
Then she set the wrench down on the clean rag,
next to the wrong one, a matched set at last,
and squared her shoulders into the version of her
that ran twelve thousand people on four hours' sleep.

"All right. Here's tonight, then," she said.
"You stay inside. You're the only person in the county who can.
Don't sign, don't resign, don't explain.
Make them grade that wall in the open, on dated glass,
against the code it was actually built under,
and make them do every bit of it slow."

"And you?"

"Outside. A seat is not the town.
I know every porch those lights were strung for,
and I know which kitchens get listened to.
By Thursday I'll know it better than whoever took my badge."

"They'll come at you again."

"They spent their best shot tonight,
and I'm still standing inside the only lit door on campus.
Let them wonder about that."

"Terms," Cal said.

"From tomorrow, out there, you don't say my name
and I don't say yours.
Not at Delmar's, not in the group, not on anybody's porch.
The county thinks you signed a death warrant.
For now the county gets to keep thinking it,
because two people in one shop
know what the top of every page says,
and that's two more than Boyd is counting on."

"That's a lonely way to run a fight," he said.

"It would be."
She came around the corner of the bench.
"If it ended at the property line."

She stopped a hand's width off from him and looked up,
and the look went long, past what even midnight excused,
and this time there was no horn, no headlights, no crowd,
nothing left awake in the county to interrupt it,
and neither of them spent a word on it.

Over by the truck tire, Ratchet's tail
went twice against the concrete.

"Second vote," Marisol said. "Your shop is rigged."

"Motion carries," Cal said, and kissed her.

Or she kissed him.
Nobody ever settled it, and nobody tried.
The bay smelled of diesel and WD-40 and cold October,
and her mouth was warm out of all of that.
She tasted like relief and like trouble,
and he quit pretending he had ever wanted one without the other.
Her hands came up somewhere in the middle of it
and took two fistfuls of canvas jacket,
which he understood, at last, from the inside.

She leaned back in his arms far enough to be looked at,
her hair down out of the day's knot for good and past saving,
the lamp catching all the loose dark of it.

"Well," she said. "The treaty has a flaw."

"Name it."

"I'm not allowed to say your name in public,
and I have just now become extremely fond of it."

Cal reached past her and ran the bay door down its track
one-handed, the chain feeding through his fist,
and the cold quit, and the town noise quit,
and the shop was one lamp, one dog, and the two of them.

"That," she said, watching the door come down
like it weighed nothing,
"is more of the exact thing I was complaining about."

She stepped back into him before the door had finished settling,
surer this time,
and this kiss had no relief left in it anywhere.
It was all the other thing.
His hands left the bench for good.
Hers found the snaps of the canvas jacket
and had them, one pull, all of them,
and she pushed the jacket back off his shoulders,
and he let it go,
and it went down onto the swept concrete,
the first thing either of them had let fall all night.
