By seven they had the whole of it out on the hall table.

The counter-report in its gray folder,
the engineer's stamp square on the back page.
The maintenance ledger, open in the middle
of Cal's years of entries.
The pledge folder, grown past the reach of its rubber band,
every envelope logged in her own hand,
because she was the bookkeeper and the bookkeeper checks.
Tomorrow the folder went to the trust,
whoever the trust sent. That much was arranged.
The windows had gone black-mirror before six,
and the porch light burned a small hole in the nearest one,
and the furnace ticked under the floor,
and the hall smelled of coffee and old varnish
and the wax paper around two sandwiches nobody had opened.

Cal had let them in at the side door with his key,
and the night had come in with them
and taken ten minutes to leave.
She'd kept her coat across her shoulders for the first hour
and then forgotten it on a chair,
because the House warmed from the middle out
and they were standing in the middle.

At half past eight he poured her coffee without asking,
the white mug off the shop shelf,
and set it at her right hand where he'd learned she'd find it.
Steam climbed off it in one thin line.
She watched his hands around the pot
a little longer than the pouring took,
and the hall, for one more minute,
was every good Saturday since August, all in one room.

"Tell me the porch folder holds," he said.

"Every pledge on it counted twice.
More when Saturday's checks clear."

"They'll clear."

"I know they'll clear. I logged them."

"Wanted to hear it in your voice."

She had logged all of it.
That was the part nobody down the hill would ever see,
the hundred small honest evenings under this one lamp,
his pencil on the drawings, her pen in the folder,
the two of them building a case
out of nothing but true things.
She wanted one more hour of it.
She checked the clock on the wall and didn't get one.

He walked the drawings one more time,
squared the folder to the ledger,
squared the ledger to the table edge.
Years of that hall stood behind his shoulders
in the lamplight, and she let herself look.
Neither of them said the word tomorrow.
It was already in the room anyway.

Up the hill the tower tolled nine,
each stroke carrying hard and clean on the still air.
On the last of them, Cal's phone lit against the drawings.

He read it standing. He read it twice.
Then he read it out loud, level,
the voice he used for lumber grades.

"'So you hear it from me and not the room.
Tomorrow the board is told it plain.
The full footprint, or the gift is withdrawn
and the shortfall stands.
Half a gift is no gift. Sleep well.'"

Her hand had gone flat on the ledger.
She noticed that a moment after it happened.
She took the pen out of her own fingers and set it down.
Two and a half years of building a life on purpose,
and it came down to a message a rich man
had thumbed out between courses.

"He can't buy the vote," she said. "So he's pricing it."

"He priced it months ago," Cal said.
"This is him showing us the tag."

"Say the rest out loud," she said.
"It's worse when it stays quiet."

He stayed where he was,
the table's whole length between them, and said it.

"By noon tomorrow half the county's phones
have the same story.
The survey's a fraud,
and the proof of the fraud reads like your campaign.
Girlfriend clears the road for her man."

"And the drive's best day was the day the lie broke,"
she said. "Outrage money.
They'll say we banked a lie's interest."

"They'll say I sat on my signature
till the price came up.
And that you're what came up with it."

"They'll say the joy is the tell."
She looked at the folder, the ledger, the drawings,
the whole true spread of it.
"Everything about us that's true is the caption.
The porch. The Saturdays. Us."
Nowhere in that story was she Marisol Pruett,
bookkeeper, who had checked every figure twice.
In that story she was the girlfriend, every telling.
She had spent two and a half years
earning her own name back one Saturday at a time,
and tomorrow a roomful of strangers
would spend it for her in an afternoon.

"Cal." She waited till he looked at her straight.
"There's no version of tonight where I wonder about you.
Not one minute of one version."

"I know it," he said. "Same."

And that was true, and it fixed nothing.
The story didn't need either of them to be false.
It needed them true, in love, and in public,
and they were all three.

He straightened the drawings he had already straightened.

"Here's what I've got," he said.
"I don't walk up that hill tomorrow.
The report goes in under the engineer's stamp
and the parents' letters,
and my name stays off the folder.
Nobody says the fix was bought
if the man they say sold it never shows."

For a moment she couldn't answer him.
He stood there in the lamplight with his hands still,
waiting to hear what that was worth.
She had watched those hands fix half of what this town owned.
She had never once watched them let go of anything.

"Say what it costs you," she said,
because one of them had to keep the books.

"It costs the crew watching me sit down
the one morning it counts," he said.
"It costs the plan getting argued by men
who never oiled a hinge in this hall.
It costs me standing in the shop at nine tomorrow
listening to that tower." He didn't look away.
"I counted it before I said it."

She got up. She walked the length of the photo wall,
past crews older than either of them,
and turned around with her chin level.

"My turn," she said.
"And you don't answer till I'm done."

He put his hands in his pockets. That meant yes.

"You know what a house costs to keep.
You don't know what mine cost to leave.
I had a house that went loud with quiet.
Two states back. Half mine still,
and there's a June date circled on my kitchen calendar."
She kept moving, slow, the hall's length and back,
because it said easier in motion.
"You could hear the refrigerator think in that house.
I drove a whole year with the windows sealed
and the radio off, and called it peace.
I stopped singing in the car, Cal.
I didn't even notice for months.
I bought a toolbox my first week alone,
piece by piece, out of principle and spite,
and learned every tool in it
so I'd never have to ask anybody
for anything smaller than a roof."
She stopped at the end of the table.
"And then you confiscated the best piece of it."

"It was the wrong wrench," Cal said.

She laughed,
and for a second the hall sounded like August.

"When I moved my life here I swore one thing," she said,
and the second was over.
"Saturdays were mine.
Not the lawyers'. Not the boxes'. Mine.
I built everything back around that one vow, and it held.
Then you walked off with my wrench and it held better.
So hear the rest,
because I'm the one person in this county
qualified to say it.
I know what losing a built life costs.
Full price. No terms. I've paid it once.
I will not be the quiet your life goes loud with, Cal.
I have lived in that sound."

"I know the sound too," he said, and that was all,
and she let it be all.

"If this House goes and my name is the caption under it,
there's no Saturday after that I know how to live in.
So here's mine.
I stay off the hill tomorrow.
I don't walk into the room I built the case for.
The letters go in without me,
and nobody says you were bought,
because the price never shows."

The hall was quiet enough to hear the furnace quit
and start again.

"Say what it costs you," he said.

"It costs me the room," she said, chin still level.
"It costs me my own kitchen at nine tomorrow,
watching a clock I can't hurry.
It costs me standing next to you in public
on the worst day you will ever need me to.
It costs me the Saturday I swore was mine,
because if that room goes wrong,
the Saturdays go with it.
Twelve thousand parents, and I'd be the one at home."
She laid both palms flat on the table edge.
"I counted it too."

She had counted it the whole walk of the photo wall.
It was the worst sum she had ever run,
and it balanced, and she hated that it balanced.
Two people who loved each other could stand in one warm hall
and offer up the best thing either of them had,
and every figure could come out right,
and it could still be wrong all the way down.

The two offers stood between the ledger and the drawings,
both real, both priced,
and neither of them reached for either one.

His hand crossed the table toward hers
and stopped on the gray folder, an inch short,
and stayed there.
She looked at the inch and let it be.
It was the longest inch in the county tonight,
and they had built it themselves, board by board,
out of everything they refused to hand the story.

"Nothing's settled," she said.

"No."

"Then nothing gets decided tonight.
Not by two tired people in the dark."

She took her coat off the chair and put it on properly,
buttons and all, and he watched her do it
with the folder still under his hand.
He lifted the pot off the warmer
and topped up the mug she hadn't touched,
out of habit, half looking at it,
and she picked it up because his hands had poured it.

"Whatever that room does tomorrow," he said,
"it doesn't get this table."

"It doesn't get this table," she said.

---

The porch air went through her coat before the door shut.
She set the mug on the rail to do up her collar,
and then her hands stayed in her pockets,
and the mug stayed on the rail.
The sandwiches were still on the sideboard behind her,
wax paper and all,
and neither of them had said a word about supper
all night, because neither of them could have eaten it.

Down the hill the town was lit, lamp by lamp.
Delmar's pit was going already for tomorrow,
the smoke standing straight up in the still air,
tall enough to navigate by,
and across the square the Checkerboard's sign
buzzed its stubborn OPEN into the dark.
Somebody was tending that pit at this hour.
Somebody was keeping that sign on.
Millrow lay below it all, every window out,
waiting on a vote it hadn't asked for.

She didn't cry.
She decided that on the top step,
on purpose, in her own favor,
and she went down the walk alone,
past the reach of the porch light.
Behind her the House went dark by stages,
the lamp over the drawings, the hall,
one window after another,
until only the porch light was left.

On the rail, the mug he'd poured her sat full
to an inch below the brim,
the handle turned toward the empty end of the porch.
The last of its warmth climbed thin into the porch light,
less every minute.
The coffee stilled.
The white mug went cold on the rail.
