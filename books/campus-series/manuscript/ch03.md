---

On the first away Saturday of the season, Ashford woke up its own size
and moved around in it like a woman trying on last year's dress,
quietly pleased that everything still fit.

The fairgrounds were a mowed field again, innocent as a church picnic.
The square had all its parking back and seemed embarrassed about it.
Somewhere down three hundred miles of interstate,
ninety thousand people were being loud in somebody else's town,
and Ashford, home alone for the weekend,
turned its radios up and got on with Saturday.

Marisol had a list.
It was penciled on the back of a window envelope,
and it said tomatoes, zinnias, birdseed, supper,
and her entire ambition for the day was to cross off all four
and call that a full and honorable Saturday.
Her son had texted at seven —
no words, just a photo of a duffel bag riding a bus seat by itself —
and she had replied with a number of exclamation points
she was not prepared to defend in court.
Her daughter, off at Bellamy, had tapped a little heart onto it
an hour later, long distance, half asleep, present.
Marisol had set the phone face down on the counter after that
and stood with both hands flat on the cool laminate for a second,
the house so quiet she could hear it being quiet.
Then she picked up her keys, which made a wonderful amount of noise,
and went.

At the light by the square she opened the glove box for her sunglasses
and spent the whole length of the red
looking at the clean empty spot where a certain crescent wrench
had lived since the divorce.
The sunglasses were sitting in it like a subletter.
She put them on, shut the glove box with tremendous dignity,
and drove on green.

---

The Doss County Curb Market ran Saturday mornings under a long open shed,
and on away weekends it was the town's whole downtown.
Boiled peanuts went around in a kettle you heard before you smelled.
Muscadines sweated through their paper sacks.
Mums stood in ranks, tomatoes lay out on newspaper
in order of self-esteem,
and down at the end the zinnia buckets carried on
in colors nobody had authorized.
On the peanut stand a radio played the away game at a polite murmur,
less a broadcast than a rumor,
and every few minutes a dozen heads leaned toward it at once
and then leaned back.

Marisol was at the zinnias, building a fistful one stem at a time,
when the market changed pressure around her.

She knew the hands before she placed the man.

Cal Sutter came across the shed carrying Verna Poteat's bushel basket,
and he carried it flat on one forearm, close to the body,
in a grip that made a bushel of anything look like a hatbox.
Verna went ahead of him at tugboat speed,
steering with two fingers over her shoulder,
and he ran exactly at the speed of Verna,
which no man that size had any business doing gracefully.
Light brown hair the sun was still working on, a little gray
at the temples, none of it looking accidental.
He took the basket straight through the boiled-peanut steam,
set it on her tailgate without a sound,
and squared it with one small push of the heel of his hand,
the kind of adjustment nobody asked for and nothing ever fell over after.

On his way back past the Dillards' table —
the Feed Store Dillards, whose folding table had wobbled
under every squash in the county since folding was invented —
he stopped for approximately four seconds.
One palm flat on the tabletop. One glance underneath.
He doubled a sheet of tomato newspaper twice,
slid it under the short leg with the toe of his boot,
pressed the table once to hear its new opinion,
and moved on before the older Dillard had finished saying his name.

A baby in a stroller by the mums reached out for the whole world
as he passed, and he tipped two fingers off his brow at it,
and the baby received this with the gravity of a returned salute.

Then he bought tomatoes.
He weighed them one at a time in his palm, unhurried, no squeezing,
kept four, laid his bills down flat and squared under the edge
of the scale, said something short that made the tomato man laugh,
and did all of it with the easy, unhurried authority
of a man who genuinely believed he was invisible.

He was not invisible.
Half the market had him on the radio at low volume,
the same as the game.
Two women by the muscadines had stopped negotiating entirely.
The tomato man's neighbor lost count of her change
and started the pile over.
And Marisol stood at the zinnia buckets with her stems dripping,
watching over the top rim of her sunglasses,
enjoying the program without one ounce of shame.
She was forty-three years old,
she knew exactly what she looked like standing there,
and she'd learned a long time ago that watching a capable man
cross a room was one of life's few reliable free amenities.
Apparently in Ashford it was municipal.

"Been like that for years," said Earlene, at her elbow, unasked.

"I didn't say anything."

"Mm," said Earlene, in the tone she used for humidity.
"Nobody ever does. Whole market says nothing, every Saturday."
She nodded at the zinnias. "Those are good this week."

"He still has my wrench."

"I know it," said Earlene, comfortably.
"Everybody knows it. It's been discussed at FAFSA night."
And she drifted on toward the peanuts,
leaving Marisol to decide whether she'd been teased,
informed, or both, which was the Earlene experience entire.

---

The picture came phone to phone, hand to hand,
which was how everything came in Ashford.

It started at the far end of the shed, by the mums.
Two heads bent over one screen, a palm cupped against the glare.
Then the phone itself went walking —
held out at arm's length to the next stall,
passed across a card table, angled, tilted, shaded,
gathering two and three faces at every stop.
Marisol watched it come up the shed toward her
like weather moving across a field,
and where it had already been, people stood differently.
Sacks got set down. Arms got crossed.

When it reached her it was in the hand of the younger Dillard,
who held it out flat on his palm like something he'd found in the road
and didn't want to be holding.

It was a rendering. An architect's lawn,
clipped and green and empty and very, very tidy,
with little digital people strolling on it in digital sunshine.
The lawn was where Hartwell House currently stood.
The caption under it said
*pending trustee approval at the November board meeting.*

Nobody knew who'd sent it first.
It had no beginning. It was simply everywhere at once,
and November landed on the whole market in one piece.
The shed went quiet enough to hear the peanut kettle rolling,
and the radio, still murmuring its faraway game,
was suddenly the loudest thing under the roof.

"That's the porch where I proposed," said the tomato man,
to no one, to everyone.

"Half this county got engaged on that porch," said the older Dillard.

"A lawn," said Verna Poteat, handing the phone back
with two fingers, at arm's length, like it had spit on her.
"I have a lawn. Nobody ever drove fifty miles
to get down on one knee in it."

The laugh that went around the shed was real,
and it did not fix anything, and everyone knew both things.

Marisol had never been inside Hartwell House.
She only knew it
from the road, the deep porch riding above Millrow
with its lights on at dusk like a ship at anchor.
You didn't have to have been inside, it turned out.
She looked at that tidy digital lawn until the phone moved on,
and caught herself holding her zinnias with both hands.

---

Between the feed store and supper she sat in the car
with the air conditioning going and the tomatoes sweating
in their newspaper on the passenger seat, and did her good deed.

The parents' group had swollen overnight —
game weekends did that.
She'd run this group for years before she ever had an Ashford
address, a team mom at long distance since her boy's freshman fall,
twelve thousand parking questions answered from two states over.
When the divorce shook everything loose, moving to the town where
her son and half her mornings already lived had simply made sense. Upstairs on the board,
the threads were going — *Parents' Weekend Hotels, Part Three*,
*drop/add ends Monday (vent here)* —
and downstairs in the queue sat four hundred and some
shiny new member requests, all wanting in at once.
She waved them through in batches, thumb, thumb, thumb,
approve and approve and welcome aboard,
twelve thousand and change, growing.
It took four minutes.
She felt brisk and useful about it,
dropped the phone in the cupholder,
and went to get supper.

---

By dusk the hickory smoke over Delmar's stood at maybe a third
of its home-game height, and Millrow was easing toward evening.
She drove it slower than she needed to,
and slower than that past the House,
where the porch stood empty
and held the last of the light anyway.
She didn't decide to look. She just looked.

Delmar's screen porch held maybe twenty people and one radio,
the game up loud now, the smell of the pit coming through
the screens in waves you could lean on.
Ray was working the register with a towel over his shoulder,
and beside the register sat a clipboard.

It was nothing. A clipboard, a fat block of ruled paper,
a pickle jar full of pens,
and one extra pen zip-tied to the clip itself,
tether cut short, so that signing with it
made everybody stoop a little, which somehow felt right.
The top page was full. So was the one under it.
Names in ballpoint, names in pencil,
one name in what appeared to be eyeliner.

"Guest book's by the register," a man on the porch called
to the couple coming in behind her, like directions to the restroom.
That was already its name, then.
The House's second guest book —
and Marisol, who had never once seen the first,
understood she was not required to have seen it
in order to sign the second.

Ray came around the counter while she waited on her order,
wiped both hands down the towel, one finger at a time,
picked the jar's last loose pen out of the pickle brine of pens,
and signed his own clipboard, unasked, in letters
you could have read across the road.
Then he squared the clipboard to the counter edge with his thumbs
and went back to the register without a word about it.

The jar was empty after Ray.
Which was, she realized, why somebody had zip-tied the spare.
Somebody in this town had stood at this counter this morning
and planned for the moment the jar ran dry.

Marisol stooped to the length of the tether
and signed her name with the full signature,
the one with the swagger in the M
that she'd been perfecting since ninth grade
and had never once wasted on a tax form.
Then she let the pen swing back against the clip
where the next hand would find it.

Her supper came over the counter warm against her forearm.
Out on Millrow the dusk had gone soft,
and behind her the porch went up all at once,
twenty people and a radio,
something going right three hundred miles away.

In the car she flattened the window envelope on her knee
and crossed off supper.
Tomatoes, zinnias, birdseed, supper. Four for four,
a full and honorable Saturday,
plus one line she hadn't put on the list,
written in somebody else's book, in her best hand,
tied by a short string to a town
that had a use for her name.
