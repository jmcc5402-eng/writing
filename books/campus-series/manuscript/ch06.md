# Chapter 6 — Table Nine

Cal heard what was wrong with the ballroom before he had his name tag
on.

The crowd was maybe a hundred and fifty people in their good clothes,
ice going into glasses, the band plugging in behind the little stage
of the rented ballroom at the event center.
Under all of it, the room's air handler carried a thin ring,
high and steady, a bearing about six weeks from quitting.
Nobody else would hear it all night.
He would hear little else.

The plan was three handshakes and gone by eight.
Boyd's, because not shaking it would be a sentence said out loud,
and two he owed from the summer.

Verna Poteat had the check-in table, reading glasses down her nose,
her list ruled in three colors of pen.

"Sutter," she said, finding him with one finger. "I've got you at
twelve."

"Twelve's fine."

"Card's at nine." She shrugged and waved him through.
"And tell people to quit asking me about rooms.
I'm booked three years deep for game weekends. I could rent the ice
machine."

He shook the two hands he owed inside the first forty feet,
and then he heard table nine before he found it.

Her laugh came up out of the middle of the room,
low in the middle with a break at the top,
and his feet had the table located
before the little number stands got involved.
He knew maybe five things about Marisol.
It bothered him that her laugh was one of them.

The dress was the dark green a bottle goes at the end of the evening,
and it had opinions.
It expressed one when she stood to shake a booster's hand,
and another when she sat back down,
and the room caught every word of it without anything getting said.

"Well," she said, when he pulled out the chair beside hers.
"Table nine."

"Card says so."

"Who am I to argue with stationery. Evening, Cal."

"Marisol."

"You clean up," Earlene Tatum said from across the table,
in the tone she used for barometric pressure.
Her earrings could have picked up AM radio.

Sonny Dillard had the seat on Cal's other side,
in the navy sport coat he wore to banquets and funerals both.
Down the table sat boosters Cal knew by handshake.
Birdie Pham came past twice before the salads, not to sit,
just turning a stem a quarter-inch in each jar,
taking the room's pulse through the flowers.

From nine, the House was out past the north wall,
dark on its hill, somewhere behind Cal's left shoulder.
He hadn't checked any compass.
He knew it the same as he knew where the door was.
He would have known it from any chair they gave him.

Tick Moran got up to welcome everybody,
and the PA put a whine under his first sentence, thin and climbing,
and half the room did the polite flinch and pretended otherwise.

Cal was up before he voted on it.
The powered speaker by the stage had its gain set against a wall
it was never going to beat.
He notched it down, walked the cabinet two inches off the plaster,
and the whine let go of the room like a hand opening.
He was back in his chair before the salads landed.

"Did you just fix the room?" Marisol said.

"Room was ringing."

"I didn't hear any ringing."

"You'll hear it now." He picked up his fork.
"It's quiet. Quiet's the sound."

She looked at him one second longer than the sentence needed.
"Handy," she said, making a whole paragraph of it.

"That ring's been in this room longer than most of the marriages
it's hosted," Earlene said. "I'd have paid money."

Tick found his radio voice once the PA behaved, ran the thank-yous,
and said "pending trustee approval at the November board meeting"
in the tone he used for station identification.
Delmar's was catering,
and the kitchen door kept a drumbeat under everything,
Ray hollering counts at his crew in a register built to carry across
smokers.

"I was at Hartwell House Sunday," Marisol said,
to her plate first, then to him.
"Stood in that front hall ten minutes longer than I meant to."

"Floor's off level in that hall."

"That's what I'm telling you. It leans at you.
A year I've been in this town,
and that's the first room that didn't feel rented.
Even the step."

He knew the step.
He did not say he knew the step.

"It leans because the sill's going," he said.
"That's not a welcome. That's water."

"You would look at a house like that and see water."

"Water's what's there.
I can name you five things wrong with that porch from the street,
and every one of them ends in water.
Water doesn't care how a room makes you feel. Water always wins."

"See, and I think a town keeps a building standing
because it can't say who it is without it.
You can't put a number on that."

"You can. It's got a comma in it. That's my whole point."

"That's not a point, Cal, that's a spreadsheet."

"Spreadsheets hold roofs up."

"Photographs hold towns up."
She had turned in her chair somewhere in there,
one elbow on the table, the whole dress facing him now.
"You've seen that photograph wall.
Everybody in those frames is somebody's whole life."

"And the gutter over them is done.
Somebody's whole life is fixing to get rained on."

They went at it through the mains,
and it took Cal a while to notice
they were describing two different buildings.
Hers was made of photographs and a front hall with manners.
His was made of sills and loads and thirty seasons of water.
Nobody was wrong. Nobody yielded an inch.
He hadn't enjoyed being this right and this wrong at one table
in longer than he cared to count.

He kept his voice at facilities the whole way
and enjoyed himself in a manner he had no plans to report.

Somewhere in there he also noticed the table ran smooth
and that she was the reason.
Earlene's tea refilled itself.
The rolls migrated south when Sonny eyed them.
When a booster wound up a story with no brakes in it,
she set one question in front of it and handed the wreck to Earlene,
who buried it kindly.
She ran that table for two hours and never once looked like she was
doing anything but sitting in a good dress laughing at the right
places.

He made a study of the centerpiece.
He read the front of the program twice.
He noticed her hands anyway — quick, certain, ringless — and the
laugh, twice more, and the dress every time she stood,
and he filed every bit of it under problems.

Tick said Rex Boyd's name and the applause came up on its own.

Boyd took the stage at the volume he took everywhere, which was one.
Cal had heard him work rooms before, and it was worth hearing,
the voice never lifting and never dropping,
arriving at each table like a man leaning on your fence.
It said gates. It said grandfathers.
It said home enough times to shingle it.

"Folks, we're calling it the Gateway," Boyd said,
warm as a sun-struck porch board.
"No drawings tonight. Drawings are paper, and paper's for lawyers.
Tonight, close your eyes and just listen.
A whole county saying the same word at the same time.
That's not a stadium, friends. That's a front door."

The room came up out of its chairs by tables.
The applause had a sound Cal knew off weather radio,
rain arriving on tin, scattered, then everywhere.

He stood with the rest of them and put his hands together
and carried what he carried.
The survey nobody at nine had heard of.
The signature that would be wanted under a finding
before the trustees sat down in November.
Twelve names on a list nobody had printed yet,
and the winter money keeping it unprinted.
Boyd had stood in Cal's own yard once, easy as this,
and called Hartwell House deferred maintenance with a porch,
in this exact warm.
None of it could be said at a table.
All of it sat under his chair.

He sat, and because his hands wanted work, he turned the program
over.
The back page was thank-yous in eight-point type,
the caterer, the florist, the table sponsors,
and near the bottom a line reading
"digital engagement: [TK: sock-vendor name] Media LLC,"
and under it, in friendly script, "ya'll come."
His eyes went over it and came away with nothing,
the same as every pair of eyes in the room.
Nobody read the back page of a program.
That was what back pages were for.

Between the speech and dessert he went and shook the third hand.

"Cal Sutter." Boyd's grip arrived with the donor wall behind it,
and the voice stayed at fence-post volume for one man,
same as for three hundred. "The man who keeps my lights on."

"University's lights."

"Give it till November," Boyd said, warm as ever,
already receiving the next hand.

That finished the plan.
Five past eight, and his truck sat forty feet from the door.
Cal stood at the edge of the room and listened to it.
Brushes on a snare as the band eased in.
The kitchen door keeping time.
Two laughs down at the bar, a man's and a woman's,
taking turns and then forgetting to take turns,
one more thing in the conversation every time it went to end.
And table nine, where her voice ran the talk with loose reins,
and his chair sat empty at an angle that said the man sitting there
meant to come back.

He went back.
A man finishes his coffee, he told himself.
He hadn't ordered coffee.

Dessert came through the kitchen door on its drumbeat,
and Marisol photographed hers before her fork moved.
He heard the phone do its little shutter click,
once for the plate, once for the flowers,
once for the program propped against the jar.
Then the parents' group started going off in her palm,
buzz after buzz, and her thumbs squeezed the phone's edges twice,
keeping some of that too.

She looked up from the phone
and caught him not looking away in time.
The glance went one beat past what a table of neighbors allows,
and then one more.

"Cal," Sonny said, leaning in with a roll in his hand like a gavel.
"Settle a thing.
Earlene's telling this whole end the room holds three hundred,
and I say the fire code says different."

"Two forty," Cal said, "with the dance floor down."

"Two forty," Sonny announced up the table,
and the far end fell to settling their dollars,
and the glance was over, and everybody survived it.

Marisol laughed her laugh at the whole business,
hand flat on the table, then leaned his direction
and dropped her voice to a register the band couldn't touch.
"You're a useful man at a party, Cal Sutter."

"That's the rumor I'm fighting."

"Mm. And the gray at your temples backs up every number you say.
It isn't fair to my side of the argument."

"File a complaint," he said. "We have a form."

She laughed again, and he did not watch, and noticed everything.

The gala ended from the edges in, coats and keys,
the band walking the volume down a step at a time
so nobody would feel themselves being sent home.
A woman with a lanyard came to pull Marisol three tables over,
because a room like this was always going to want her.
She stood, and the dress had a closing statement.

"Well, table nine," she said, putting out her hand.
"You argue like somebody who's been inside the building."

"I argue like facilities. Somebody at this table had to count."

Her hand was warm and quick and certain,
and the handshake went one beat long, same as the glance had.
"Next time bring the drawings," she said.
"I'll bring the photographs. We'll trade."

"I've heard worse deals."

Then she was three tables over,
running that room too without appearing to run anything,
and the crowd noise closed over her laugh last of all.

Cal pushed his chair in.
The place card stood by his water glass,
his name in careful gala cursive, folded into a little tent.
He picked it up.
He could not have said why, and didn't ask himself to.
It went into his jacket pocket on the way to the door.

The lot was going quiet by rows, engines and gravel,
one whoop somewhere that got shushed and then laughed at.
Behind him the air handler kept its thin ring going
for an empty room.
Out past the north edge of things
the House stood on its hill in the dark,
and he knew right where it was without turning his head.

Two hours of her, filed under problems.
He drove home with the windows up and the radio off,
and the quietest thing in the truck was the card in his pocket,
and he heard it the whole way anyway.
