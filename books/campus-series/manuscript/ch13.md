> **On Grapevine, Ashford —**
>
> **Patti Ann Roby:** FOUND — one retainer, in a waffle. Praise God and
> come claim it. I am not touching it twice.

The Doss County Curb Market broke camp at noon on Saturdays,
all at once, in every direction, with advice.

The last Saturday of September had sold out of muscadines by ten
and was going home heavy on mums.
Marisol had come at nine for eggs.
By noon she was loading, the tote long since in the car,
carrying Earlene Tatum's crates two at a time
while Earlene pointed out where each one went, and why.

"You're too dressed for crates," Earlene said.

"These are my crate clothes."

"Then I'd pay money to see the church ones."

"Tomorrow. Fifth Sunday."

"I know what Sunday it is," Earlene said. "I'm bringing dahlias.
Tell Birdie the centerpieces are spoken for."

The Zinnia Wagon picked that exact hour to die.

It quit loaded, buckets to the rails,
cranking fast and dry and catching nothing,
while Birdie Pham sat at the wheel with the window down
and talked to it low in two languages.
It caught in neither.

The truck drew six leaning men before the hood was all the way up,
and six diagnoses, and no two of the six agreed.
Fuel pump was in there somewhere, in the minority.

Marisol had the station's number saved under T,
between the tag office and the tire shop,
and Tick Moran took her call where Tick took every call,
which was on the air.

"Public-service item on the swap shop," Tick told the county.
"Flower truck down at the Curb Market, loaded and lovely,
needing a hook and a slow road.
First rig on the square gets thanked by name on this station.
Caller five, go ahead. Is the tiller sold or is it not?"

Eleven minutes later the turf tractor came down the hill.

Eli Fountain backed it to the Wagon's bumper on the first try,
hooked on a strap that had plainly done this work before,
and stood up off the seat to read the route,
the bricks, the angles, the parked half-tons,
twice, before he touched anything else.

"Somebody holds the Wagon's wheel," he said.

"Not me," Birdie said. "I'm not riding shotgun in my own bad news.
I'm riding where the town can see me."

"I'll steer," Marisol said, and had the passenger door open
on the second pull, which was the pull it took,
and slid across to the wheel.

Birdie stepped up on the tractor's running board.

"Square's got a crown in it," Eli said.
"Bricks sit higher down the middle than at the rails.
You can't see it.
You'll feel it in your feet if you're stood exactly right."

"Then stand me right," Birdie said.

He took her by the elbow and moved her half a step in,
set her hand on the mirror bracket and closed her fingers on it,
like the half step mattered.

They crossed the square at parade speed.

Three miles an hour, maybe four, the strap riding low and easy,
and the Wagon's radio still worked fine,
so Marisol steered a dead truck across the middle of Ashford
while Tick narrated her doing it.

"Update from the square, we are moving," Tick said,
somewhere between a tiller and a gooseneck trailer.
"Turf tractor, flower truck, westbound past the courthouse
at four mile an hour. Give them the road, y'all,
it will not cost you much of your afternoon.
Caller six, hold that thought."

Birdie kept one hand on the bracket where Eli had closed it
and gave the square the other one,
elbow steady, wrist turning, a homecoming queen on a float of one,
and the square gave it back,
hands up all along the awnings,
a dish towel out the Checkerboard door,
and Marisol laughed with both windows down,
steering a full load of zinnias at a walking pace
through the middle of everything.

Halfway over, the whole rig rose an inch and set back down,
and the crown came up through the wheel into her hands,
right where Eli had said it was.
On the running board Birdie looked down at her own feet,
then over at Eli,
and whatever her face did next stayed between the two of them.

"They have arrived behind the Checkerboard," Tick told the county.
"That's the news. Somebody buy that gooseneck."

They put the Wagon to bed in the diner's back lot,
and Birdie sent the tall zinnias home in Marisol's hatchback.

"Church gets the tall ones," Birdie said. "Tables run long."

***

The fifth Sunday belonged to Mt. Nebo.

Whenever a month ran long enough to grow one, the circuit turned,
and this one had come up Mt. Nebo,
oaks older than the county behind the fellowship hall
and folding tables borrowed from Providence Primitive
that had to be home by dark or there'd be words.

By half past twelve the grass held every Ashford there was.
Fried chicken arrived by the congregation-load.
The pudding table had its own deacon.
The building-fund thermometer stood at the fellowship-hall door,
plywood, red paint up to the four-foot line,
and Odell Fountain set tables to a map
he kept entirely in his head.

The Zinnia Wagon stood out in the lot among the church vans,
running again since morning.

Marisol came in with the tall zinnias
and jarred them down the length of three tables
before Delores Fountain-Mabry put her on sheet cake.

"You've got steady hands and you don't cut doubles," Delores said.
Delores had run the elementary school for thirty years
and had never entirely stopped.
"Cake table. It's an honor. Act like it."

Tunk Ferrell came across the grass with his usher's tie loosened,
taking congratulations nobody had offered.

"Both services," he announced. "Back to back.
I passed the plate at both and never dipped in it once.
I am here for the pudding and I have earned the pudding."

"Methodists count that as works," Earlene said. "It won't save you."

"It'll save me a place in that line," Tunk said, going.

The serving line ran her through half the county,
and then it ran her through Cal Sutter.

The shirt was white and ironed, the collar open,
and the early-afternoon sun found every outdoor year on the man
and called it a finish instead of wear,
which was more information than a cake table needed.

"That man irons," Verna Poteat said, from behind the pies,
in the voice she used for verdicts.

"I hear the square had a parade Saturday," Cal said, to his plate.

"A float and a queen," Marisol said, to the oaks.
"You'd have hated it. People enjoyed themselves in public."

"White or chocolate?" Marisol asked the cake.

"Whichever corner's biggest," Cal told the tea urn.

She cut the corner with icing on two sides.
She'd passed a hundred plates that afternoon one-handed.
His she took with both.

His hand came flat under the plate and hers came down over it,
skin over knuckles, in front of God and the whole fifth Sunday,
and stayed a half second past anything cake requires.
Delores was telling the line where the forks had walked off to.
The plate hung level between them,
neither hand going anywhere, both of them looking anywhere else,
her pulse loud in her ears,
and then the plate was his, and the line was moving,
and nobody under those oaks remarked on one single thing.

"Best table at this church," Cal said, to the tea urn, leaving.

"The line moves, Mr. Sutter," she said, to the next plate,
and the next dozen slices came out generous.

Word of the tow had beaten everybody to dinner.

"Four mile an hour," Sonny Dillard was saying at Odell's table.
"You held the whole county to four mile an hour, Fountain.
On a Saturday."

"Three and a half on the bricks," Eli said.

"What I want to know," Odell said, courtly on top of it,
"is what exactly you are waiting on."

Nobody at that table looked at Birdie,
which was how everybody at that table looked at Birdie.

Eli finished chewing at his own speed.

"The truck needed a fuel pump," he said.

It was the worst answer ever given under those oaks,
and every man at the table admired it.
Odell put his fork down out of plain respect.
The laugh carried three tables in each direction,
and Birdie, four tables off, went on arranging her own zinnias
and let it carry.

"Put a fuel pump on that tractor and you've got a sprinkler,"
Sonny said.

Nobody laughed. The tea went around.

Servers ate last.
When the line finally let her go,
Marisol took the end seat at Earlene's table
with a plate she'd earned twice over,
and the folding chair beside her stood empty,
and for a minute it was a particular chair.
She had two of those, one twenty minutes off and one at Bellamy.
Twenty minutes up the road her boy was sleeping through
the whole gorgeous Sunday, which was his job now.
He'd have gone back for pudding twice.
He'd have let Delores call him baby.

"This seat spoken for?" Verna said, pie plate first.

"It is now," Marisol said, and it was.

November came up eventually.
It came up at every table now,
low, and worried, and no place useful.
Delores did not stand up to say it.
She said it at plate level,
and the tables went quiet anyway, which was an old skill.

"I have chaired meetings four people came to,
and one of them was the janitor.
This town has never once been talked into anything at a meeting,"
Delores said.
"Meetings are where we go to be against things in front of witnesses.
What this town does is suppers.
So somebody haul every folding table this county owns
up to that House on Millrow.
String lights on that porch. Set that lawn like Sunday.
Put the whole town at one table,
and make November look it in the eye."

It didn't need a second.
Pencils came up out of church purses.
Birdie claimed the flowers before anyone asked.
Earlene assigned pies to three women who had not volunteered.
Odell wanted to know who had a hitch.

Cal had gone still over his cake in the middle of it.

Still, on that man, was not rest.
She'd seen him at rest on a dark porch with a thermos,
and it looked nothing like this.
Whatever he was looking at was not on the table.

She was still watching when he looked up and found her at it.

She didn't look away. Neither did he.
The yard went on being a yard around them,
coffee and chair legs and somebody's dish list,
and the look ran on long past what a church dinner
gives anybody cover for,
and neither one of them reached for the excuse.

"Sutter," Odell called across the grass.
"You've got the hitch. Providence wants its tables home by dark."

"I've got it," Cal said, and went,
and swung folded tables into his truck bed two at a time,
and made the weight look like a rumor.

The light ran long under the oaks.
The yard came down around her, chair by chair.

Marisol sat with the last of the tea and did her Sunday reading.

Grapevine had kept the Sabbath, mostly.
Casserole photos. A lost retainer, found in a waffle.
And riding in the middle of all that quiet, twice,
the cursive and the stock sunset of Concerned4Ashford,
posted at nine fifty-eight and again at eleven fourteen,
with AshfordSafetyMom agreeing underneath each one
inside of four minutes.

She didn't read them again. She read the clocks on them.

The first sat square in the middle of the early service.
The second landed in the long prayer of the late one.

Two posts, one to a service, filling both slots
a Sunday morning has.
Both services at First Methodist ran the whole length
of that morning, call to worship to benediction, twice over,
and through every minute of it
somebody had been sitting somewhere with two free thumbs
and a great deal of feeling about Ashford,
while Tunk Ferrell stood in plain sight of God
and two hundred neighbors,
handing an offering plate down the pews, hand over hand.

It was the thing Earlene had said the week the sunsets started,
reading over a shoulder at the Table:
"I've got a feeling some of these new users ain't real users.
But they can sway a riled-up group with just a few clicks."

She looked up.
Tunk Ferrell was at the pudding table for his third trip,
usher's tie folded in his shirt pocket,
a man who had stood at the back of both those hours
with a brass plate in his hands
and half of First Methodist able to swear to it.

Nobody else on that grass had a phone out.
She put hers away too and got the notebook instead.

Back page, where the questions went.
*Tunk Ferrell?* had headed it since the night
the Blind Mule convicted him for the fun of it.
She moved it, name and mark and all,
down to the bottom of the page where it could keep.
A man could usher two services and still want two parking decks.
The mark was his to keep.

But he went to the bottom, and the lines above him stayed empty.
Whoever had talked to this town straight through both sermons
had never once eaten under these oaks,
and she did not have a name to put on the line.
