> **On Grapevine, Ashford County —**
>
> **Marv Kessler:** Whatever that beeping is over by the campus, it has
> been going since Thursday. I am not complaining. I am documenting.

On Mondays, Cal Sutter ran the campus by ear.

He came through the motor-pool gate at six
with the sky still deciding what it wanted to be,
cut the engine, and sat a minute with the window down,
letting the kingdom report in.
The air compressor cycled in the far bay, healthy.
The ice machine labored through a fill,
which gave it about a week.
Under it all lay the swept-out quiet of a campus
that had thrown a party for ninety thousand people Saturday
and had since been hauled, hosed, and put back to bed,
mostly by him.

Down the hill a leaf blower started up. One of his.
He could tell by the throttle it was the new hire,
feathering it, still half afraid of the machine.
He'd cure that by Friday.

What Cal wanted out of the fall was not complicated.
He wanted the mowers running and the dorms watertight.
He wanted WDSS on the shop radio and the boilers behaving,
and he wanted, above everything, one season
where nobody needed him to attend anything.
He was forty-seven years old,
and he had spent most of those years learning
that a machine will tell you exactly what is wrong with it
if you shut up and listen,
which was more than he had ever gotten out of a meeting.

Ratchet met the truck at her parking spot.
The motor pool's dog had a parking spot of her own,
third from the office door,
her name stenciled on the asphalt by a paint crew
that would deny it under oath,
and she sat in it with the full confidence of tenure.
She graded everybody who came through that yard,
and she graded by ear, long before the gate.
Delivery van, one tail thump. Crew trucks, two.
Anything with a rental hum in it —
administrators, salesmen, men in thin shoes —
got the head up and the tail gone still,
and Cal had never once known her system
to return a wrong answer.

He got two thumps and an escort to the door.

The radio went on before the lights did, out of respect.
WDSS 1340 out of Ashford, the Monday show,
callers relitigating Saturday one third down at a time,
every one of them opening with "I'll be brief"
and not one of them meaning it.
The shop filled in around the radio piece by piece,
diesel notes in the yard, each one a name
he didn't have to look up to know,
the roll door going up with its long complaint,
somebody dropping a socket and drawing applause for it.
By seven the whole place was running,
and it ran on a sound Cal could have conducted
with his eyes shut.

His second came in off the yard with the clipboard.
"Forty-one work orders out of the dorms," she said.
"The semester has landed, and now it itemizes."

"Give me the worst three."

"Lofted bed let go at two in the morning.
No injuries, but loud enough the whole floor heard it land.
A mini-fridge abandoned in a stairwell like a foundling.
And a smell on East Third that four residents have described
four different ways, all of them with feeling."

"Smell first," Cal said. "Smells don't improve.
And the fridge is a rescue, not an orphan.
Check her over and put her to work in the paint bay."

"She'll want a name," his second said.

"They always do."

Out in the yard somebody cranked mower four and let it idle,
and before long three men stood around it with their arms crossed,
a posture that has never in recorded history fixed an engine.
Cal stayed in the office doorway.
The idle came to him past the radio and the compressor
with a small dry tick riding up top of it,
left side, regular as a metronome with a grudge.

"Lifter," he called across the yard.
"Left bank. Quit staring at her."

There was a pause, then a cover coming off,
then the particular silence of men discovering he was right,
which was a sound too, and one of his favorites.

On the end of his bench sat a crescent wrench
that wasn't his, wrong size for the job
it had been caught attempting,
and it had ridden home with him from the stadium lawn Saturday.
Under it sat a note in his own handwriting that said RETURN.
It had said RETURN since Saturday night.
It did not say how she'd stood there, palm out,
calling it a hostage situation,
or what her laugh had done to the word hostage,
or that a man could return a wrench any Tuesday he liked
and had not.
The wrench kept its own counsel, and Cal let it.

His phone made its noise at nine forty.

Cal and his phone were cordial and distant,
two neighbors who wave.
If it rang, he answered.
If it chimed, he finished what his hands were doing first.
So it had gone nine fifty before he stood at the bench,
wiped his thumb clean, and read the screen.

A calendar invite. One he had never accepted,
sitting on his Thursday anyway,
the same as a cousin's boat sits on your trailer hitch.
Condition survey, Millrow structures. Two o'clock.
From the development office, no name attached,
which in Cal's experience meant several names, later, all at once.

Cal's position on meetings was, on the whole,
Ratchet's position on bath day.
The record was clear. There was no ambiguity in it.
You could get either of them to attend,
but you would be carrying somebody the last forty feet.

He very nearly declined it standing up.
Then he opened the attachment, because he was thorough,
and read the list of structures down the page.
A pump house. A storage barn. The retired steam plant.
And fourth from the top, between the barn and the plant,
Hartwell House.

His stomach dropped.

He read the list again from the top, to be fair to it,
with the shop loud around him and the radio taking a caller
who had strong opinions about clock management.
His stomach dropped at the same line the second time too.

Across the office, Ratchet lifted her head.
She could hear a man go quiet from forty yards.

He put the phone in his pocket face down,
which changed nothing and helped anyway,
and went to see about the smell on East Third.

---

The rest of Monday mostly behaved itself.
The press box called about a stuck door,
and Cal talked them through it without leaving the yard.
"Door's not stuck, it's proud," he said.
"Lift on the handle, then push. Don't shoulder it.
You'll bruise yourself and embarrass the door."
The smell turned out to be a science experiment
behind a lounge sofa and surrendered without negotiation.
The bed got unlofted, the fridge got adopted,
and at the stadium the flag halyard had wrapped its pole
in Saturday's wind, so he freed it and stood a second
while it took up its slap again, lazy, off the beat,
the sound of a season getting comfortable.

His second raised him on the radio at four
with a question about grandstand quotes,
and mentioned, in the tone of a woman laying groundwork,
that parents' weekend was only six weeks out.
"That's a Tuesday problem," Cal said, and meant it,
and for one more hour it was even true.

He knocked off at five thirty and fed Ratchet,
who lived at the shop on the reasonable grounds that she ran it,
and told her to mind the gate,
which she took under advisement.

The last stop of a Monday was not in any system.
There was no work order for it, no account code,
no line in any budget that campus had ever passed,
and the parts for it rode in a milk crate in his own truck bed,
paid for in cash at the supply house,
where they had long since quit asking what job it was for.

Millrow Street ran along the downhill edge of campus,
where the streetlights were older stock
and came on with a hum you could hear a block out.
He turned the radio off at the top of the street.
That was the routine.
Hartwell House stood back behind two oaks,
trust-owned, tall, dark, and, to anybody walking past, asleep.
The key to it rode by itself in his shirt pocket,
on a plain steel ring with nothing else,
and it did not get explained to anyone,
because no one had ever thought to ask.

He let himself in at the side door
and stood in the kitchen dark a minute, listening,
the same as he'd done at the motor-pool gate that morning,
because a building reports too, if you give it the chance.
The refrigerator held its note.
The kitchen faucet had quit its drip since the new washer,
and he took the silence as thanks.
Upstairs a window shade moved on the draft
he had been hunting since June,
both of them patient about it.

The boiler lived in the cellar,
and he went down to it on stairs he took by feel.
The shutoff wheel hadn't moved since spring and said so;
he broke it loose one-handed, forearm taking the argument
and winning it without raising its voice.
He fired the boiler for its first test of the fall
and stood with two fingers on the supply line while it lit,
and it kettled, right on cue,
banging away down there in the dark
with all the sincerity of stage thunder.
He bled the line until the note came down
out of argument, through mutter, into hum.
"That's more like it," he told it.
"You old fraud. Frightening people in August."
Then he shut it back down to wait for the season,
which would come whatever the heat outside kept claiming.

He carried the extension ladder up from the truck one-armed,
set it with a small exact drop of his shoulder,
and the gutters took twenty minutes by the porch light,
oak trash from Saturday's wind, two tennis balls,
no explanation offered or requested,
and then he ran the hose into the run
until the downspout stopped gargling and said clean.

The upstairs sash had been sticking,
and by the sound of the radio there was rain due midweek,
so he went up, since he was there.
He went up in the dark,
because he knew this house in the dark.

He stepped over the third step without thinking about it.
The tread was worn pale there, even by streetlight;
its creak was the one sound in the building
he never asked for,
and long habit carried his boot past it clean.

There were photographs going up the staircase wall.
He kept his hand on the rail and his eyes on the landing
and climbed at an even pace, practiced,
a man who had made this particular climb so many times
he could do it without seeing anything at all.

The sash gave up its stick to a candle stub
run along the channel,
and the latch went from a fight to a click,
and the click was the sort of sound
Cal would drive across town for.

Coming down, he stepped over the third step again.
The house, which reported everything else in it,
kept that one sound, and he let it keep it.

He locked the side door and stood on the step a moment
with his hand still on the knob,
listening the house down.
Refrigerator. Shade on its draft. Nothing else moving.
He let go of the knob.

In the truck he sat with the phone a second
and looked at Thursday.
He could decline it, and it would only come back
with reinforcements.
He accepted, thumb unhurried about it,
and set the phone face up on the bench seat
where he could keep an eye on it.
A quiet season.
He'd had his mouth all set for one.

At the corner he brought WDSS back up,
the night show running its swap-and-shop hour,
a man with a stock trailer and a woman who wanted one
finding each other on the open air,
and Cal turned it up and drove home through the middle of it,
forty-one work orders behind him, Thursday ahead of him,
and the season one Monday older.
He had wanted a quiet one.
He would settle for a running one.
