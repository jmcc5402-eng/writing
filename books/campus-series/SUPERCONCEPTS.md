# Campus Series — Super Concepts

**Status: LOCKED — ratified by the author 2026-08-13 (PR #45,
option A: "approve the four promises as written").**
The concepts that make these books win. Every chapter must *value*
them: not necessarily incorporate, never cheapen (workspace hard
rule 8). Editorial agents judge chapters against this file.

Scope rule (per STANDARDS 22–23 and `notes/set-one-first-2026-08-11.md`
F6): every concept below is fully deliverable inside ONE book. None
requires the reader to know, or care, that a sixteen-book arc exists.
A reader who stops after Set 1 got the whole value of all four.

SC1 carries the author's own draft from STANDARDS.md item 21 — the
wording and both tests are hers, not reinvented here.

## SC1 — The town is the character

A college town is a small town with a calendar, a stadium, and a bell
tower — and this series is about the towns themselves: the whole adult
ecosystem that makes one run. Every book shows the town *working* —
its economy, rituals, seasons, and rooms — beyond the campus gates and
beyond the parent boards. The diner counter, the inn, the pub, the
paper, the sugarhouse, the BBQ smoker at 8 a.m. carry the story as
much as any quad. The reader buys the next book the way you book the
same cottage next summer: to go back to a place that felt run by real
people — while game days, foliage, and June Gloom make it a town no
ranch-and-diner comp can fake.

**Chapter test:** could this scene happen in any town? If yes, it
hasn't earned this one. Second test: does the chapter touch at least
one town institution that is not the college and not a chat room (the
diner, the inn, the pub, the paper, the sugarhouse, the market)?

**Today:** the standard exists; the town doesn't. The Ashford town has
no name, map, or non-campus economy ([TK — queue item 2, town census],
`notes/set-one-first-2026-08-11.md` F5). Until the census names the
rooms, the second test can't be run against an outline. The plot
sweep's Set 1 spine already leans the right way (the council vote, the
BBQ joint, the paper) — protect that in revision.

## SC2 — Cozy is earned (the town saves itself in summer light)

The warm room is never wallpaper. It is always genuinely threatened —
a house to bulldoze, an inn to sell, a pub priced out — and always
saved by named community effort, never luck, never a rich stranger's
check (workspace hard rule 5, on the page). And each quartet keeps a
ritual promise: the fourth book ends with the whole town assembled in
warm summer light — a council vote won at the doors, a reunion, a
hearing packed with neighbors — the way a comedy ends with a wedding.
The reader buys book four *for* that assembly, and it lands because
the threat was real and the rescue was work the reader watched people
do. This absorbs the plot sweep's summer-ritual declaration (audit
finding 25's disposition): the repetition is deliberate, and it
escalates in kind.

**Chapter test:** does the chapter treat the setting as something
someone fights for, or as wallpaper? And when anything is rescued on
the page, can you name the planted effort that earned it — or did the
truth just arrive?

**Today:** designed in, not yet drafted. 1.1's resolution is already
the model — the payroll answer is *found, not wished* (the restoration
contract employs Walt's crew, `notes/plot-sweep-2026-08-08.md` §3).
1.4's summer council vote is the Set 1 assembly; its venue is [TK]
(audit blocker 10). Watch 1.4's spiked-story escape — the sweep flags
it must be planted, not lucky [TK].

## SC3 — Home is a person (and everything else has a lease)

Everyone in these towns is built to leave — not by semesters now, but
by the clocks adults actually live on: a one-year appointment, a
sponsored visa, a tenure vote, a custody calendar, a lease the
college's growth just repriced. And behind every adult stands the
biggest leaving of all: the kid whose chair is empty at Thanksgiving,
the dot on the app, the voicemail not yet returned. Every couple must
solve staying honestly — staying costs something real, and the book
says what. That ache is the 35–45 reader's exact ache
(ADULT-CAST-IDEAS §1.7); she buys the next book because these are the
only romances on the shelf about *her* leases, her clocks, her empty
chair.

**Chapter test:** does the chapter deepen or dodge the leaving/staying
question — and can you name the adult clock (contract, visa, lease,
tenure, custody, season) pressing on it?

**Today:** the ripple table grades this needs-tweak — the transience
vocabulary must be regrown from adult sources (ADULT-CAST-IDEAS §4).
Set 1's clocks exist and are institutional (the structural report, the
review of Aisha's calls, the demolition survey, the council date);
keep every book's one hard clock visible early (ADULT-CAST-IDEAS §1,
cost 1).

## SC4 — Load-bearing obstacles

No conflict in these books would dissolve in a five-minute honest
conversation. The obstacles are structural — a payroll his signature
ends, medical authority over his roster, a visa married to a buyout,
custody math, compounding debt — so the third-act misunderstanding,
the overheard half-sentence, the withheld text never appear. This is
the brand's trust contract with a readership that has lived through
too many books that waste her evening on a problem a phone call fixes;
she preorders because this series never does.

**Chapter test:** if these two talked frankly, does the conflict
survive? (Test kept verbatim from BRAINSTORM §5 per the ripple table.)

**Today:** the strongest concept on paper. ADULT-CAST-IDEAS §2's
structure check certifies every Set 1 obstacle survives honesty
(payrolls, medical authority, livelihoods, the leak). The risk is
drift at drafting time — hold outlines and scenes to the verbatim
test, especially secret-keeping beats (1.3's sat-on invoices), which
must stay betrayals with structural reasons, never misunderstandings.

## Antagonist pressure check (variance card A1)

A superconcept the set villain can't threaten is decoration. Against
Set 1's Rex Boyd:

- **SC1:** Boyd's expansion literally remakes the town — razes the
  House, paves the BBQ joint, buys the paper's silence. He attacks
  the character itself. Pass.
- **SC2:** Boyd is why cozy must be earned — every warm room in Set 1
  is warm *under threat of him*, and beating him takes the whole
  town's effort. Pass.
- **SC3:** Boyd's money decides who gets to stay — Walt's crew's
  jobs, Ray's land, Priya's career. The villain owns the leases.
  Pass.
- **SC4:** Boyd's leverage IS the load-bearing obstacle in 1.1 and
  1.3; honesty can't dissolve what his payroll enforces. Pass.

Later set antagonists (Meridian, Veritas/the developer, the trustee)
apply the same four pressures by inspection — each is a money story
aimed at a town's rooms, clocks, and livelihoods.

## Dispositions (what was merged or not carried)

- **BRAINSTORM §5 theme 4, "every love story finishes an older one":
  not carried as a superconcept.** Its series expression (Cordelia's
  letters) is S01 — texture governed by standard 22, and a
  superconcept built on it would tax every chapter for a Set 4 payoff,
  exactly what F6 bans. Its local kernel — elders with weight, one
  70s–80s voice per set, the board's 2 a.m. thread — survives as
  craft guidance inside SC1's town chorus and SC2's community, and
  belongs in each set's bible notes, not here.
- **The summer-ritual set finale** (plot-sweep §5; open decision 9):
  included, folded into SC2 rather than given a fifth slot — a
  concept that only tests finale chapters fails hard rule 8's
  any-chapter test, but as the payoff shape of cozy-is-earned it
  binds every chapter's threats and efforts to the assembly they buy.
- **The parent-board engine:** deliberately not a superconcept. Per
  locked standard 3 it is one town institution among several; it
  lives inside SC1's second test as one room the chapter may touch,
  never the room.
