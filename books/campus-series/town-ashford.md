## 1. The town's name

Three candidates for the TOWN (the university stays "Ashford State"
until its own naming pass):

1. **Ashford** — the town came first, by sixty years; the college
   took the town's name, not the reverse. The whole series argument
   in one fact: when Rex Boyd says "Ashford," he means the stadium,
   and half the town flinches. (The Auburn model — shared name,
   contested ownership — without echoing any real town's geography.)
2. **Calder Bluff** — the town named for its ground: a red-clay
   bluff over the Tallow River. Flavor of a river town the railroad
   found before the college did; loses the shared-name argument.
3. **Redbud** — named for the two loud pink weeks in March; a town
   that photographs itself annually. Sweetest of the three, and the
   most small-town-romance-coded; risks cozy-by-default.

**Recommendation: Ashford.** The shared name is load-bearing for SC1
— every fight over the expansion is secretly a fight over who the
word "Ashford" belongs to — and it keeps the Set 1 brand coherent
across the docs. [CHECK: a real Ashford, Alabama exists (pop. ~2,000,

## 2. The town in one paragraph (written for a stranger)

Ashford is a courthouse town of thirty-eight thousand on a bluff
above the Tallow River, and for seven Saturdays every fall it is the
third-largest city in the state. The university sits uphill from the
square, close enough that the carillon and the drumline drift down
Millrow Street to the smokers behind Delmar's, and the whole town
runs on that closeness: the college pays the wages, the town feeds
the college, and neither will admit which one is the landlord.
Live-oak shade, magnolia and charcoal on an August morning, pollen
you can write your name in by March. Everybody's second question is
who your people are; the first is whether you're staying for the
game. It is a progressive island in a rural county — a poetry slam
two blocks from the feed store, a Pride picnic and five Baptist
homecomings in the same park — and it has razed itself twice before
in the stadium's name, which is why the third time is the story.

**Canon note — the town's morning clock (accepted ch 9, PR #91).**
The town comes on in order, to the minute: the Checkerboard's
griddle 5:40 · the Courier's upstairs window 5:50 · **the carillon
tries itself at 6:00 sharp** · Verna's office light 6:15 · the crew
trucks 6:25. Cal recites it "like a psalm learned by attendance";
the page proves it light by light. Any scene set in the 5–7 a.m.
band inherits this order.

**Canon note — the tower (T07, PROPOSED, recorded on merge).** The
campus has one tower and it is **the carillon**; **"the clock
tower" is the town's name for it** — what townspeople call it
(Earlene, ch 1: *"Been here longer than the clock tower"*), while
campus and the university's own documents say the carillon. One
object, two registers: town characters may say "clock tower,"
campus voices say "carillon," and no chapter should treat them as
two buildings. (Without this line, book four inherits two towers.)

## 3. Economy beyond the campus

- **The university is the largest employer** (~6,800 jobs with the
  physical plant, dining, and the medical clinic) — but the census
  is about everyone else: the county hospital (900 jobs), Tallow
  Hardwood Flooring at the old mill site (400), the poultry complex
  east of town (1,100, largely Latino and Black line workers — §8's
  pitfall holds: this county is not white by default), the
  courthouse-and-lawyers economy of a county seat, and the
  hospitality layer that triples every fall weekend.
- **Game days:** seven home Saturdays. The Chamber of Commerce
  claims $12 million a Saturday; the honest studies of comparable
  towns put the sticky local figure nearer a third of that — still
  enough that the Checkerboard makes half its year in the fall,
  and enough that no alderman votes against football lightly.
  [CHECK: figures modeled on published ranges — rigorous studies
  ~$2M/game vs. chamber claims up to $46M/game at Ole Miss scale;
  Ashford's invented numbers sit deliberately mid-range.]
- **Who is priced out:** gameday condos on the square — owned by
  alumni in Atlanta and Dallas, lit seven weekends a year, dark the
  other forty-five — have pushed year-round renters off the square;
  short-term-rental conversion has done the same to the mill-worker
  blocks. Walt's facilities crew largely commutes in from the
  county now; the people who keep the university running cannot
  afford to live in the town it made expensive. (§8's town–gown
  wound, given an address.)
- **The fiscal wound under the vote:** university land pays no
  property tax, so 38,000 taxpayers service 100,000 visitors' roads,
  police, and garbage. Boyd's development agreement dangles new
  taxable frontage — which is why the council vote is genuinely
  hard, not pantomime (mega-theme 3: the obstacle survives honesty).
- **Doss County around it:** row crops, timber, chicken houses, one
  struggling school system, and a county commission that regards
  Ashford's politics as a weather system. The county is not rubes
  (§8 pitfall, New England edition, applies here too); it is where
  half the town's workforce sleeps.

3. **The Ashford Courier** (est. 1889) — the weekly Jo comes home to

4. **Doss Farm & Feed** — Dillard family, since 1946; alfalfa

   where the county and the town stand in the same checkout line.
5. **Fountain's Barbershop** — Fountain Row; Odell Fountain, third-

7. **Magnolia Court Motel** (1962) — Verna Poteat, widowed, still at
   the desk under the original neon; chlorine, cut grass, window-

8. **Doss County Curb Market** — Saturday shed market, WPA-built
   1936, run by a growers' co-op; tomatoes on newspaper, boiled-
   peanut steam, zinnias in coffee cans; worth: the town's weekly

9. **WDSS 1340 AM** — Moran family license since 1951; hot dust on
   tube amps, carpet-walled booth; swap-shop mornings, high-school
   football Fridays, gospel Sundays; worth: the voice of everyone

10. **The Fifth-Sunday circuit** — the rotating church homecoming-
    dinner and singing circuit: Mt. Nebo Missionary Baptist (est.
    1867 by freedpeople), First Methodist, Ebenezer AME, Providence
    Primitive Baptist out in the county; fried chicken, sheet cake,
    cut grass under folding tables beneath the oaks; worth: the

## 5. Civic machinery (audit finding 10)

**The body: the Ashford Board of Aldermen** — a code-charter town:
mayor plus seven aldermen, six wards and one at-large seat. Meets
first and third Tuesdays in **Ashford Municipal Hall**, the 1911
opera house on the square (chamber seats 78 under a pressed-tin
ceiling; one window unit; minutes kept by hand and by habit). The
mayor presides, votes only to break ties, and holds a veto the
board can override by two-thirds — mechanics verified against the
Mississippi code-charter model (Miss. Code §21-3-15; MSU Extension's
*Municipal Government in Mississippi*), which Alabama and Georgia
towns mirror in shape if not detail. [TK: the state — mechanics
hold across the plausible options; details (aldermen vs. council
nomenclature) adjust once the state is chosen.]

**Why there is a vote at all (the plausibility spine):** as a state
institution, Ashford State is broadly immune from town zoning *on
its own campus* — the expansion's on-campus phases never see the
Board. But Boyd's full footprint (north end-zone tower, two parking
decks, and the "Boyd Gateway" fan plaza) spills across Millrow into
trust-owned and private town land. For that, the plan needs the
town to (1) **vacate two blocks of Millrow Street and its alleys**,
(2) **rezone the Millrow parcels** to Planned Institutional
District, and (3) approve a **development agreement** (tax deal,
infrastructure cost-share) — with condemnation of holdouts like
Delmar's riding on a redevelopment resolution behind it. Each is a
Board vote after a Planning & Zoning Commission recommendation and
an advertised public hearing, sign posted on the property fifteen
days out. Kill the street vacation and the footprint fails: that is
the vote that beats Boyd, and it is won 4–3. "Won at the doors"
now has an address — two swing wards (2 and 5), walked in July.

**The venue:** the hearing outgrows the 78-seat chamber and moves
to **the Ashford High School gymnasium** — folding chairs on the
tarped ball court, box fans in the propped doors, sign-up sheet on
a cafeteria table, three minutes a speaker, roll-call vote under
the state-championship banners. (A3 note: July, no air
conditioning; the town sweats out its verdict together.)

**Who sits on it (PROPOSED, minimum viable):** Mayor **Bobbie
Greer** (owns the office-supply store; ties only). Aldermen:
**Tunk Ferrell** (Ward 2, HVAC contractor, Boyd-leaning — his crews
would build the decks); **Delores Fountain-Mabry** (Ward 4, retired
principal, Fountain Row family — the institutional memory of both
razings); **Cy Puckett** (at-large, farm bureau, votes the county's
mood); **Lena Okafor** (Ward 5, hospital nurse-manager, the other
swing vote); wards 1, 3, 6 [TK — name at 1.4 outline time; two
should be reliably pro-expansion so the math stays honest].

**Timing as tactic:** Boyd calendars the vote for mid-July, when
the students are gone, half the faculty is at the beach, and the
town is at its smallest — a documented real-world maneuver in
college towns, and exactly why Book 1.4 is the summer book. The
counter is the census's own machinery: the Fifth-Sunday circuit,
the Curb Market, WDSS, and the doors.

## 7. Calendar and seasons (keyed to Books 1.1–1.4)

**Fall (1.1).** The town doubles. August air you wear like a wet
jacket; charcoal lit by eight on Saturdays; drumline and cicada
static after dusk (§8's spine, kept). Millrow smells of hickory from
Delmar's and diesel from the RV city that grows overnight on the
fairgrounds. The Curb Market shifts to muscadines and mums; the
Magnolia Court's NO on the vacancy sign hasn't lit since June. The
petition clipboard appears beside Delmar's cash register in
September, and by homecoming it is the town's second guest book.

**Winter (1.2).** The gray season nobody photographs. Students
scatter in December and the town exhales — you can park on the
square again; the Checkerboard drops to one cook; the Blind Mule's
TV goes dark. Smells shrink indoors: radiator dust at the Courier,
collards and smoke at the Fifth-Sunday dinners that keep the
calendar alive, cold rain on red clay. The stadium stands empty and
audible — wind in the upper deck — while Boyd's survey crews chalk
the Millrow curbs. Signing day in February is the one loud week.

**Spring (1.3).** Azaleas detonate; pollen films every windshield
yellow-green and is the town's genuine small talk (§8, kept). The
Curb Market reopens with onion sets and chick crates at the Feed
Store; crawfish-boil cayenne steam drifts from the fairgrounds; the
spring game gives one Saturday's dress rehearsal of fall. This is
gala-and-groundbreaking season — Boyd's "unity" campaign works the
same church picnics Marisol's petition worked, and the rezoning
signs go up on Millrow's frontage fifteen days before the first
hearing, right at eye level from Delmar's porch.

**Summer (1.4 — the town without students).** The proof of SC1:
thirty thousand people leave and Ashford is still here. Kudzu takes
the fence lines; the square belongs to lawn mowers, box fans, and
the peach truck from the county; church homecomings run every
fifth Sunday and the gym at Vacation Bible School smells of glue
and Kool-Aid. The Checkerboard closes at nine. The stadium hums
with contractors instead of crowds. This is when Boyd schedules the
vote — betting the town is smallest in July — and when the town
discovers its actual size: the census above, walking door to door
in the heat, sweating through the gymnasium hearing together.
(Answers audit finding 13: the off-season is not silence; it is the
town undiluted.)

## 8. Pitfalls carried forward (binding, per §8)

Restated from BRAINSTORM §8 World 1 and quartet notes — still
binding here:

- One phonetic drawl loses Southern readers; render voice through
  cadence and diction, not spelling.
- The South is not white, rural, or Baptist by default; Ashford is
  a progressive island with a large Black community, a Latino and
  Vietnamese working class, and an indie scene two blocks from the
  feed store. The census's rooms are cast accordingly (the Phams,
  the Fountains, the poultry complex) — keep them so.
- "Y'all" is plural. Students and town alike dress UP for games.
  Louisiana is not Alabama; once the state locks, its specifics
  rule. [TK: state]
- No charged real-school symbols imported as neutral decor (R6).
- The Southern book engages at least glancingly with the region's
  racial history and present; a Southern and/or Black Southern
  sensitivity reader pass is worth it — now concretely: for
  Fountain Row, Mt. Nebo, Odell, and Canaan Bottom below.

What this census adds:

- **Where the racial history gets purchase (who owns what, and
  since when):** Mt. Nebo founded 1867 by freedpeople; Fountain Row
  Black-owned since Reconstruction; and **Canaan Bottom** — the
  riverside Black neighborhood the town condemned in 1968, under
  the federal urban-renewal program, for the original stadium
  parking lots. This is modeled on the documented razing of
  Linnentown in Athens, GA (22 acres, ~50 Black families, taken by
  eminent domain for UGA dorms; the county passed a reparative
  resolution in 2021) — invented in every particular, per the
  invent-freely-don't-echo-specifics rule. The 2006 eminent-domain
  fight that split Jo's and Ray's families is the pattern's second
  act; Boyd's expansion is the third. "The town has done this twice
  before" is what makes 1.4's vote historical, not just civic.
- **[TK — author call, with sensitivity reader]:** whether Ray's
  family (Delmar's) is Black. Southern pit-BBQ heritage is
  substantially Black Southern heritage, and a Black family holding
  Millrow land through two prior takings gives the series its most
  honest spine — but it makes 1.4 a book that must carry that
  history with care, and the choice should be made knowingly,
  not defaulted either way.
- **Invented place names stay English/surname-based on purpose.**
  Much of the real region carries Muskogee- and Choctaw-derived
  toponyms; inventing faux-Indigenous names casually is its own
  stereotype risk. If the author wants one, it gets its own
  research pass. [TK]
- **Don't flatten the county.** Doss County disagrees with Ashford
  and is not a punchline; Cy Puckett and Sonny Dillard exist so the
  county has faces, not a caricature.
- **The gym hearing is not a courtroom drama.** Southern civic
  meetings run on procedure, politeness, and sign-up sheets; the
  drama is who shows up in July, not gavel-banging.

## Grapevine — the PLATFORM model (author, 2026-08-29, RULED)

Grapevine is a platform that hosts ROOMS — any number, any
shape, size, or clientele, opened and retired as the series
needs. Established rooms so far: **the parents' board** (Book
1.2's engine — parent-insecurity register; canon since ch29
"the parents' board"), **the town room** (separate; lost-dog /
road-work register; background texture, never the drama
engine), **The Shed Thread** (ch 3, market vendors). The
series model: each book's cast brings its NATIVE room — a
room's register matches its clientele, not the town's — and
rooms recur, splinter, or die between books the way real
groups do. Moderation lineage is canon material: Marisol's
house rules ("where to park, my loves") are the platform's
founding constitution; who inherits mod power in any room is
always a story lever. Guard (author): rooms stay in line with
real-life group behavior — "real life chat rooms are
outrageous enough" — per the realism rulebook
(book2/notes/grapevine-realism-*.md).
