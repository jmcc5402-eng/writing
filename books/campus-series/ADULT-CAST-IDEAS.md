# Campus Series — Adult-Cast Regeneration

Ideas for the pivot locked in `STANDARDS.md` (no students as active
characters; target reader 35–45). Produced 2026-08-06 by a
`plot-architect` run (variance card A2 — subplot audit: every book
names its B-story and what it earns). **Proposal, not canon.** The
container, set antagonists, mega-themes, and setting research from
`BRAINSTORM.md` carry forward; this doc replaces its invalidated
student couple grid.

---

## 1. What the pivot unlocks

1. **Stakes stop being borrowed.** Student romance had to inflate a
   scholarship or a roster spot into life-or-death. Adults come with
   mortgages, custody calendars, tenure clocks, a pub lease, a late
   spouse's medical debt. "I leave in May" becomes "my contract, my
   visa, my kid's last year here" — the same transience engine, now
   with teeth the reader has personally felt.
2. **Load-bearing obstacles come free.** Mega-theme 3 (no conflict a
   five-minute honest talk dissolves) was the hardest discipline with
   twenty-year-olds, whose obstacles are often manufactured. Adults
   have twenty years of history, sunk costs, and dependents; their
   obstacles are structural by default.
3. **The Hartwell container upgrades from contrivance to day job.**
   Trusts, trustees, counsel, covenants, hearings, and stewardship
   always sat awkwardly on student shoulders. On coaches, innkeepers,
   CFOs, and program directors, the container *is* the cast's working
   life. The finale hearing is now populated entirely by people with
   actual standing.
4. **Recognition comedy replaces observed comedy.** The parent-board
   humor was funny *about* someone; now it's funny *as* someone. The
   35–45 buyer is on that Facebook group. "Told partly in unhinged
   parent-group posts" is a hook aimed at the exact reader who wrote
   those posts — lovingly unhinged.
5. **The power tropes of the shelf become available.** Second chance,
   widow/widower, divorced-and-done, marriage of convenience at 45,
   rivals with twenty years of history — the 35–45 bestseller tropes
   *require* lived history students structurally cannot have. The old
   grid had to fake maturity; this one banks it.
6. **Small-town-romance adjacency with a fresh coat.** A college town
   is a small town with a calendar, a stadium, and a bell tower. The
   pivot moves the series from crowded NA sports romance onto the
   proven, steady small-town shelf — while game days, foliage, a
   Bloomsbury pub, and June Gloom differentiate it from every
   ranch-and-diner comp. (Coach heroes, innkeeper heroines, and pub
   landlords are already proven adult-contemporary archetypes.)
7. **The offstage student is a stakes amplifier, not a loss.** The
   empty chair at Thanksgiving, the dot on Life360, the jersey number
   — everything the adults fight for is *for* someone they can no
   longer control or protect. That ache is the target reader's exact
   ache. On-page students would dilute it.
8. **Town economics become romance texture.** Townies priced out by
   the college they serve, the plow-and-diner economy, NIL money
   warping a family, booster capital vs. a BBQ joint — the age band
   reads money honestly, and every set antagonist is a money story.
9. **The four-world contrast architecture deepens.** Heat/ritual/crowd
   vs. cold/village vs. city/translation vs. light/precarity now plays
   out in adult institutions — booster galas, faculty meetings, pub
   licensing, planning permission — instead of four flavors of dorm.

**Honest costs, and the compensation:**

- **Cost 1 — the built-in clock.** The student year gave free urgency
  (finals, graduation, the May goodbye). *Compensate:* the adult
  calendar is the same calendar — season, festival, term — plus
  deadlines students never had: a lease renewal, a tenure vote, a
  planning-permission hearing, a Home Office interview, the sunset
  date. Every book gets one hard institutional clock.
- **Cost 2 — the shelf keywords.** "Campus romance" search traffic and
  sports-romance packaging no longer describe the product; mis-aimed
  covers would put NA readers in front of 45-year-old leads.
  *Compensate:* re-run the market pass (already `[TK]` in STANDARDS)
  aimed at small-town + workplace + second-chance comps before any
  packaging decision.
- **Cost 3 — ensemble blur.** Sixteen adult professionals can smear
  into "town officials litigating a trust." *Compensate:* the
  no-repeat trope contract, one dominant sense per set, and the
  standing rule — keep every threat physical (a house, an inn, a pub,
  a shoreline program), never administrative.

## 2. The regenerated 4×4 grid

The sixteen-trope contract is deliberately **retained** — the tropes
were never the invalidated part. Every couple seeded before their
book; seeds noted per set. Ages given so the bible's age spreadsheet
starts from real math. B-story tagged **[SA]** set-antagonist arc,
**[PB]** parent-board arc, or **[both]** (variance card A2).

### Set 1 — Ashford State (SEC; antagonist: booster Rex Boyd's
stadium expansion, which would raze Hartwell House)

**1.1 — Grumpy/sunshine.** *Marisol, 43, divorced,* admin of the
12,000-member Ashford Parents & Families group — walk-on son at
Ashford, daughter at Bellamy, both offstage — leading the
save-the-House petition × *Walt, 47, widowed,* university facilities
director; the House is his late wife's memorial (she was a Hartwell
fellow). **Obstacle:** Boyd's expansion funds the facilities budget
that employs Walt's whole crew, and Boyd needs *his* signature on the
structural report that would condemn the House. Her petition asks him
to end his people's jobs; honesty doesn't dissolve a payroll.
**B-story [PB]:** the admin war — Boyd astroturfs the board with sock
accounts and Marisol's moderator seat becomes the thing he has to
capture. *Earns its pages:* the A-story is two people; the B-story
makes the antagonist's method visible town-wide, plants S11, and
establishes the board as battlefield for the whole series.

**1.2 — Forbidden (chain of command).** *Coach Dan Merritt, 48,
divorced* — the old fraternization scandal cost him a season and his
marriage, so the rule has *reasons* × *Dr. Aisha Cole, 41,
never-married,* new head team physician, whose return-to-play calls
overrule him weekly. **Obstacle:** she holds medical authority over
his roster; any relationship taints every call she makes, in both
directions — and he's been burned in exactly this fire. Clearing the
annex for Boyd's demolition survey, she finds the first Cordelia
letter in a wall (S01). **B-story [PB]:** the athlete-parent board
campaigns to get a walk-on cut (Marisol's son — carry-through) and
second-guesses Aisha's injury calls in real time; Dan reads the
board's verdicts on himself nightly. *Earns its pages:* the secret
romance needs watching eyes to be dangerous — the board supplies a
hundred of them, and it's how the offstage students stay *felt*.

**1.3 — Fake dating.** *Priya, 39, never-married,* CFO of the Boyd
Foundation, quietly appalled by the books × *Marcus, 42, divorced,
two elementary-age kids* (on page — allowed), the university's
donor-relations director, ordered to front Boyd's "unity" capital
campaign. Boyd stage-manages them as the campaign's public couple.
**Obstacle:** the fake courtship IS the propaganda — and she's the
anonymous leak feeding the save-the-House side. Going real means he
learns the woman on his arm is destroying the campaign his
scholarships depend on. **B-story [SA]:** the paper trail — Priya's
astroturf receipts and gala ledgers start the evidence stack (S05).
*Earns its pages:* the romance can't beat Boyd; only the documents
can, and the B-story is where they're won.

**1.4 — Second chance.** *Jo, 45, divorced,* the journalist who fled
town twenty years ago after an eminent-domain fight split two
families, back to run the dying local paper × *Ray, 46, widowed,
freshman kid offstage,* who stayed and runs the family BBQ joint now
sitting in the expansion's path — the same fight, one generation
later. **Obstacle:** her byline broke his family last time; his
staying is a rebuke of her leaving; the story she must write to save
his land is the story that ruined them. **B-story [both]:** the
covenant hunt (1.2's letter points to the deed) plus the door-to-door
petition drive — and the parent-board archive coughs up a years-old
tailgate photo proving Boyd knew about the covenant (S05/S11).
**Set resolves:** vote won at the doors; Boyd beaten. *Earns its
pages:* the set finale must be won by community effort, not the
couple (mega-theme 1) — the B-story is the community.

*Seeding:* Dan and Aisha argue at 1.1's board town-hall; Priya signs
Boyd's checks in 1.1–1.2 background; Ray caters every tailgate from
1.1; Jo's byline appears on 1.3's leak story. Marisol's Bellamy
daughter and the listserv she posts on seed Set 2. **Tess Larkin
(program coordinator) × Everett Shaw (trust counsel)** — S03's
slow-burn pair, named and slotted by PR #49, 2026-08-13. They appear
nowhere in 1.1–1.3; their adversarial first meeting is at the 1.4
convocation, arguing publicly about whether the trust's name goes on
the evening's program, then one dance cut short by his phone.
Neither is framed as romance — to a reader who never opens Set 2 they
are two strangers arguing at a party (STANDARDS 22).

### Set 2 — Bellamy College (New England; antagonist: the enrollment
cliff and Meridian Partners)

**2.1 — Rivals with twenty years of history.** *Nora, 39,
never-married,* folklore professor, and *Pete, 44, divorced, shared
custody of two middle-schoolers* (on page), continuing-education
director — rivals since grad school. Meridian consolidates their two
programs into **one surviving line**. **Obstacle:** one of them loses
a livelihood, and his custody arrangement means he can't leave Hollis
Falls to find another; there is no honest conversation that makes two
jobs. Their third door: proving the consolidation violates the
Hartwell trust's terms — a door they must *build*, not find.
**B-story [SA]:** that trust-violation case (evidence → 2.4, 4.4,
S05). *Earns its pages:* it converts the rivalry's cause into the
villain's vulnerability and keeps the resolution luck-free.

**2.2 — Forced proximity.** *Sam, 47, widowed, sophomore kid offstage
at Bellamy* — innkeeper of the Lantern Inn, secretly negotiating its
sale after Meridian's cancelled parents' weekends gutted his year ×
*Dr. Elena Vasquez, 44, divorced,* visiting professor in work-housing
at the inn, one-year appointment, career reinvention after her
marriage and her old department imploded in the same semester. A
blizzard strands the skeleton staff. **Obstacle:** he is selling the
building she lives in — and the town's parents'-weekend anchor — and
the secret surfaces mid-book as a true betrayal to earn back, not a
misunderstanding. Her appointment ends in May regardless (mega-theme
2, adult edition). **B-story [both]:** the inn's ledger vs. Meridian
— and the listserv parents mount a booking campaign to fill the
winter calendar. *Earns its pages:* the romance can forgive Sam; only
the B-story can make the inn *survivable*, so the rescue is earned,
not sentimental.

**2.3 — Secret identity.** *"Wren,"* the anonymous listserv columnist
whose dispatches are shredding Meridian — actually *Gil, 46,
never-married,* the diner owner who hears everything at the counter ×
*Claire, 38, divorced,* the college's new communications director,
hired on Meridian's recommendation and ordered to unmask and silence
Wren. She's been corresponding with Wren seeking a truce — while
falling for the man at the counter. Same man. **Obstacle:** unmasking
him is her job and her rebuilt career; not unmasking him is
insubordination Meridian will use to gut the comms office next.
Hunting Wren in the library's special collections, she finds the
letter that matches 1.2's (S01). **B-story [PB]:** the moderation
power struggle — Meridian pressures the college to shutter the
listserv and route all communication through "official channels."
*Earns its pages:* it raises the unmasking above embarrassment —
outing Wren means handing Meridian the town's last free voice.

**2.4 — Friends-to-lovers at forty.** *Maggie, 42, widowed young,*
Bellamy's alumni director, and *Ben, 43, never-married,* the
sugarhouse owner — Winter Festival co-chairs, best friends for twenty
years, the town's assumed-but-never couple. **Obstacle:** not "risk
the friendship" — divergent futures: she's been offered the Kingsway
exchange directorship in London; he is all that keeps the sugarhouse
and his failing father upright. Neither future bends. **B-story
[both]:** the Winter Festival as public referendum on Meridian, with
2.1's evidence presented to the trustees; the listserv runs the
festival logistics. **Set resolves:** Meridian ousted. *Earns its
pages:* the ousting must be the community's act; and the B-story's
outcome (the recharter needs a London director) is what makes their
resolution honest rather than someone quietly surrendering a life.

*Seeding:* Marisol's daughter's listserv (1.1) opens the set; Elena
guest-lectures in 2.1; Gil's diner is 2.1–2.2's neutral ground;
Maggie and Ben co-run 2.1's emergency job fair. Maggie's London offer
seeds Set 3; coordinator × counsel snowbound argument at the 2.4
festival (S03 near-miss).

### Set 3 — Kingsway London (antagonist: Veritas Abroad packaging the
exchange; development pricing out the Cartographer's Arms)

**3.1 — Marriage of convenience at 45.** *Dana, 44, divorced,
college-junior kid offstage at Cabrillo* — the exchange's London
operations manager, whose sponsored visa dies when Veritas absorbs
her post × *Colm, 46, widowed,* landlord of the Arms, whose
community-buyout bid against the developer needs exactly the
respectable co-licensee and program partnership she represents. They
marry: her visa, his bid. **Obstacle:** the marriage is a fraud they
are both committing, with a genuine-relationship Home Office
interview on the calendar — and real feelings make every rehearsed
answer true and every true answer dangerous. **B-story [SA]:** the
Assets-of-Community-Value campaign for the Arms. *Earns its pages:*
it supplies the external clock and makes the pub's fate the
neighbourhood's business — without it, the marriage plot is two
people alone in a room.

**3.2 — Enemies-to-lovers.** *Fiona, 42, never-married,* the
journalist whose exposé fifteen years ago got an American program
director sacked in disgrace × *that American — Theo, 45, divorced* —
back in London as the independent reviewer Kingsway hires to assess
the exchange, forced onto the Veritas investigation *with her*.
**Obstacle:** she was partly right and partly *used* — her old source
was Veritas's founder clearing a rival — and reopening the story that
ruined him is the price of taking Veritas down. His vindication and
her humiliation are the same document. **B-story [SA]:** the Veritas
investigation itself. *Earns its pages:* only the case can resolve
the past — the wound and the file are literally one folder; no
apology scene could do the B-story's work.

**3.3 — Opposites (class clash).** *Danny, 38, single dad of a
nine-year-old* (on page), the Arms's first-gen, working-class manager
× *Imogen, 39, never-married,* the developer's project lead — her
family's firm IS the developer, and her name is on the planning
application that ends his home and workplace. **Obstacle:** choosing
him means defecting from the family, the firm, and the career built
on both; and she is the only person positioned to bless or kill the
scheme — either act is a betrayal of someone. **B-story [SA]:** her
slow defection inside the firm, ending in the leak (S05). *Earns its
pages:* the A-story is the motive; the B-story is the act — and it's
the set's only view inside the antagonist's machine, which keeps
Veritas and the developer human rather than cartoon.

**3.4 — Best friend's sibling.** *Ade, 47, divorced-and-done,* Colm's
best mate since school, whose entire found family is the Arms ×
*Siobhán, 41, never-married,* Colm's younger sister, the pub's chef —
whose long-deferred plan is a head-chef post in Edinburgh.
**Obstacle:** his roots vs. her leaving, complicated by the best-mate
code and by grief logistics (the pub is where his people are).
Taking the cellar inventory for the buyout, she finds the bundle:
Cordelia's letters were addressed to the Arms's founder (S01 reveal).
**B-story [both]:** the recharter case — investigation (3.2) + leak
(3.3) + the letters, argued to the trust; the transatlantic parents'
chat livestreams the hearing at 3 a.m. their time. **Set resolves:**
exchange rechartered with the Arms as its London home. *Earns its
pages:* the recharter creates the stewardship the couple's resolution
stands on — she helped win the thing that lets staying be a choice,
not a sacrifice; nothing is luck.

*Seeding:* Dana appears at the 2.4 festival recruiting for London;
Fiona's byline runs in 2.3's press cycle; Danny and Imogen collide at
3.1's licensing hearing; Ade tends bar from 3.1 chapter one. Maggie
(2.4) arrives as exchange director mid-set. Dana's Cabrillo kid and
Veritas's ed-tech pivot (S09) seed Set 4. **Tess × Everett** rupture
at the 3.4 hearing (S03).

### Set 4 — Cabrillo Coast (antagonist: the trust's sunset clause;
the trustee moves to dissolve; the ed-tech giant circles)

**4.1 — Celebrity / private person.** *Renata, 44, divorced,* the
volleyball star's mother — she runs her offstage daughter's fan
account and NIL brand, and the ed-tech sponsor owns the contract she
signed × *Owen, 46, never-married,* steward of the Hartwell "analog
house," whose program covenant is privacy. **Obstacle:** their
relationship is monetizable content — the sponsor's contract gives it
claims on her family's public life, and his program dies the day it
becomes a storyline. "Just log off" isn't available: the account is
her daughter's income and her own livelihood. **B-story [both]:** the
content economy — sponsor pressure on the fan account, private-board
parents vs. comment-section parents, Veritas's "global campus"
product circling (S09). *Earns its pages:* the B-story is what
*enforces* the obstacle; without the sponsor and the boards, the
privacy conflict dissolves in one honest talk.

**4.2 — Single parent.** *Lucía, 43, divorced, seven-year-old on
page,* director of the Hartwell nontraditional-scholars program — the
first line the trustee cuts × *Nate, 38, never-married,* who runs the
campus childcare co-op her life depends on. **Obstacle:** time itself
— custody calendar, quarter-system sprint, a program dying on a
deadline; every hour for him is taken from a ledger that has no spare
hours. The obstacle is arithmetic, and arithmetic survives honesty.
**B-story [both]:** saving the scholars' line at the trustee's budget
hearings, with the parent board's meal-train machinery repurposed as
a campaign; the program director's own quiet second chance (S10)
resolves here in the margins. *Earns its pages:* "time as antagonist"
needs an institutional face, and the B-story gives the romance its
clock and its villain.

**4.3 — Co-founders.** *June, 42, widowed* (her late husband's
medical debt still compounding), digital-archives librarian, and
*Aaron, 45, divorced,* documentary podcaster — co-founders of the
oral-history project on the exchange's fifty years. The ed-tech
acquisition offer clears her debt — and hands the letters to the
trustee's ally. **Obstacle:** her solvency vs. their life's work; he
can't ask her to stay poor for an archive, and she can't pretend the
sale isn't a burial. Feelings resolve nothing; only the disposition
of the archive does. Their project surfaces Cordelia's last letter
(S01 → 4.4). **B-story [SA]:** the acquisition fight — who owns a
story (the set's stated theme), feeding S05. *Earns its pages:* the
couple's business conflict IS the series conflict in miniature; the
B-story makes book and series climax the same question.

**4.4 — Slow burn (series finale).** *Unchanged, as locked:* the
program's traveling coordinator × the trust's junior counsel — now
~45 and ~48, adversaries by job description across four sets
(near-miss 1.4, snowbound 2.4, rupture 3.4). He finds the codicil —
the trust perpetuates if the program "finishes Cordelia's story" —
and disclosing it ends his career. **B-story [both]:** the sunset
hearing itself — the full evidence stack (S05: 1.4 covenant, 2.1
violation, 3.3 leak, 3.4 recharter, 4.3 archive) plus the board's
photo archive (S11) and the Wren columns read into the record (S07).
**Series resolves:** hearing won, trust renewed under alumni
stewardship, reunion convocation with every couple on page, final
photograph (S04), toddy served (S08). *Earns its pages:* by design —
the romance climax and the plot climax are one scene; the B-story is
the room it happens in.

*Seeding:* Renata's fan account is visible from 3.4's livestream
chapter; Owen hosts the 3.4 evacuated archive boxes; Lucía speaks at
the Set 3 recharter hearing by video; June corresponded with Claire
(2.3) over the library letter-match; Aaron interviewed Jo (1.4) for
the project's first episode.

**Structure check:** sixteen tropes, no repeats (the original
contract table, recast). Life stages: five leads are parents of
offstage college kids (Marisol, Ray, Sam, Dana, Renata — all had kids
at 23–28, so the math holds), four parents of on-page younger kids
(Marcus, Pete, Danny, Lucía), five widowed, several divorced or
never-married, one divorced-and-done. Every obstacle survives the
five-minute honest conversation (payrolls, medical authority,
livelihoods, visas, custody math, debt — all structural). Escalation
preserved: one building → one college → the program's identity → the
program's existence. Students appear only in aggregate and as
artifacts throughout.

## 3. The parent-board engine

> **Scope note (locked standard 3, added after this run):** the town
> is the theme and the spine; the boards are one town institution
> among several. Everything below stands, but scaled as one engine
> alongside the diner, the inn, the pub, the paper, and the gala —
> not as the series' organizing device.

1. **Admin rights as plot currency.** When the leads are the parents,
   moderator seats are political offices. Boyd must capture Marisol's
   admin seat (1.1); Meridian tries to shutter the listserv (2.3);
   the sponsor wants the fan account's keys (4.1). Who can pin, who
   can delete, who approves members — each set's antagonist has to
   win the board to win the town, which makes moderation drama
   load-bearing, not wallpaper.
2. **The chorus now sings about the leads.** Keep the interstitial
   posts, but invert them: the board live-blogs the leads' own
   romances, wildly misreading them ("who was that with Coach Merritt
   at the tailgate, asking for a friend"). The tailgate-photo
   relationship reveal replaces the town dance — and the leads read
   their own story getting it wrong, which is both the comedy and,
   twice a set, the plot.
3. **Anonymity as identity plot.** Adults have more to lose behind a
   handle: Wren the columnist (2.3), Priya's leak account (1.3), and
   the series-long burner with suspiciously good inside information —
   payoff candidate: the trustee himself, lurking, which humanizes
   him before Set 4 needs him human.
4. **The surveillance arc is now a character arc.** Life360, the
   flight tracker, the dining-hall panic — played on leads, not
   extras. Marisol watching her son's dot; the Set 3 chat's "she
   hasn't answered in NINE hours" (she's at the pub). Learning to
   close the app is a real 35–45 emotional beat: the offstage-student
   mechanic *enforced by* the comedy engine. Recognition, never
   contempt.
5. **The board as evidence locker.** The decade-deep photo archive is
   discovery material: the years-old tailgate photo that sinks Boyd
   (1.4),
   booking records that save the inn (2.2), timestamps at the sunset
   hearing (4.4 → S05). What the group over-documented for love turns
   out to be the case — theme 1's "cozy is earned by community"
   stated as literal exhibits.
6. **The 2 a.m. thread.** The board's soft underside: posts from
   parents whose kids stopped calling, the empty-nest confessions,
   the meal train for a widowed lead. This is where mega-theme 4
   lives now, where at least one board regular gets a quiet arc per
   set — and it's the page the target reader dog-ears.
