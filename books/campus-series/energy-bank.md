# Ashford — The College-Town Energy Bank

**STATUS: PROPOSED — author ratification via PR. Nothing below is
canon.** Produced 2026-08-14 by a `culture-researcher` run (variance
card A5 — midpoint-first: the middle of the academic year is banked

---

## 1. How to use this file

This is a bank, not an outline.
Every entry is written to be spendable by an **adult** who lives in
Ashford year-round — the coach, the innkeeper, the caterer, the
facilities director, the barber, the parent — because the energy
source of a college town may never walk on stage here.
Students appear as weather, traffic, noise, revenue, trash, and
absence.
That constraint is the opportunity: the forty-year-old's college town
is a nearly unwritten subject, and it is the one this series owns.

**Evidence key**, applied to every entry.

- **[F]** — researched fact, sourced in §13.
- **[R]** — plausible regional texture: true of the comps in shape,
  not verified for any one town; safe to write, not safe to assert as
  fact in marketing copy.
- **[I]** — invention. Ashford's own; not researched, not borrowed.
  Becomes canon only by PR.

Real institutions are named here as **referents**, then fictionalized,
per R6.
Nothing real gets skinned onto Ashford.

## 2. The middle of the year (January 2 – spring break)

The card is right about the money.
A bank made of game days and graduation is a postcard, and fourteen
more books have to eat.
The middle is also where the town is most itself, because it is the
stretch nobody visits it for.

### 2.1 February, at full depth — the hinge month

February in a football town is a paradox worth an entire book.
It is the calendar's emptiest month and the year's most dangerous one.

**The economics of the dead month.** January and February are the
slowest revenue months in the restaurant business generally — guests
go out about 11% less often and spend about 3% less per ticket [F] —
and in a college town the post-holiday dip lands on top of a student
body that came back broke.
The Checkerboard runs one cook.
The Blind Mule's Tuesday take is forty dollars and a bar bet.
This is the month Ray looks at Delmar's payroll and does arithmetic
he does not tell anyone about, and it is why Boyd's money is a real
argument and not a cartoon (SC4).

**Severe weather is the month's actual event.** The Deep South's
tornado season is bimodal, and its primary peak runs **February
through mid-April**, with a second peak in November and December that
occurs nowhere else in the country [F].
This is the single most under-written high-energy communal ritual
available to this series, and it is adults-only by nature.

- The watch goes up in the afternoon and the whole town's evening
  reorganizes around a television [R]. In Alabama the referent is a
  suspendered TV meteorologist the entire state obeys by first name;
  fictionalize him into a regional broadcast that WDSS relays, and
  let Tick Moran read county-by-county polygons at 9 p.m. [I]
- The specifics that make it real: shoes on, bike helmets on the
  kids, the bathtub with a mattress over it, the weather radio that
  only ever goes off at 2 a.m., the church fellowship hall unlocked
  as a shelter, the county EMA volunteer who is also the guy who
  sells you feed [R].
- The morning after is the town at its best and most photographable —
  chainsaws by seven, the same fifteen trucks, a line at the one
  restaurant with power [R].
- **Sensitivity flag.** Tornadoes killed real people in real towns in
  living memory. Ashford may be frightened and may be spared; a
  fictional catastrophe used as romance atmosphere would be a bad
  look. Near-miss is the register.

**Baseball is the month's crowd.** SEC baseball opens mid-February —
the 2026 season began the weekend of February 13–15 [F] — and opening
weekends at the top programs set attendance records [F].
This is the first outdoor crowd since November, and it is the most
adult crowd of the year: no student section to speak of, sixty-three
degrees, sunburn on the left side of everyone's face, retirees with
scorebooks, families with a cooler.

- **The referent to fictionalize** is Mississippi State's Left Field
  Lounge, where fans backed custom-built truck rigs to the outfield
  fence for decades [F]. **Ashford's version [I]: "the Truck Row"** —
  a gravel strip beyond the right-field fence where twenty pickups
  park tailgate-out, the same twenty, by an unwritten seniority
  nobody will explain to a newcomer.
- The concession stand sells boiled peanuts out of a roaster and the
  bag sweats through [R].

**Basketball is the month's honest joke.** The football school's
midweek basketball game is half a house, the pep band outnumbers the
students, and the people in the good seats are there because of a
donor obligation [R].
Then one Saturday it sells out because a ranked team is in town, and
the whole town remembers it has a basketball team.
Both versions are usable, and the contrast is character.

**February signing day is now theater without a play.** See §11 FC1 —
this is the bank's most important correction.
The December early signing period has absorbed essentially all of the
top recruiting class; the February signing day passed in 2026 with
one of the top 300 prospects unsigned, and all of them had signed in
December [F].
The comic gift: **WDSS still runs its three-hour February signing-day
broadcast** for a class that signed ten weeks ago, complete with
call-in analysis of a fax machine that hasn't received anything since
2011 [I]. That is an 80s-movie joke built entirely out of a true fact.

**What blooms, and what freezes.** Camellias are at peak — the
Camellia japonica season is winter into spring [F], and the camellia
is Alabama's state flower [F] — and the Gulf South's garden clubs run
judged camellia shows through January and February, in extension
buildings and armories, blooms floated in cups, ACS-accredited judges,
entries closed at 9:30 a.m. sharp [F].
Japanese magnolias open in February before their leaves, and an open
magnolia flower turns brown at about 30°F [F].
That is the town's annual heartbreak on a two-week cycle, and it is a
free metaphor the prose should use exactly once per series.

**Ashford's own February inventions [I], all inventable, none
researched.**

- **The Camellia Court.** The garden club's February show in the
  National Guard armory. Same three families have taken Best Bloom
  since 1958; the standing scandal is whether a winning japonica was
  actually grown eighty miles south and driven up in a cooler.
- **First Warm Friday.** Unscheduled and unofficial: the first Friday
  over seventy degrees, when the whole town eats outside without
  anyone deciding to. The Blind Mule props its door, Delmar's sells
  out by two, and the schools lose the last hour of the day. A
  tradition with no date is the truest small-town tradition there is,
  and it is a free chapter opener in any book set in the middle.
- **The Millrow Burn.** Mid-February chili cookoff on the closed block
  of Millrow, run by the volunteer fire department, judged by Odell
  Fountain, trophy welded from an exhaust manifold. Students think it
  is deeply uncool, which is the joke and the point.
- **Coach's Table.** Thursday nights on WDSS, live from Delmar's
  screen porch, the coach takes actual phone calls. Referent: only
  about ten schools out of eighty-plus surveyed still let fans call in
  live, four of them in the SEC [F] — so a town that still does it is
  a town that has decided something about itself. Runs in season;
  its *absence* is what February feels like.

**The rest of February's real furniture.** Valentine's Day is the one
night the slowest month's restaurants are full, and the florist's
whole year [R]. Ash Wednesday and the start of Lent land here most
years, with Wednesday-night church suppers running all spring [R].
Black History Month programming runs at the college and in the town,
and §12 has the note on writing it without tokenism. Chamber of
Commerce annual banquets and Citizen of the Year awards are a real
small-town Southern February staple [R]. Spring football practice
begins in late February or March [R]. Rose pruning on Valentine's Day
and potatoes in the ground around Presidents' Day are genuine Gulf
South garden-calendar rules of thumb [R]. Chick brooders arrive at the
feed store [R].

**Mardi Gras is regional, not automatic.** Mobile hosted the first
organized Mardi Gras in what became the United States, in 1703, and in
Mobile and Baldwin County the schools close for Lundi Gras and Mardi
Gras regardless of anyone's religion [F].
Two hundred miles inland it is a king cake somebody's cousin sent, a
plastic baby in the office break room, and beads on a mailbox [R].
Write it as *borrowed* and it is charming; write it as native to an
inland town and a Gulf Coast reader will put the book down.
[TK: the state — see town census §5.]

### 2.2 January — the loud week nobody outside the town notices

January is quieter than February in every way except one.

- **The transfer portal window is now the winter's earthquake.** The
  NCAA eliminated football's spring transfer window; for 2026 the
  only window ran January 2–16 [F], which means a two-week stretch
  when a coach's roster can dissolve, and the coach's wife, his
  assistants' spouses, the realtor, and the apartment manager all
  know it before the newspaper does. [CHECK: portal rules are being
  revised annually — confirm the current window before drafting.]
- The town is genuinely empty for the first two weeks of January.
  Students return around the second or third week; the Checkerboard's
  2 a.m. shift comes back overnight [R].
- MLK Day is a real civic event in Southern towns — typically a march
  from a Black church to the courthouse and a program afterward, with
  the live local question of who is invited to speak [R]. If the
  series uses it, see §12.
- Deer season runs into January across the Deep South and empties the
  county's weekends of a specific slice of men [R].
- The university's own January is budget hearings, faculty tenure
  files moving, and the annual rumor of a hiring freeze [R]. This is
  Priya's and the dean-adjacent cast's genuine season of dread.

### 2.3 The stretch nobody schedules (mid-Feb to spring break)

Three weeks with nothing on them.
This is the series' most valuable untouched territory, because it is
when an adult friendship or an adult romance would actually have time
to happen.
What fills it, truthfully.

- Standing weekly things: trivia night at the Blind Mule, Wednesday
  church supper, the rec-league basketball a forty-year-old coaches
  badly, a book club that is 20% book, the gym in January and not in
  March [R].
- The kids' calendar takes over: elementary science fair, county
  spelling bee, high school basketball playoffs, the FFA banquet,
  Little League signups and the fundraiser at the drive-in window [R].
- Crappie start biting in March; turkey season opens in the spring;
  the county's men reorganize their weekends twice more [R].
- **The Dark Saturday [I].** Ashford's joke-name for the first
  Saturday in February with nothing on television, nothing at the
  stadium, and nothing on the square. Somebody always says "we could
  drive to Birmingham," and nobody ever does.

## 3. The turn (March through May)

### 3.1 March

- **Spring break is two different weeks.** The university's break and
  the county schools' break usually do not match [R], which is a
  small, real, constantly-usable friction for any parent character
  and for any business that staffs to both.
- Pollen. Tree pollen ramps from February; the yellow-green film thick
  enough to write in is a late-March-into-April event in the Deep
  South [R] (see §11 FC3). It is genuine small talk, genuine misery,
  and genuine comedy — the man who washes his truck anyway.
- Redbuds, then azaleas mid-March into early April [R]. Dogwoods after.
- Severe weather peaks and the February drill repeats, louder.
- The Curb Market comes back up to full strength — onion sets, chick
  crates, the first lettuce [R]. It never actually closed (FC2).

### 3.2 April

- **The spring game.** Historically a free Saturday that filled the
  stadium and gave the town one extra hotel weekend [R]. But this is
  changing under the reader's nose: programs have begun canceling
  spring games to keep rivals from evaluating their players [F].
  **This is a plot engine, not a detail.** If Ashford State cancels
  its spring game, the town loses a weekend of revenue by a memo it
  had no vote on — which is SC1's whole thesis in one decision, and
  a perfect B-story for a spring book. [CHECK: how widespread
  cancellations are by drafting time.]
- Admitted-student days bring a second, smaller wave of parents [R].
- Relay for Life, Little League opening day, the volunteer fire
  department's fish fry, and church revivals are the town's own April
  [R].
- Strawberries arrive in April–May depending on latitude [R].

### 3.3 May — the town's second-biggest weekend

- **Move-out week is a physical event.** U.S. colleges generate on the
  order of 230 tons of move-out waste each May [F]; universities run
  organized donation drives with a dozen box trucks and volunteer
  sorters — UGA's "Dawgs Ditch the Dumpster," Keep America Beautiful's
  "Give and Go," Luther College's "Trash to Treasure" are the
  referents [F]. **Ashford's version [I]: "the Millrow Pickup"** — the
  churches and the fire department run it, the good stuff goes to the
  county, and everyone in town furnishes a rental house for free.
  A mini-fridge on a curb in the rain is this series' most honest
  image of what students leave behind.
- **Graduation weekend is the single most crowded 48 hours the town
  has outside football** [R]. Restaurants booked months out, hotel
  minimum-stay requirements, brunch turned three times, a caterer's
  entire quarter, mortarboards blowing down Millrow, and grandparents
  in wheelchairs on grass. Every adult in the cast works this weekend.
- Then, within about seventy-two hours, thirty thousand people leave.

## 4. The empty town (June and July)

The census already establishes summer as SC1's proof.
What the research adds is that the empty town is not idle — it is on a
different payroll.

- **Orientation is the town's summer lifeline.** Large universities
  run something like a dozen or more two-day orientation sessions
  across June and July, drawing thousands of incoming students and
  roughly a couple thousand parents through the summer [F]. That
  means a **Tuesday-Wednesday hotel and lunch bump every single week**
  — the innkeeper's whole June, the barbecue joint's box-lunch
  contract, a town putting on its good clothes for people who will not
  return until August.
- **The summer slump is measurable and uneven.** Taxable sales in
  college towns fall on the order of 4% overall in summer, but
  individual businesses report far worse — a convenience store at
  −60%, a coffee shop at −40% [F]. Some owners call it a slump; others
  call it a breather before football, which is the real money [F].
  Both attitudes should exist in the cast, and they should argue.
- **The facilities crew's year is inverted.** Offseason is when
  stadium work happens — painting, repairs, resodding, plumbing
  shutdowns in the parts of the building nobody uses [F], and turf
  renovation needs a 60–90 day establishment window [F], which is
  precisely why the work starts the Monday after the last home game
  and must be finished before August. Walt is busiest when the town is
  emptiest and idle when the town is full. That is a whole character
  in one sentence and it belongs to no other genre setting.
- Summer camps fill the dorms: football camps, cheer camps, band camp,
  Boys State [R]. The town hears a drumline in July.
- **The Fourth of July [R/I].** Many college towns hold the municipal
  fireworks at the university's stadium lot because it is the only
  parking in the county. **Ashford's version [I]:** one night a year
  the town owns the stadium — lawn chairs on the concourse, the field
  roped off, the PA playing the high school band's recording because
  the college band is gone. The most SC1 image available anywhere in
  this bank.
- Vacation Bible School, the peach truck, church homecomings, and the
  Fifth-Sunday circuit are already canon and carry June and July.
- Juneteenth is a federal holiday and increasingly a town event across
  the South [R]; see §12 before using it.

## 5. The bookends, done last and kept in proportion

Fall is the famous part and gets the least new research here on
purpose, because the census already banked it.
What follows is only what the census does not have.

### 5.1 August

- **Recruitment week runs before classes start**, and it is an
  economic event for the town, not just the campus. The referent is
  Alabama's, the largest in the country; a local retailer reported
  roughly triple its July business in the first half of August [F],
  and hotels and restaurants book out for families in town [F].
  Written from the adult side — the nail salon at 7 a.m., the mother
  in the Magnolia Court's parking lot on the phone to another mother,
  Verna out of towels by Thursday — it is funny, warm, and entirely
  compliant with locked 1. Written from the girls' side it is both
  illegal under R1 and a cheap shot (§12).
- **Move-in is a logistics event.** Every dolly and hand truck in the
  county is rented; the big-box store is stripped of command hooks and
  shower caddies; the police run a one-way traffic plan; church crews
  in matching shirts carry other people's boxes up four flights [R].
  Somebody's elevator breaks and the whole day bends around it.
- Heat: high nineties, heat index past a hundred, and the town's real
  rule is that nothing outdoors happens between eleven and four [R].

### 5.2 September through November

- Seven home Saturdays; the census has the sensory core.
- Homecoming week carries a parade and, at most SEC-scale schools, an
  **NPHC step show** — Divine Nine organizations competing before
  judges, with alumni back to see the new members [F]. This is
  alumni-anchored and lifelong, not a college phase, and it is the
  one homecoming event with adults legitimately at its center. It is
  also where a Black Ashford alumna character comes home every year.
- High school football on Friday nights is the town's *other*
  stadium, and for a large slice of the county it is the more
  important one [R].
- The rivalry game in late November is the natural climax the census
  already assigns to 1.2.
- **Ashford's win-signal [I].** After a home win, Delmar's flies a
  flag from the smokestack; after a loss it does not. Half the town
  checks the smokestack before it checks its phone. Cheap, visible
  from six blocks, and adult.

### 5.3 December

- Finals, then the exodus. You can park on the square again.
- The Christmas parade on the square, first weekend of December, fire
  truck with Santa on the back, high school band, the Shriners [R].
- **December is now the real signing day** [F] — see §11 FC1.
- Bowl-trip send-off: the town lines the street at six in the morning
  to watch buses leave [R]. Adults in bathrobes, an entire fire
  department's ladder truck flying a flag over the road.

## 7. Chapter-1 opener slate

Directive 5 requires every book to open on a big fun college event.
Ranked within each season, each written so no student speaks and no
two books open the same way.
Each entry names the adult work that gives the scene a POV.

### Fall

1. **Game-day morning, 6:50 a.m.** — the town before the crowd. Smoke
   already up at Delmar's, generators starting in the RV city, a cop
   setting cones in the dark, a caterer's van blocked by a tent, the
   first cornhole board out at seven. Energy without a single kickoff.
   The series' signature image; give it to 1.1. **POV work:** any of
   them. Everyone has a job at 7 a.m.
2. **Move-in Tuesday** — the logistics comedy. Best if the POV
   character's job is to make it work and it is not working.
3. **Recruitment week from the salon and the motel** — funny, adult,
   and unexpected. Strong alternate; carries a representation caveat
   (§12).
4. **Homecoming parade staging** — the hour before, which is more fun
   than the parade: mis-numbered floats, a mayor on a flatbed, the
   step show's sound check bleeding across the square.

### Winter

1. **Rivalry-week bonfire and pep rally on the square, Thursday
   night** — the fire department's controlled burn, the band coming
   down Millrow, adults on the hardware store's roof with a cooler.
   Huge, warm, loud, and entirely stageable without a student line.
   Recommended for 1.2.
2. **Bowl send-off at six in the morning** — bathrobes, a ladder truck
   flying a flag, an entire town waving at tinted windows.
3. **The one sold-out basketball night** — the football school
   discovering its basketball team; the fire marshal counting.
4. **The tornado-watch night** — the most electric scene in the bank,
   but it is not *fun*, so it should be a midpoint set piece, not an
   opener. Noted here so no one wastes it in chapter 1.

### Spring

1. **The spring game Saturday** — one free dress rehearsal for fall,
   with the modern wrinkle that it might be canceled by memo. Opening
   on the town setting up for a Saturday it may not get is both fun
   and load-bearing. Recommended for 1.3.
2. **The Millrow Burn [I]** — a February chili cookoff with a welded
   trophy. Pure 80s-movie warmth, zero students required.
3. **First Warm Friday [I]** — the unplanned festival. Loveliest, and
   the least "event-shaped," so it needs a strong scene goal under it.
4. **Crawfish boil at the fairgrounds** — already in the census;
   good, but the most expected Southern-spring choice.

### Summer

1. **Fourth of July in the stadium lot** — the one night the town owns
   the stadium. Fireworks over an empty bowl, the PA playing the high
   school band because the college band is gone. Recommended for 1.4,
   and it states SC1 in an image before the plot says a word.
2. **Graduation weekend at full tilt** — three brunch turns, a hotel
   with a three-night minimum, a caterer out of chairs. Huge energy;
   sits at the very front edge of 1.4's window.
3. **Orientation Tuesday** — a town dressing up weekly for strangers.
   Warm and specific but lower-wattage; better as chapter 2.
4. **Youth football camp** — eight hundred kids on the field and the
   coach happier than he has been since November.

## 8. The sensory bank

Hour-stamped, because the hour is where the specificity lives.
"Game day" is a postcard; 6:50 a.m. on a game day is a scene.

**Game-day morning, 6:50 a.m.** Hickory smoke already sour-sweet
because the fire was lit at four. Wet grass through canvas shoes.
Generators, one at a time, like an engine yard waking up. The rattle
of a tent frame going up wrong. Ice — the sound of an entire town
buying ice, forty pounds at a time, dragged across concrete. Somebody
running an extension cord across a public sidewalk, which is illegal
and which nobody will mention.

**Noon kickoff, 12:04 p.m., from a porch six blocks away.** You hear
the stadium before the television says anything, because the broadcast
runs behind. The roar arrives, then the neighbors' TVs catch up
through screen doors half a block apart, out of sync, like an echo
that got the news late. A whole town leaning the same direction.

**11:40 p.m. after a win, versus after a loss.** Horns after a win are
rhythmic — two shorts and a long, answered from the next street.
After a loss the town is not silent; it is *conversational*, which is
worse, and the Checkerboard's booths are full of people relitigating a
third-down call at a volume they would use for a funeral.

**Sunday, 6:15 a.m., after a home game.** Leaf blowers before light.
The smell is warm garbage and stale beer going flat in the sun, which
is the true smell of the day after. Cups in the church parking lot.
Somebody's folding chair left in the median, still there Wednesday.

**A February Tuesday, 5:50 p.m.** The sun is finally setting after
five-thirty and it feels like a rumor of something. Delmar's has four
cars. The square's streetlights come on with nobody under them. A
basketball game on a TV nobody is watching. The specific loneliness of
a town built for a hundred thousand people, holding thirty-eight.

**A February tornado watch, 9:20 p.m.** The air goes soft and warm at
night in the wrong way. Green-black light to the southwest at dusk.
The weather radio's flat two-tone. Every phone in a room going off at
the same second, then a beat, then everybody's second phone. Shoes on.
The dog under the bed and not coming out.

**Baseball, mid-February, 1:15 p.m.** Sixty-three degrees and everyone
sunburned on the left side. Aluminum bleachers that are cold under you
and hot under your hand. Boiled peanuts, wet, salt in a cut on your
thumb. Somebody's radio calling the game you are watching, three
seconds late.

**First Warm Friday, 4:40 p.m.** Every door on the square propped with
a brick. The smell of the sidewalk warming for the first time since
November. Somebody's truck stereo, windows down, at a light.

**A March pollen morning.** A yellow-green film on the windshield that
takes two passes of the wipers and leaves a smear the color of pond
water. You can see your own handprint on the hood from yesterday.
Sneezing as a form of greeting.

**May move-out, 4 p.m.** A mini-fridge on the curb in the rain, door
open, one string cheese in it. A futon frame with no futon. Box trucks
with church names on the side. The particular guilt of taking a
perfectly good lamp.

**Graduation Saturday, 10:40 a.m.** Heels sinking into grass. A
grandmother in a folding chair in the shade of a live oak, fanning
with the program. The caterer's van idling in the alley because the
eleven o'clock brunch has to turn by twelve-thirty. A mortarboard
skidding down Millrow like a hubcap.

**A June Tuesday, 11:45 a.m., orientation lunch.** Forty parents in
new university polos, all of them slightly overdressed, all of them
asking the same three questions of a nineteen-year-old tour guide
whose voice the reader never hears. The innkeeper knows the schedule
better than the university does.

**July 4th, 9:15 p.m., in the stadium lot.** The concussion of a shell

built for a crowd that isn't here. Kids running the ramps in the dark.

from the generator. The chain gang, in street clothes, who have done
it for twenty years.

**Food bank, quick.** Boiled peanuts from a roadside cooker. Sausage
biscuits in foil at six a.m., grease going translucent through the
paper bag. Tomato sandwiches in July, white bread, mayonnaise, salt.
King cake in a break room in February. Chicken salad from the tea room
as a social currency. Sheet cake at every civic occasion. A frozen
casserole delivered to a grieving house, with the dish's owner's name
on masking tape on the bottom. Sweet tea, and the fact that "unsweet"
requires an explanation.

## 9. The 80s-college-movie register, decoded

This is the section most likely to be got wrong, so here is the line,
concretely.

### 9.1 Start with the reader's actual arithmetic

A 35–45 reader in 2026 was born between 1981 and 1991.
Her own college years were roughly 1999–2013.
**She did not live the 1980s.**
Her relationship to the 80s college movie is inherited — cable
reruns, a Halloween costume, her parents' stories, a poster in a
sports bar.
This has one enormous consequence.

**Reproduce the feeling; never the period.**
No 80s needle drops, no toga party, no beer-can pyramid, no
production-design nostalgia.
The instant the prose reaches for a period signifier, the reader
stops feeling energy and starts recognizing a reference, which is a
colder and more self-conscious pleasure.

The closest correct modern referent for this exact reader is *Old
School* (2003), where thirtysomething men chase college energy [F] —
because that film is structurally what the author is describing:
**adults, not students, going after the fun.**
Linklater's *Everybody Wants Some!!* is useful for texture and
appetite.
But the prose target is nearer warm ensemble television — the town
scenes of *Friday Night Lights*, the goodwill engine of *Ted Lasso* —
than any 1980s comedy, because what the author asked for is a
*feeling* (energy, appetite, fun), and in prose that feeling is
produced by pace and ensemble, not by references.

### 9.2 The five mechanisms that actually make the feeling

1. **Appetite, on the page.** Those movies are about wanting things
   out loud — food, a night out, a win, a person. Characters eat.
   Characters are hungry at inconvenient times. A romance lead who
   never wants anything but the plot reads cold; give her a specific
   craving and a bad idea.
2. **An ensemble that already knows each other.** No introductions.
   The reader walks into a room where everybody has a nickname and a
   grudge from 1998, and figures it out from context. Ashford's census
   is already engineered for exactly this — spend it.
3. **Low-stakes chaos over high-stakes heart.** The set pieces are
   silly; the feelings underneath are not. The engine of a scene is
   usually a *scheme* — a small conspiracy with a deadline — not a
   trauma.
4. **The authority figure is outmatched by fun, not by tragedy.** Dean
   Wormer is funny because the party is bigger than he is. Rex Boyd is
   canonically a serious structural threat (SC4), so the fix is
   division of labor: Boyd stays genuinely dangerous, and **his
   methods and subordinates are where the comedy lives** — the
   consultant with the laminated boards, the drone that lands in the
   smoker, the survey stake that keeps getting moved by somebody's
   grandmother.
5. **Warmth: the group survives.** Every one of those films ends with
   the gang intact and the town's version of them still standing.
   That is the same promise as SC2's assembly, which means the
   register and the superconcept are already pointing the same way.

### 9.3 Where it stops — the pastiche line, stated four ways

- **The reference test.** If a joke requires the reader to recognize a
  movie, it is pastiche. Homage-by-quotation is the failure mode. The
  book may *feel* like those movies and may never *mention* them.
- **The Monday test.** Student fun has no Monday. Adult fun has a
  Monday and is fun anyway. If the mischief costs nothing — no
  hangover, no unanswered email, no fourteen-year-old who needs a
  ride at seven — it reads as forty-year-olds playing college, which
  is sad, not fun. If Monday is *only* regret, it is not this book's
  fun either. Both halves matter.
- **The permission test.** Adults' mischief is about *permission*, not
  destruction. The closed street, the borrowed key, the sign nobody
  authorized, the party that runs an hour past the noise ordinance,
  the golf cart that should not be on that lawn. Nothing gets broken
  that somebody in the cast would have to pay to fix.
- **The four hard stops.** The 80s comedies did four things this book
  cannot: leering at college-age women (R1 makes it a wall anyway),
  humiliation comedy at the expense of a fat, nerdy, or gay
  character, hazing played as charming, and non-consent played as a
  gag (the notorious *Revenge of the Nerds* scene). Say it plainly in
  briefs so no drafting agent reaches for the shelf without thinking.

### 9.4 The register dial

| Dimension | Too much (excluded by the author) | Right | Too little |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chaos | property destroyed, police called seriously | a street closed without a permit | a well-run event |
| Drink | a bender played for laughs | one more round than planned on a porch | a wine reference |
| Sex | student-adjacent, leering | grown people who want each other and say so | a closed door and no heat |
| Comedy | a set piece that stops the plot | the scheme *is* the plot beat | wry narration only |
| Nostalgia | 80s soundtrack cues | a town that still does a thing it can't justify | none |
| Crowd | a riot | a hundred people all yelling the same word | a couple alone |

**The one-sentence version, for briefs.** *Write the energy of an 80s
college movie with none of its furniture and none of its cruelty: an
ensemble who already know each other, hungry for a good night, running
a small scheme against an outmatched authority, in a town that is
loud on purpose — and every one of them still has to be at work
Monday.*

## 10. The town's own tempo

### 10.1 What the dead weeks look like from inside

The town's cash flow is not a year; it is roughly **seven Saturdays,
plus graduation weekend, plus a weekly orientation bump, plus
recruitment week** — and eight months of holding on.
Every adult in the cast is doing one of three things with that.

- **Riding the spike.** Ray, Verna, the caterer, the Checkerboard.
  They make the year in ten weekends [F, in shape: small-town
  businesses report up to half their annual revenue from game days]
  and spend February proving they can hold their breath.
- **Working the trough.** Walt's crew, the physical plant, the
  contractors. Their busy season is the town's empty one [F], which
  means the man who keeps the place standing is never once free when
  everybody else is celebrating. This inversion is the best structural
  gift the setting gives a romance: the two leads' calendars can be
  genuinely, non-artificially incompatible (SC4-compatible obstacle,
  no misunderstanding required).
- **Living on a clock somebody else sets.** Jo, Priya, the aldermen,
  the school board. Their year is set by a football schedule released
  eighteen months out and a university budget they do not vote on.

### 10.2 Seven ways a college town is not a small town with a school

1. **Population elasticity.** Thirty-eight thousand people, a hundred
   thousand on seven days, and something like twenty-six thousand in
   July. Water, garbage, police overtime, and ER staffing are all
   sized for a town that does not exist most of the year. No small
   town does this, and no small-town-romance comp can fake it.
2. **Churn is the baseline, not the crisis.** A large share of the
   population is on a nine-month lease and a four-year clock. Every
   friendship in the book is structurally asymmetric — the townspeople
   stay, and everybody else is on a timer. That is SC3 handed to the
   author by the setting itself.
3. **A second economy of ideas.** A bookstore, a symphony, a poetry
   slam, a Vietnamese restaurant, and a touring band on a Tuesday, in
   a town of thirty-eight thousand — amenities two population classes
   above its weight. This is why people move there and refuse to
   leave, and it is the "progressive island" already in canon.
4. **The biggest employer pays no property tax, can't be fired, and
   doesn't answer to the council.** It can change the town's shape by
   memo. Already canon in the census; worth restating as *tempo*,
   because it means the town's mood is set by an institution the town
   cannot schedule.
5. **Everyone's calendar bends to someone else's.** You cannot get
   married in October. You cannot get a plumber the week of the
   rivalry game. The school board moves a meeting for a kickoff time.
6. **Age structure.** The median age is in the low-to-mid twenties,
   which makes forty-two simultaneously the grown-up in every room and
   a demographic minority in the town's own streets. This is the exact
   sensation the target reader has never seen written.
7. **National visibility for three hours, seven times a year.** A
   drone shot of your roof on television; a local scandal becomes a
   national one because it has a logo attached. The town is famous and
   anonymous in the same week.

### 10.3 The tempo in one line per season, for briefs

Fall is *performance*.
Winter is *maintenance*.
Spring is *repair and rehearsal*.
Summer is *construction and proof*.

## 12. Representation notes

The census's §8 pitfalls are binding and are not restated.
What this bank adds, all of it in the seams the calendar opens.

- **Black History Month is where tokenism will happen if it happens.**
  A single scene of a choir singing is the failure. The fix is
  specificity and institutional reality: Mt. Nebo's February program
  is a *business meeting* about the church's own 1968 loss at Canaan
  Bottom, the men's choir is two tenors short, the fifth-grade essay
  contest is judged by Odell, who is late. Black institutions in this
  book have budgets, agendas, and internal disagreements.
- **MLK Day is a live civic event, not a memorial.** In Southern
  towns it is typically a march from a Black church to the
  courthouse, with a program, and a genuinely current question about
  who is invited to speak from the courthouse steps [R]. Rich, and
  requires a Black Southern reader if it goes on the page.
- **NPHC is not Panhellenic and must not be lumped with it.** The
  Divine Nine are lifelong, alumni-anchored organizations, and the
  homecoming step show is a judged competition with alumni at its
  center [F]. Writing rush comedy and a step show in the same chapter
  without distinguishing them would read as ignorance.
- **Recruitment-week comedy has one fair angle and one cheap one.**
  Fair: the adults — the salon, the motel, the mothers, the retailer
  tripling July's business [F]. Cheap: mocking eighteen-year-old girls
  for wanting to belong somewhere, which R1 bars from the page anyway
  and which would age the book badly.
- **The poultry complex has February lives.** Shift work at three
  a.m., a Spanish-language Mass, a Sunday soccer league on the county
  field, a remittance app, a school with a bilingual family liaison
  [R]. Write those specifics or leave the plant out; a plant that
  exists only as an economic statistic is scenery made of people.
- **Church warmth has a nostalgia trap.** Church-as-tornado-shelter
  and the fellowship-hall supper can slide into a white-Southern
  sentimentality register. Keep every church a *working institution*
  with a budget, a fight, and a pastor who is tired.
- **Football itself needs an exact posture.** A book that treats
  football as silly loses Southern readers on page one; a book that
  treats it as sacred and costless loses everyone else. The line the
  series already implies: football is this town's livelihood *and* its
  genuine pleasure, and the book can indict the industry (Boyd,
  boosters, condos dark forty-five weekends a year) without ever
  sneering at the ritual or the people in the stands.
- **Class, in both directions.** The visiting parents are richer than
  the town, and the town knows it. Write the resentment without making
  every alumnus a caricature and without making the town noble by
  default.
- **Weather catastrophe.** See §2.1. Near-miss, not a fictional body
  count used for romantic atmosphere.
- **Sensitivity reads recommended.** One Black Southern reader
  (Fountain Row, Mt. Nebo, Canaan Bottom, MLK Day, homecoming and
  NPHC) and, if the poultry complex reaches the page, one
  Latino/immigrant-South reader. Both are already implied by the
  census; the calendar makes them concrete and datable.
