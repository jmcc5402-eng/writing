## Job 1 — the hero's name

Three candidates, all checked against the Set 1–4 grids and the town
chorus (no collisions; "Walt" retired everywhere below).

1. **Cal Sutter.** Cal is short, sun-cut, and current — a name a
   35–45 reader files next to worn denim and good forearms, not next
   to anyone's grandfather. Sutter has sawmill and river-crossing in
   it, right for a man who keeps a level in his truck. Says easily in
   a Southern mouth without costume.
2. **Wade Ellison.** Wade is a proven hero name on exactly this
   shelf — river-water, unhurried, a little dangerous in the right
   light. Ellison keeps it landed and local. Slight risk: Wade leans
   ranch-romance, and Ashford is not a ranch.
3. **Brooks Lanier.** The most golden of the three — collegiate,
   Redford-in-a-barn-jacket. Lanier is an old Huguenot Southern
   surname with music in it. Risk: reads a shade country-club for a
   man who runs a physical plant.

**Recommendation: Cal Sutter** — used in every moment below. The name
does what the man does: nothing extra, and you notice it anyway.

## Job 2 — fifteen moments

### 1. Third-largest city in the state

Marisol wants one Saturday that belongs to her and not to the year
she just survived, and the first home game hands her a whole city's
worth. The RV lots grew on the fairgrounds overnight like a carnival
nobody had to buy a ticket to, the charcoal is lit by eight, and the
drumline comes down Millrow loud enough to move the coffee in her
cup. She is running the parents' tailgate — three hundred name tags,
a sheet cake in team colors, one canopy that will not stay up — and
she is good at it, and being good at something in public feels like
new skin. When the canopy folds for the third time, a man in a green
facilities polo steps out of the crowd, plants one boot, resets the
frame in two motions, and is gone before she can say thank you.
"That's Cal Sutter," Earlene says from the next tent, in the voice of
a woman reporting the weather. Marisol spends the whole second half
realizing she has been scanning ninety thousand people for one green
polo. The team wins, the town sings past midnight, and she drives
home with the windows down for the first time since the divorce.

### 2. The man who doesn't know

Cal wants twenty minutes and a sack of tomatoes before kickoff eats
the day, and that is all he thinks is happening. He crosses the Curb
Market shed carrying a bushel basket Verna Poteat had no business
trying to lift, boiled-peanut steam parting around him, and he
honest-to-God does not know that half the market slows down to watch
him do it. Marisol knows, because she is standing at the zinnia
buckets being one of the half. He sets the basket down, straightens
the wobble in the Dillards' folding table on his way past, tips two
fingers at somebody's baby, and pays for his tomatoes like a man who
believes he is invisible. "Been like that for years," Earlene says
beside her, unasked, arranging dahlias. "Crosses a market like it's a
hayfield and wonders why the tomatoes always find him." Marisol says
she has no idea what Earlene means, and buys zinnias she did not come
for, and Earlene lets the lie sit there in the sun between them.

### 3. Five o'clock smoke

Marisol wants to outrun the four a.m. hour, the one where the house
is loudest with nobody in it, so she walks. Millrow before dawn
belongs to the hickory smoke off Delmar's pit and to exactly one
other person — Cal, on the screen porch with a thermos, an hour
before his crew, because Ray leaves the porch unlocked for him and
has for years. He pours her a cup without asking a single question,
which she will understand later was the kindest thing anyone had done
for her in months. They watch the town come on a light at a time,
the Checkerboard's griddle, the Courier's upstairs window, the
carillon testing itself up the hill. He tells her which lights come
on in which order, every morning, like a man reciting a psalm he
didn't know he'd memorized. She walks home when the crew trucks pull
in, and that night she sets her alarm on purpose.

### 4. The third step

Marisol wants to understand why a grown man spends his day off in a
house that isn't his, so she follows the sound of a wood plane into
the Hartwell House front hall. The staircase wall carries a hundred
years of the same photograph — different dresses, different wars,
different haircuts, and every couple standing on the same third step.
Cal can name them without looking up from his work. Verna and her
Mr. Poteat, 1962, her hand out like a queen; the Phams in 1998 with
the diner keys still on his belt; a soldier and his girl from 1943
who got two weeks and took them. The third tread is worn pale in the
middle, a hundred years of men getting down on one knee in the same
spot, and Cal is kneeling on it now with a plane, keeping it level
for whoever climbs it next. Nobody explains one word to her, and she
understands the whole house before he's finished the pass. She goes
home and does not tell anyone that she stood a minute on the step,
just to see.

### 5. The roses are hers

Cal wants ten minutes where missing his wife doesn't need explaining,
which is why he's alone at the east porch of the House on an off
Saturday, deadheading a rosebush with his hat on the rail. Marisol
comes up the walk with market zinnias for the hall table, sees his
back, and starts a quiet retreat. He asks her, without turning
around, to hand him the twine. So she holds the canes while he ties
them, and the only things said for a while are shears and cicadas.
Then he tells her, conversational as a weather report, that his wife
planted the bush her fellowship year and threatened it every fall for
blooming late, and that it has bloomed late every year since out of
what he can only assume is spite. Marisol laughs before she can
check whether laughing is allowed, and his shoulders come down an
inch, and that is the whole conversation. She leaves the zinnias on
the porch rail, and he waters them.

### 6. The dot on the map

Marisol wants to stop checking whether her son's dot has moved, and
tonight she is losing. Booth for one at the Checkerboard, first fall
with both kids gone, phone face-down by the pie fork and turned over
twice already. Through the window she can see the stadium glow above
the rooflines, and her boy is under it somewhere, a walk-on number on
a roster she knows by heart, twenty minutes and a whole life away.
Earlene slides into the opposite bench uninvited, orders two pies
with the authority of a woman who has buried two mayors, and says
that the first fall is the worst and the second one is also the
worst. Marisol laughs the kind of laugh that has to elbow past a
lump, and eats pie she didn't order, and the phone stays face-down
the rest of the night. It buzzes once on the walk home. She lets it
ride to the corner before she looks.

### 7. The night the Mule ran out of glasses

The whole town wants the same thing at the same moment, which is the
rarest weather there is, and tonight it happens — Ashford beats a
ranked team it has no business beating. The Blind Mule fills past
fire code in eleven minutes, and Peanut Kyzer serves beer in mason
jars, then coffee mugs, then a flower vase Birdie donates off her
truck. The TV's Saturday sound privilege gives out under the singing.
Half the drumline shows up still in uniform and plays the fight song
in a bar built for forty, and Cal's whole crew hoists the smallest
man on it like a trophy. Somebody's grandmother is dancing with
somebody's dentist, and Marisol gets passed down a line of strangers'
hands like a bucket at a fire, laughing too hard to apologize to any
of them. At the door, leaving, she looks back and finds Cal across
the room already looking at her. The jukebox has been unplugged for
an hour and nobody has noticed yet.

### 8. First to see it

Eli wants one person to see the field before ninety thousand people
trample what it took him a week to make. Friday, still dark, he
unlocks the players' gate for Birdie, whose truck is parked at the
north gate with the headlights on, and walks her out of the tunnel
onto the fifty. The empty stadium is audible — wind moving through
the upper deck like breath in a bottle — and under the work lights
the checkerboard he cut into the grass runs to the end zones sharp as
a pressed collar. Birdie turns a slow circle in the middle of it with
her arms out. She tells him it's the biggest flower bed in the state,
and he says grass is just a flower that learned to take a beating,
and then he goes quiet because he's never said that out loud before.
She looks at the field a long time. He looks at her the whole time
and doesn't know that, either.

### 9. Parade speed

Birdie wants to disappear, and Ashford has voted otherwise. The
Zinnia Wagon dies at market pack-up in front of everybody, hood up,
mums stacked to the rails, and before she can even call anyone, Eli
comes around the corner from the stadium lot driving the turf tractor
like it's the most natural thing in the world. He tows her truck the
length of the square at parade speed, Birdie standing on the running
board holding her hat, flowers going by like a float that lost its
parade. Tick Moran spots it from the WDSS window and narrates the
whole crossing live between swap-shop callers. Everyone on the square
waves; there is nothing else to be done; she gives up and waves back
like a homecoming queen. By the fifth-Sunday dinner at Mt. Nebo the
two of them have been married off so thoroughly that Odell asks Eli
straight out what he's waiting on. Eli says the truck needed a fuel
pump, and every man at the table takes a moment to admire the worst
answer ever given.

### 10. Under the home stands

Marisol wants a shortcut to the parents' tent before halftime, and
Cal knows one, which is how she ends up under the home stands with
him while homecoming roars overhead. The light comes down through the
bleacher slats in moving stripes, the steel breathes when the crowd
does, and every footfall above them is rain on a tin world. He walks
slower here than he does anywhere else in town, naming what his hands
have fixed — this brace, that stair, twenty years of Saturdays
holding up over their heads. Somewhere above, the ball must do
something wonderful, because the whole sky of people goes off at
once, and the noise pushes them a step closer together like weather.
She looks up at him in the strobing light and he stops naming things.
The band strikes up the extra point, and whatever was about to happen
folds itself away, polite as a letter going back in its envelope, and
they walk on out into the sun not touching harder than she has ever
not touched anyone.

### 11. The porch holds

The town wants one more dance and will not say why. The fall dance at
Hartwell House goes up the way it has for generations — string lights
along the porch rail, a fiddle band on the steps, cake tables under
the live oaks — but this year the goodbyes are careful, and more
photographs get taken than any one night needs. Nobody names the
thing circling the House; it is just there, the way you can smell
rain through an open door, in how long Verna stands at the third step
with her hand on the rail. Delores Fountain-Mabry dances with Odell.
Ray sends over a whole brisket unasked. Cal, who has spent the
evening being furniture along the rail, crosses the porch in front of
the entire town and asks Marisol for the last dance, and the fiddler,
who has been waiting years to be useful in exactly this way, slows
the tune down without being asked. The porch boards give and hold,
give and hold, under every couple Ashford has. That is all the porch
has ever done, and the whole town dances on it like an answer.

### 12. Millrow, after

Cal wants to walk her home slowly enough that Millrow runs out after
his nerve does, and it is a short street, so it's going to be close.
Behind them the string lights are going dark a strand at a time;
Delmar's pit is banked and sweet on the night air; somewhere up the
hill a few drumline diehards are still practicing to nobody. He tells
her the porch boards were quarter-sawn oak and she says she has never
heard anything more romantic, and the terrible part, she realizes, is
that she means it. At her gate, under the live oak, he takes off his
cap the way men did in movies made before either of them was born.
The kiss is unhurried and sure, exactly like watching him fix
something, and her hand finds his jaw like it had the address the
whole time. The drumline, far off, finishes the cadence. They start
laughing before their foreheads even touch.

### 13. Asking for a friend

Marisol wants to control the story, and the story has twelve thousand
co-authors. Somebody's tailgate photo is making the rounds — her,
head thrown back laughing, and a green facilities polo at her elbow —
and the parents' group has identified the man variously as a new
strength coach, a visiting country singer, and "the tall one from
facilities, asking for a friend." She reads the best guesses aloud to
Cal in a Checkerboard booth, fully prepared to die of it. He listens
the way he checks a roof, all the way through, then flags down the
counter for pie and asks whether her friend would like his phone
number. She laughs loudly enough that the Liars' Table looks over,
which by Ashford law is the same as a press release. He walks her out
past every one of those grinning faces with his hand at her back, and
she decides the co-authors can have this chapter.

### 14. Coffee for twelve

Marisol wants something to do with her hands the morning the pecan
limb comes through the House's porch roof, because standing in the
wet street feeling her stomach fall isn't it. The storm blew through
overnight; Cal and his crew are there at first light with tarps and
jacks, and Ashford's whole answer to the sight is already forming —
Ray sends biscuits, Sonny Dillard shows up off shift with a chainsaw.
She brings Checkerboard coffee for twelve and then simply does not
leave, holding ladders, hauling brush, learning three men's names and
all their opinions. Watching Cal work is its own weather report —
no wasted motion, no raised voice, the porch coming back level under
him almost shyly. By noon the roof is dried in and the rail is
clamped and gluing, and the crew accepts her the way they'd accept a
good tool, which she understands is the highest compliment on offer.
She drives home with sawdust in her hair and catches herself in the
mirror not minding at all.

### 15. Seven Saturdays

Marisol wants to see what the town looks like when it exhales, and
Cal knows the best seat, so they take the House porch for the last
home Saturday of the year. Below them the RV city is folding itself
up lot by lot, taillights threading out toward the county, and the
stadium's last roar of the fall comes down Millrow the way it has
seven times, worn familiar as a hymn. The mums on the rail have gone
rusty, the air finally has an edge, and Cal hands her a mug of
something hot without being asked, which is his entire love language
and by now she is fluent. Her son's team won; her phone is in her
coat pocket and has been for hours, and she is not counting. She
thinks about how in August she wanted the year to be over, and how
somewhere between the smoke and the third step she stopped wanting
time to move at all. The porch boards tick as the cold comes on. She
pulls her chair an inch closer to his, and the town goes quiet
around them like a house settling for the night.
