**Run 2026-09-14 · romance-reader-panel · P1 binge posture (standing
book card) · commissioned by the author, direct.**
**Scope:** Book 1.1 ch 1–30 (accepted), Book 1.2 ch 1–19 (accepted).
**Question:** the author's diagnosis — "mostly plot books with
occasional romance" — and whether the romance and the clothes-on charge
can be raised NOW without reading as bolted on.

Companion instrument to `notes/heat-retail-read-2026-09-14.md`

No manuscript file was edited.

---

## THE SHORT ANSWER

> **He is right, and he is right about the wrong book.**

**Book 1.1 is not a plot book.** It runs ~42% romance-forward, flat,
ch 1 → ch 30, and never goes cold longer than a chapter. What it has is
a **touch** problem — fifteen chapters before skin meets skin — and a
**body** problem: the survey can say what Cal's forearms can lift and
cannot say once, in thirty chapters, what Marisol's pulse is doing in
the same room.

**Book 1.2 has a genuine drought: chapters 1–10.** 26% romance-forward
against 54% for ch 11–19. From ch 14 on it is the hottest, most
romantic thing in the repo. "The problem is that a reader has to
survive ten chapters of excellent football to get there, and the Kindle
sample ends at chapter three."

**So the retrofit is not one job. It is two different jobs on two
different books, and one of them is much easier than he thinks.**

## 1. THE MEASUREMENT

**Method.** Both manuscripts use semantic line breaks, so lines were
counted, not paragraphs. A line is romance-forward if it is one of
standard 26's eight kinds — proximity, noticing, wanting, the other in
absence, between them, the town ships them, the want against its wall,
the private admission — or is a side-romance beat (METERS: side
romances feed the ROMANCE meter). Shares ±5 points; "the shape they
make is not within ±5 points."

**The CHARGE scale:** 0 not in each other's weather · 1 awareness ·
2 charge in company · 3 want named to the reader · 4 contact with cover
(a cup, a plate, a sleeve) · 5 deliberate touch, no errand · 6 mouth ·
7 the approach, garment line in sight · 8 the garment lands, the door
closes (standard 9's ceiling).

### Book 1.1 — aggregate

**42% romance-forward. Mean CHARGE 3.2. Leads share the page in 22 of
30 chapters (73%).** Apart: ch 2, 4, 7, 12, 14, 15, 18, 24 — never two
running except 14/15.

Chapter highs: ch 16 (80%, CHARGE 8 — the right wrench in her open
palm), ch 23 (86%, CHARGE 8 — the cap off, both hands on the brim),
ch 20 (84% — her hand riding his the length of one ledger line),
ch 27 (84% — the hand that stops an inch short), ch 9 (79% — the
thermos lid taken back "unhurried about her fingers").

Chapter lows: **ch 2 (4%)**, **ch 26 (6%)**, **ch 4 (6%)**, ch 17
(16%), ch 14 (16%), ch 24 (18%), ch 28 (18%), ch 18 (19%), ch 19 (19%).

**Longest cold gaps:**
1. **ch 4, l.15–314 — ~2,800 words.** The Boyd fence walk. One wrench
   at the end.
2. **ch 26, l.18–350 — ~3,000 words.** Marisol is *in the room* and
   speaks four times, all plot. **Worst ratio in the book.**
3. ch 2, l.123–303 — ~1,700 words.

### Book 1.2 — aggregate

**39% overall — but ch 1–10: 26%, ch 11–19: 54%. Mean CHARGE 3.5
overall; 2.1 for ch 1–10, 5.1 for ch 11–19.**

> **Leads share the page in 17 of 19 chapters (89%) — HIGHER than 1.1
> — which is exactly why the author's instinct feels off to him. They

Chapter highs: ch 18 (82%, CHARGE 8 — five nights on foot down a hill

### What the aggregate says

- **Both books are ~40% romance by volume. That is not a plot book.
  That is a romance with a strong B-story.**
- **1.1's problem is temperature, not volume.**
- **1.2's problem is distribution, not volume.** Ch 1–3 — the
  sample-length stretch — averages 23% at charge 1.3, **the coldest
  opening of either book.**
- Worst chapter by charge-to-page in either book: **1.2 ch 6.** Then
  1.1 ch 26. Then 1.1 ch 4.

## 2. THE DIAGNOSIS — six mechanisms, in cost order

**(1) The shared scene carries an errand, and the errand brings a
chaperone.** 1.2's leads' only recurring room for ten chapters is the
4:15/5:15 medical check — a printed sheet, a professional disagreement,
**and a named third person listening.** Counted at ch 2 l.174, ch 10
l.277, ch 11 l.47, ch 13 l.371, ch 15 l.151, ch 19 l.335. "Six times
the prose stops to tell me somebody is in earshot. That is craft; it's
also a reader being told six times that nothing can happen."

**(2) Wanting is named and then restated instead of escalated.** 1.2
names her want at ch 5 ("She wanted him.") and his at ch 8 ("He wanted
her.") — then ch 9, ch 11, ch 13, ch 17. **The same sentence five times
across twelve chapters. Volume went up; *kind* never changed.**

> Compare 1.1, which escalates *the object* instead: the wrench (ch 1)
> → the place card (ch 6) → the thermos lid (ch 9) → the zinnias
> watered (ch 10) → the twine in his back pocket (ch 10) → her pen
> (ch 28) → a new pane of glass in her handwriting's place (ch 30).
> **Each one is a different kind of possession. That is escalation.**

**(3) The chapter ending lands on the plot object.** 1.2's first twelve
end on: a latched suitcase, "Kickoff was at two thirty," a quiet phone,
an unread board, a whiteboard, "The game was on the nineteenth," rolled
cuffs, two plates *(good)*, boots under a rack, keys on a hook *(good)*,
a dome light, a phone face up. **Four of twelve end on the couple.**
1.1's first twelve: **eight of twelve end on feeling.** "A binge reader
decides whether to start the next chapter in the last six lines. 1.1
sends her on; 1.2 hands her a football."

**(4) His interiority is about the county, not the woman.** 1.2 ch 6 is
a Dan chapter of ~440 body lines; lines about Aisha total **26 — six
percent** — and she is on the same porch the entire time. "1.2 ch 6 is
about a television." (Confirms taste entry 18, which the author has
already raised twice.)

**(5) Nobody ships them.** Standard 26's sixth kind is absent from 1.2
for eleven chapters. 1.1 has the town in the couple's business from
ch 3. **In 1.2 the town first pairs them at ch 12 — as a scandal.**
Deliberate, and the book's best antagonist idea; the cost is that "for
eleven chapters the reader has no ally in the room. She's shipping them
alone."

**(6) Nobody has a body. — THE BIGGEST ONE, and the answer to the
author's question.** Across 131,000 words, **three** places where the
POV's own body reports back:

- 1.1 ch 5 l.190 — *"Heat arrived at the back of her neck and stayed,
  on no schedule she had approved."*
- 1.1 ch 13 l.200 — *"her pulse loud in her ears"*
- 1.2 ch 17 l.514 — *"felt her pulse where her hand was going to go"*

> **The same three the superfan found independently, from a different
> direction, on the same day.**

Everything else describes the *other* person as **capability** — Cal's
forearms, Dan's shoulders. Standard 25 is satisfied. "But the reader
doesn't get warm from looking at a man. She gets warm from *being in a
body that is looking at a man and having trouble.* Both books show me
the view and almost never the viewer. **That is the whole gap between
'romance-forward' and 'hot,' and it costs nothing structural to
close.**"

### The four tests

- **POEM — finding.** 1.2 ch 9 l.163–170: a ~68-word chained sentence
  as a whole paragraph. "On audio that is a paragraph nobody can hold."
- **MYSTERY — finding.** The Cordelia Hartwell letter (ch 9) is found,
  hidden, then appears once in ten chapters. "The page's weight exceeds
  the plot's by a wide margin." Second: **KNEES, 1994** — three
  appearances, sealed, never opened.
- **THE JOKE — one finding.** 1.2 ch 5 l.170 (Tunk on oxygen chambers)
  — nobody reacts, nothing follows.
- **APART, WHY — PASS, and 1.2's structural strength.** Said plainly at
  ch 2 l.157 and stated whole at ch 15 l.353–363.

## 4. THE BOLTED-ON TEST

### Book 1.2 — **POSSIBLE CLEANLY**

1. **The chemistry already supports it, and the book proves it.** Ch 17
   runs a thirty-sentence approach and does not read as escalated — "it
   reads as *arrived*." Ch 18 is 82% romance-forward, 800 lines, "and I
   never once wanted it to move faster."
2. **The wanting is planted early enough** — her want at ch 5 of 30,
   his at ch 8. "What's missing between ch 5 and ch 13 is not plant,
   it's *escalation*, and escalation is added by changing the kind of a
   beat, not by moving a scene."
3. **Nearly every fix lives inside a scene that already exists.**
   > **"This is not a book where the couple is apart. It is a book
   > where the couple is in the room and the prose is looking at the
   > football."**
4. **The register won't jar, because the register is already there** —
   "the heat off her came through two coats," "a yard of December air
   with him on the other side of it," "his breath came against her
   cheek when he said it." **"There are four of them. There should be
   twenty, and the twenty would be in the same voice."**

**The one caution:** do not raise ch 1–3 by adding *contact*. Raise it
by adding **body and ending-register**. "The book's whole engine is a
rule that keeps them apart, and a reader who sees them touch in chapter
two will not believe the rule in chapter fifteen. **Heat in the first
act must be entirely interior.** That is also, conveniently, the
cheapest kind." *(Independently matches the superfan's warning #10.)*

### Book 1.1 — **POSSIBLE WITH LIMITS**

**Can absorb:** body beats, interiority, look-lengthening, ending
re-orders, one coda per cold chapter. "None of it touches a fact, a
name, a date, a THREADS entry, or a payoff. I count roughly forty
insertion points across ch 1–15, none longer than three sentences."

**Cannot absorb:** any change to the rung ladder (the wrench debt, the
first touch at ch 9, the kiss at ch 16, the second at ch 23), or new
scenes — "adding a two-hander to ch 2 or ch 4 would require a reason,
and a reason is a plot change."

**Would the books mismatch if only 1.2 is upgraded?** Not in the
direction feared. DIALS already sets 1.2 at Heat +1 and Edge +1 over
1.1 (ratified PR #104), so 1.1 → 1.2 is "a series getting warmer, which
is a series doing its job." **The mismatch that would hurt is the
reader who enters at 1.2 and goes back** — she'd meet fifteen chapters
at charge 2–4 with no bodies and call Book One tame. "That is the
argument for doing a 1.1 pass, and it is an argument for doing it only
on chapters 1–15. **Forty sentences. No structural risk. It's a line
pass with a mandate, not a revision.**"

**Said plainly to the author:** "if he does nothing to 1.1 at all, 1.1
still works. Its 42% is honest, its swoon inventory is deep, and the
wrench is one of the best heat engines I've read in this shelf. **The
upgrade is optional for 1.1 and close to mandatory for 1.2 ch 1–10.**"

## 5. THE HIGHEST-LEVERAGE TEN

1. **Give the POV a body — ~40 sentences, both books**, concentrated in
   1.2 ch 1–13 and 1.1 ch 1–15. "Buys the entire clothes-on register at
   zero structural cost. **This is the answer to the question he
   asked.**" *(FREE)*
2. **Fix 1.2 ch 6** — the DNF page. Two FREE lines plus one CHEAP
   re-cut.
3. **Write the four sentences of approach into 1.2 ch 14's forearm.**
   *(FREE)*
4. **Change every second chapter ending in 1.2 ch 1–12 to land on the
   couple.** The material is already in the last paragraph of most of
   them. "Buys the binge — the change a reader feels most and can least
   articulate." *(FREE, reordering only)*
5. **Put one awkward beat in each book before the first kiss.**
   "Highest ratio of charm-per-word in this entire report." *(FREE)*
6. **Give 1.2 a debt object and plant it by ch 3** — the facemask bolts
   are already there and already unreturned. *(small STRUCTURAL)*
7. **De-terse 1.2 ch 3's flap and ch 12's office.** No dialogue
   changes; faces at the turns. *(CHEAP)*
8. **Stop restating "she wanted him." Change the kind instead.**
9. **Let the town ship them earlier in 1.2** — two epigraphs and one
   chorus line before ch 10. *(FREE)*
10. **Extend the Kindle sample to include 1.1 ch 5.** Not a manuscript
    change at all. "Costs nothing and recovers most of what a ch 2–4
    rewrite would."

  never said; the reader still can't repeat it ten chapters later.
- **4 (the leads get closer every chapter)** — RISK, 1.2 ch 1–10: mean
  charge 2.1 with no rung movement between ch 5 and ch 13. Also 1.1

  times in 1.2. "A phrase that has become a template."
- **7 (upbeat, light, and hot)** — RISK, 1.2 ch 1–10: four of the first
  ten chapters end on a woman alone in a room.
- **8 (it is a romance first)** — **RISK, and this is the author's
  question.** 1.2 ch 3 and ch 6 "could each be summarized without the
