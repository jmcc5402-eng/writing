## Findings (ranked by cost)

**F1 — ch17:223–226 — the shippy wink after Aisha's stand.**
> "He gets under every nerve I own," she said.
> "How tall is he again?" Marisol said.
> "Cut the cake, Pruett."

Cost: **evaporated stakes**, plus trust. Aisha has just staked her license in public — "Do not hang my license on your lawn plans" is the best combat line in the sample — and Marisol stood beside her *in silence*, which the text itself says "said the only thing it needed to" (205–206). Then, one beat later, Marisol converts the whole stand into a courtship tease. The line's real listener is the reader who knows the enemies-to-lovers trope; it tells her *don't worry, this quarrel is flirting*, which pre-forgives a conflict SC4 needs to stay load-bearing (medical authority over his roster is certified as a structural obstacle, SUPERCONCEPTS 110–115). If the Aisha–Dan pairing is planned, "every nerve I own" already does the trope work without the authorial elbow. What Marisol would need to want: to bring Aisha down off the adrenaline — and her established tool for that is silence and cake, not a wink. **Minimal fix:** cut Marisol's line. Let Aisha's "He gets under every nerve I own" hang, Marisol answer with nothing but the look she's already been using, and keep "Cut the cake, Pruett" — now aimed at the sympathy in Marisol's face instead of at a joke. Nothing new written.

**F2 — ch11:114–127 — the third comic unit of the mug parade.**
> "This is a candle holder," the dentist said, peering into his mug.
> "It's a mug," Marisol said, rinsing.
> "It says Ashford Sportsman's Club."
> "Then it's a collectible, and you're welcome."

Cost: **trust** (the rewarded-run tell) and **flattened voice**. This is the third consecutive improvised-vessel gag (mason jars → Pyrex grandmother → candle holder) and the fourth-quip-in-a-beat case from the brief. The narration at 98–106 already made this joke, beautifully ("a parade of mismatched churches and bass tournaments"); the grandmother's "Then fill it to the handle" (112) is its perfect dialogue button. The candle-holder unit re-makes it, and puts an authored quip in Marisol's mouth mid-labor — she wants nothing from the dentist; she's rinsing. This is the passage the author was listening to, and on audio, with the narration's spacing compressed, three units in a row is exactly where "everyone is the author" becomes audible. **Minimal fix:** cut 114–120 (candle holder through "collectible"), jump from "Then fill it to the handle" straight to the dentist's moon-landing toast (122–127) — which should stay: "Says me. I'm saying it" has a real want (a drunk man seizing the authority to declare history) and seeds the callback at 408. Fallback if the author loves the candle holder: keep the dentist's two lines, flatten Marisol's to pure transaction ("It's a mug." — full stop, cut "collectible, and you're welcome").

**F3 — ch11:337–342 — the double aphorism at the notebook.**
> "It's the difference between a list and a mob," Marisol said.
> Earlene gave that the consideration she gave a sky in April.
> "Keep the pen," she said. "Town's got plenty of chalk."

Cost: **trust** — two quotables in four lines, and the second is the tell. Marisol's aphorism earns its place: it answers a direct question, it's the chapter's thesis, and she wants Earlene to understand the mark. Earlene's reply then competes with it — a matched epigram, pen-versus-chalk, polished to the same authorial finish. Earlene is licensed to perform, but her want here is small and warm (bless the recording, cede the authority), and the plain version of the line *is already in the line*. **Minimal fix:** end Earlene at "Keep the pen." Cut "Town's got plenty of chalk." One epigram per beat; the beat keeps the one that was asked for.

**F4 (minor) — ch11:92–95 — the manufactured feed.**
> Peanut looked at her one second longer.
> "Where'd you learn bar triage?"
> "Twelve thousand parents," Marisol said. "This is a slow night."

Cost: **flattened voice**, mildly — Peanut, mid-flood, pauses to turn straight man so her punchline can land; the narration ("looked at her one second longer") is the machinery showing. Against it: "Twelve thousand parents" is genuine biography and the exchange is only two lines. Ranked last; the honest options are (a) accept it, or (b) cut the feed and let "Two flats." → "Then tonight you run a canning operation. Go." close the beat, which is already a perfect pair. I'd only take (b) if F2's cut isn't taken — the chapter can afford one of these, not both plus the stack.

## Counterexamples (the law calibrating, not prosecuting)

- **ch11:391–403, Cal's walk offer.** "Truck's not lost. It was the company I was offering." Wit at full load: the line corrects her deflection *to keep the offer alive*. Every clever word is spent wanting something from the person across from him. This is the book's wit working exactly as licensed.
- **ch24:203–221, Verna in the Mule's doorway.** "Don't get up. I'm not coming in. I've seen the bathrooms." A licensed performer performing because performance is the *tool* — she wants the room disarmed so the verdict lands ("you don't trim a thing that fits"), and she builds "nowhere for an answer to land." Wit as advocacy. Also ch24:93, Travis's "County schools got Wednesday off, I heard" — small talk whose emptiness is the content; the radio-off narration frames it as men not-saying the thing. That's how zero-wit talk earns a place.
- **ch20:229–233, the pie at the tears.** "That story runs you a pie. I don't set the rates. Verna sets the rates." A quip at the sample's most exposed moment, and it's the opposite of varnish: she wants to hand him a way to stand back up, and the narration prices it exactly ("brisk as change counted at a register"). Bonus citation: ch17:208–211, Tunk's "the fiddle needed the rest" — a varnish-shaped quip that the *book itself prosecutes* ("Nobody laughed. The quiet stayed where it was."). Tunk wants to defuse; the room refuses; stakes hold. The scene already knows the law.

## Highest-leverage fix

If the author takes one proposal, take **F1**. F2 and F3 cost a little reader trust in a scene licensed for excess; F1 spends stakes the series brand (SC4) has promised never to spend — it's one cut line, zero new words, and it protects the best public confrontation in the book.

## Where the book sits, and the rule for the next wave

On the too-witty ↔ too-small-talkish axis, this book never touches the small-talk end: in all four scenes I found no line that merely transacts pleasantness without the emptiness itself being dramatized. The lean is entirely toward wit, and it is scene-typed: crisis scenes (ch24) and two-handers (ch20) run clean, because the wants are loud enough to discipline every line; the varnish concentrates in chorus scenes (ch11, edge of ch17), where the license is real but the quota gets exceeded — and specifically in the *reply* position. All three ranked findings are caps: a clever answer to a line that had already landed ("collectible" after "fill it to the handle"; "plenty of chalk" after "list and a mob"; "how tall is he" after "every nerve I own"). The rule of thumb I'd hand the next wave's drafters: **when a line lands, the next speaker's job is to want, not to match it — the first witty line in a beat belongs to the character; the second usually belongs to you. Cut yours.**

Verdict on the sample: **close** — genuinely close. Four proposals across four accepted chapters, three of them pure cuts, none structural; ch24 and ch20 need nothing. The author's ear caught something real, but it is localized to reply-position quips in chorus scenes, not a book-wide condition. Proposals only; nothing applied — these are shipped pages, and every change above waits for the author to name it.
