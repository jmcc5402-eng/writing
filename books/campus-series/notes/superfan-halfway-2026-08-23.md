**★★★★★ — "Grown folks, slow smoke, real feelings" — the devoted superfan**

FINALLY a romance where the heroine is 43, divorced, freckled on purpose, and the book treats her like the catch she is. Marisol runs twelve thousand parents on four hours of sleep and I would follow her into battle. Cal Sutter is a man who fixes a ballroom's ringing with two motions and says "It's quiet. Quiet's the sound" (ch 6) and I had to put the Kindle DOWN. The lines in this book, y'all: "Sugar, in this heat that's practically a courtship." "The second fall is also the worst, so you may as well eat" (Earlene, ch 7 — put it on a mug). "A pause is not a no." "You can't kneel on sod." The wrench that stays six weeks on the one clean rag in a working shop — when he finally hands over the right one in chapter 16 and says "Handing it over meant the errand was done," I cried on an airplane. And chapter 20 — the ledger, her handwriting going in on the line where his late wife's stops, "She's not paid up. She's winning" — that is the best grief scene I've read in this genre in years, and it never once got sad-book on me. The dog votes twice. The dog is always right. Preordering everything this town ever does.

**★★★★ — "I almost quit at chapter 4 and I'm so glad I didn't" — the skeptic**

Full honesty: chapters 1–4 nearly lost me. Chapter 1 spends a long time on RVs and hickory smoke before anybody flirts, and chapter 4 is two men discussing budget shortfalls at a fence. I do not read romance for budget shortfalls. Chapter 5 (the kneeling step, the photograph wall, "Second's too low. Fourth's showing off") is where the book grabbed my collar, and from the gala at table nine onward I was gone. My one real gripe, and it's the one keeping this from five stars: chapter 17 ends with her asking "Will you sign it?" live on the radio, eleven seconds of dead air, chapter break — and then chapter 18 SKIPS THE MOMENT. I stayed up to hear his answer and got a typed transcript on a corkboard that says [pause]. I have now decided [pause] is genius. At 12:40 a.m. I said a word this closed-door book would not print. Also, everyone in Ashford — the dentist, the feed man, the grandmother with the Pyrex measuring cup — is exactly this quotable, and around chapter 11 the bits start elbowing the couple for room. Trim two townsfolk monologues, keep everything else forever.

**★★★★★ — "Borrowed it at 9 p.m., finished the available chapters at 2" — the KU binge-reader**

The chapter 14–15–16 run is the best three-chapter stretch I've read in KU this year. The locked drawer. The badge going out at 9:41. The red-boxed screenshot detonating over a lawn of half-hung string lights. Her driving at the only lit door on campus — and then the shop scene, which does confrontation, forgiveness, full confession, the wrench, AND the kiss, and earns every one of them. "Don't," Marisol told the dog. "I'm furious." Ratchet stayed. I screamed. Closed door handled exactly right: the jacket hits the swept concrete and the chapter ends, and it's somehow hotter than half the open-door books in my library. Notes for the author: chapter 18 is the one place I checked how many pages were left (Cal alone with deadlines and a nameless engineer at a fence — the sleeping-dog photo at the end saved it), and I want it on record that the cake-plate hand touch at the church dinner (ch 13) did more for me than most books' whole third act. Auto-borrowing the sequel.

**★☆ — "Mis-shelved. This is a zoning dispute with a kiss" — the crank**

Bought this expecting a romance. What I got: three chapters about a football town, a chapter about a facilities budget, an hour of radio about a pontoon boat, and a hero and heroine who do not kiss until PAST THE HALFWAY MARK and then immediately agree to pretend they don't know each other. The heroine's big move is a spiral notebook. The hero's big move is not signing a piece of paper. # PROMISE-KEEPING AUDIT, READER EDITION

**What chapter 1 promised:** a town that's a character (delivered, almost to a fault); a 43-year-old woman deciding things "on purpose... and in her own favor" (delivered — the sawdust-in-the-mirror moment in ch 19 is that promise paid in full); a wrench-thief with good hands and a smile "like a door opening on a lit room and closing again" (delivered, and that exact image gets its payoff when she stares at the lit bay door in ch 15 — chef's kiss); the dusk "who knows" couples beat promising romance in every direction (delivered three times over — Birdie/Eli, Verna's history, the porch-kiss couple in ch 1). Closed-door promise: kept, and kept elegantly — the garment falls, the door closes, no one feels cheated in either direction. Nothing cheating-adjacent anywhere (divorced heroine with the papers signed, widowed hero — clean). The one honest-text problem (Cal sitting on the draft, ch 14) resolves in ONE chapter, in person, and she names the price out loud — "Silent, with me, you don't get to be again" — which is exactly how you do a secret without making readers throw the Kindle.

**The slow-burn tolerance check:** kiss at 16 of 30 is not stingy — it's near-perfect FOR THIS BUILD, because the ladder underneath it never stalls: glance (1), first laugh extracted (8), handshake that "cost him something" (8), coffee with no questions attached (9), thorn-holding (10), elbow at the step (11), the plate held between four hands at church (13). Every chapter puts one more coin in the jar. The wait is delicious the entire way up.

**Where the wait stops being delicious:** after the kiss, not before. The secrecy terms (16) are smart and we buy them, but chapters 17–21 give the couple who just kissed a total of one cross-lawn stare, one nine-second dog-photo text, one half-second coffee-cup finger graze, and two nights of measuring staircases. That's five chapters of a couple we've been PAID and can't spend. Chapters 19 and 20 survive it beautifully (the coffee-for-twelve reveal and the ledger are the two best quiet chapters in the book). Chapter 18 is the pinch — the one chapter where the reader can feel herself waiting. Public at 22 with the last dance, gate kiss at 23: that curve lands with maybe one chapter to spare. If anything between 17 and 21 gets longer, the three-star "I wanted to love this" reviews start writing themselves.

# THE SMILE QUESTION

We noticed. Of course we noticed — the book taught us to. "Smiling at a point somewhere past his ear" in chapter 1, again in 5, again in 10, and then chapter 8 made it text: the stranger dad gets the smile "straight on, dead level," and Cal realizes "every smile Cal had ever gotten off that woman had gone on past his ear, and he had not counted that a loss until right now." From that sentence forward, this reader is keeping score on every page. That's a promise now, and promises this well-trained have a due date.

**When we WANT it: chapter 22, the last dance, whole town watching.** Here's the reader logic: the withheld smile has always been a PUBLIC deflection — she smiles past his ear in front of name-tag lines, porches, cake tables. She's given him plenty in private (the laughs, the kiss, the ledger). What she's never given him is the public, straight-on, dead-level smile she hands strangers for free. So the debt is a public debt, and it comes due at the exact moment secrecy dies — when he crosses that porch in front of everyone and asks for the dance. That's the frame the whole town photographs. That's the screenshot. If chapter 22 ends and her smile went past his ear ONE more time, we will assume it's on purpose and grant you exactly one more chapter of credit.

**Chapter 23 (the gate kiss): acceptable, minus points.** A private smile pays a private debt, and this debt isn't private. It'd still land — "they're laughing before their foreheads even touch" is right next door to it — but the town-watching version is worth more, and we'd quietly feel the dance scene was missing its cap.

**Held to 28 or 30: now we're mad.** After they go public at 22–23, every subsequent smile-past-the-ear stops reading as Marisol's armor and starts reading as the author sitting on our payoff. By the Checkerboard scene in 25 it's conspicuous; by the podium in 28 it's a trick, and the reviews will say "the author was so proud of her withheld thing she forgot to give it to us while it still meant something." The third step in chapter 30 is the wrong place for the FIRST one — but it is the perfect place for the SECOND one. Land the smile at 22; let 30 echo it on the step where nobody has ever stood two at a time. First one for the town, second one for him alone. That's five stars twice.

# SERIES-FIRST-BOOK ALLOWANCE

**Seeded and welcome, even unpaid here:**
- **Birdie and Eli.** The two coffees he doesn't drink, "It's a cool-season grass," the fifty-yard-line slow circle in chapter 12, "The truck needed a fuel pump" — this is the best B-couple this panel has read in years, and the ch 29 payoff (asking her to the game out loud, "no fuel pump anywhere in the sentence") is exactly enough for Book 1. If Book 1.2 is not their book, there will be reviews about it. Loving, but firm.
- **Aisha and Coach Merritt.** "How tall is he?" / "I am not doing this." Two scenes, full engine, zero payment due. Perfect seeding.
- **Kat the realtor and the margarita boyfriend** — one interrupted story (25) plus the zipped folder she carries all morning before the listing-papers scene in 29, which by the way is going to wreck people in the best way. Sequel-bait done as friendship. Approved.
- **Kudzu Media LLC in eight-point type on the gala program's back page (ch 6)** — we caught it, we felt smart, and we're fine with the astroturf machine being a series villain as long as this book's House fight closes.
- **The kids.** The walk-on son twenty minutes and a locker room away, the Bellamy daughter, the empty folding chair at Mt. Nebo — a whole future shelf of books, planted without ever slowing this one down.

**The one seed that worries us:** chapter 28's last Boyd beat — moving the demolition survey up "before he is out of the room." We accept Boyd surviving; a series needs its weather. But if the FINAL image of the House fight is the villain scheduling its death, a loud minority of reviews will say "the house isn't saved, the ending is a coupon for book two," and this book has otherwise earned a clean landing (gift taken shy of Millrow, crew contracted through March, the trust standing up from the third row). Let Boyd loom in a scene the couple wins; don't let him have the last calendar. Chapters 29–30 as planned — the clipboard under glass, the fresh glass slide dated in HIS hand, the word said on the third step with nobody watching — are a finish worth the whole wait.

**What makes us one-click the next one the night we finish:** a Book 1.2 that opens on Birdie's truck; one page of Aisha and that coach in the back matter; and the promise that Ashford's clocks keep running — the griddle at five-forty, Verna's light at quarter past, the carillon that quits before showing off. We didn't fall for a plot. We fell for a town that knows what order things wake in. Buy-worthy now; preorder-worthy the day the dance chapter exists.
