# The review section, predicted — retail heat read

**Run 2026-09-14 · superfan-reviewer · Card C8 (read as a reader who
skips to the good parts) · Posture P2 (1.2 read as serialized)**
**Scope:** Book 1.1 ch 1–30 (complete) and Book 1.2 ch 1–19.
**Commissioned by:** the author, direct, in the heat-and-money thread.
**Question put to the instrument:** the author's own diagnosis —
"mostly plot books with occasional romance" — and whether the heat can

## 1. THE SKIP TEST

### Book 1.1 — 18 of 30 chapters pay a skipper (60% hit rate)

Genuinely high. This is a book you can skim and still get fed.

Jackpot chapters: 1, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 16, 20, 22, 23, 25, 27, 28,
29, 30. Solid: 13, 19, 21. Thin: 7, 14, 17, 24, 26. **Nothing: 2, 4,
12, 18.**

**Worst gap: chapters 2–4.** Three chapters, ~7,500 words. A Monday of
work orders, a market she watches him leave, a man admiring a fence.
Ch 2 has no heroine on the page at all. This is the same "drought"
finding filed 2026-08-19 and **it has not moved.**

**Second gap: 17–18**, immediately after the first kiss. One look
across a lawn, then a photo of a sleeping dog. "You paid me and then
you took my money back." Ch 19–20 rescue it inside ten pages — half a
star, not a star.

**The sample problem (ch 1–3): yes, and it is the wrong gap in the
wrong place.** Ch 1 is the best meet-cute on this shelf. Ch 2 has no
heroine. Ch 3 gives a man touching his hat at a door. The sample opens
with a firecracker, hands over two chapters of a competent man being
competent alone, and ends on a chrysanthemum on a step. It still
converts — ch 1 is that good — but at maybe 70% of what it should,
**and the woman who bounces bounces at ch 2, not ch 1, and never learns
ch 5 exists.**

### Book 1.2 — 9 of 19, and the real number is uglier

A different disease. In 1.1 the leads are often apart. In 1.2 they are
almost never apart — **and that is worse**, because the book keeps
showing the couple and keeps not paying.

> **Through ch 13 — roughly 40,000 words, nearly half the book — Aisha
> and Dan are never once alone in a room without a job between them.**
> A patient, a sheet of paper, a memo, a whiteboard, a tablet, a
> microphone, or a man cutting tape three feet away. Every single time.

The courtship is a form handed across a yard of December air, eight to
ten times, with an injury update on it. Electric the first time. By the
eighth the reader is **skipping the couple scenes** — a thing that
should never happen in a romance, and it happened, in ch 9–12.

Jackpot: 4 (small), 14, 15, 17, 18 (the best chapter for a skipper in
either book). Good: 2, 8, 13, 16. Thin: 5, 10, 11, 12, 19. Crumb: 3, 6.
**Zero: 1, 9.**

**Worst gap: chapters 5–9.** Five chapters. Ch 6 is ~150 lines of a
county hearing its name on television and then four lines about shoe
polish. Ch 9 is the heroine alone with a dead woman's letter — lovely,
and not what was bought.

**The 1.2 sample contains almost nothing else:**

> **Book 1.2's first chapter contains no interaction between hero and
> heroine whatsoever.** Not a word, not a touch. She stands at a
> first-aid table at one end of a square and admires his crowd
> management from the other end. First contact is 40% into ch 2, and it
> is an argument about a medical clearance form.

Measured honestly — "time until two people who want each other are
alone with time to spare and nothing to file" — **1.2's wade is
chapters 1 through 14**, arriving at ~70% of what is drafted. Read
serialized: fourteen weeks.

time in eighty pages the book tells me these two people are a

## 5. THE PROMISE QUESTION

**The closed-door promise is kept, and kept beautifully — both
directions.** Three garments hit three floors and the page closes every
time. No bait-and-switch scene at the 80% mark. **This is the promise
that generates actual one-stars and these books will not generate
them.** On the record.

**No pet-peeve landmines at all** — no cheating, no infidelity, no
miscommunication one honest text solves, nobody is an idiot, consent on
the page twice as good manners rather than a lecture, the dog present
and correct. "Genuinely: I have no pet-peeve findings on either book.
That is rare."

**"If a reader buys it for the couple, does she get the couple?"**

- **1.1: yes.** Every rung on the ladder is on the page; the ladder
  never stalls. She closes the book satisfied.
- **1.2: she gets the couple starting at chapter 14.** Before that, a
  workplace with two attractive people doing their jobs impeccably,
  near each other, forever — *the idea* of a couple, happening off the
  page in two people's heads.

> **The structural tell in 1.2: the romance is told mostly in private
> interiority, one lead at a time, at the ends of chapters.** She wants
> him — alone, ch 5. He wants her — alone, ch 8. She wants him again —
> alone, ch 9. **"A woman alone in a room deciding she wants a man is
> not a romance beat. It's a *report* of a romance beat. The beat is
> when he's standing there."**

### The metadata bright line

- If 1.2 is packaged identically to 1.1, **it takes a full star more in
  one- and two-star reviews**, because 1.1's sample has a meet-cute and
  1.2's has a concussion.
- 1.2's blurb cannot open on the protocol, the bracket, the letter, or
  the boy. "If the first line of the blurb has a quarterback in it, the
  wrong reader buys it and the right reader doesn't."
- "Steamy" stays off both. It is off both. Good.
- "College" in the blurb only, never the categories. The books hold
  this; keep holding it.
- **No box set with matched cover treatment until 1.2's front third
  gives a sampler the couple.**

## 6. THE HEAT QUESTION — WHAT IS ACTUALLY MISSING

> "Closed-door readers are not the people who couldn't handle the other
> thing. We are the people who think the wanting is better than the
> having, and we are *demanding* about the wanting… **Right now these
> books think closed door means shorter. It doesn't. It means longer,
> everywhere except the last room.**"

**(a) Nobody's body ever betrays them.** Three involuntary-body beats
in forty-nine chapters (1.1 ch 5, ch 13; 1.2 ch 17). "That's the engine
of the entire subgenre and it's running at idle."

**(b) The noticing is all forearms, shoulders, hands, jaw.** A mouth is
wanted once, in ch 1, never again. No throat, no waist, no back through
a shirt when someone reaches high, no collarbone, no where-the-sleeve-
is-rolled-and-the-skin-starts. Smell appears **once in 49 chapters**
(1.2 ch 11, the cold and the crumb rubber and the man warm all the way
through) and it is the best sensory beat in either book. "Everybody
smells like hickory and cut grass and nobody smells like a person."

**(c) The almost is always broken by the plot, never by a choice.**
Horn, headlights, carillon, Sonny, chainsaw, Ronnie, a cart, a TV that
turns itself on. "By the fourth one I hear it coming. The device is
*free* — nobody chose anything, so nothing cost anything." The single
exception is 1.1 ch 11: *"Any other fall, I'd let you."* — "a five-star
beat. It costs both of them. It is also the only one."

**(d) The kisses are summaries.** The emotional climax of thirty
chapters is one sentence: "He kissed her on the third step, her hands
coming up cold to his jaw and warming there." Ch 23's gate kiss is
"slow and sure, with nothing left to prove" — **"that's the review of
the kiss, not the kiss."** The one done right (1.1 ch 16, four
paragraphs) proves it can be. **Both books follow the leads' first kiss
immediately with a rules discussion.**

**(e) The approach is one sentence long.** Standard 9 promises "the
approach is fully on the page." It is, on average, one to three
sentences and then a garment. 1.1 ch 23: five words of approach, the
jacket lands, chapter break. "The books I'd compare to would spend
forty lines between 'the door shut behind them' and the jacket.
**Not one of those forty lines has to break the line.**"

**(f) Nobody is ever undone in public.** The genre's crown jewel. 1.1
has it exactly once — ch 22, Cal crossing twenty feet in front of the
county — "and it is the best scene in either book and every reviewer
will say so." 1.2 has zero through ch 19, and by design cannot have one
until February.

**(g)** The two hottest passages in the series are a side couple and
the one chapter where somebody clearly set out to write heat.

## 7. THE BOLTED-ON WARNING — eleven tells

What would give a retrofit away, in the reviewer's own words:

1. **A heat chapter.** "The tell is prose length: the sentences in the
   added passage will be twice as long as the sentences around them."
   1.2 ch 17 already has a mild version. "In a full retrofit that seam
   is everywhere and I will say the word 'uneven.'"
2. **Heat that doesn't change the scene it's in.** "The wanting has to
   make one of them worse at their job for one beat — a number read
   twice, a name said wrong, a hand that shakes over a form. If the
   added desire has no consequence inside its own scene, it is a note
   to the reader and I will smell it."
3. **Retroactive noticing.** "Any body part added in a pass has to be
   planted in the meet. If ch 12 suddenly has a throat in it, ch 2
   needs a throat in it."
4. **One move, used everywhere.** Both books already lean on
   heat-through-two-coats; doubling it makes it a tic.
5. **The word "heat."** Already over-used. "A retrofit will double it
   and it will read like a thermostat, not a body."
6. **Chapter-ending want paragraphs.** 1.2 already has three. "**The
   single most obvious retrofit shape in this genre.** It is where the
   fix goes because it's the easiest place to put it, and that is
   exactly why we spot it."
7. **Generic desire inside a specific book.** "The rye is a cool-season
   grass. The cam lock doesn't torque. If the added wanting is
   furnished out of stock parts — a racing pulse, a heat pooling — the
   review will say *the town is written with more love than the
   couple*."
8. **More garments. THE BIG ONE.** "If the fix is more things hitting
   more floors, that is not more heat, that is more endings… **The
   forty lines you're missing are *before* the garment, and the garment
   line doesn't have to move an inch to get them.**"
9. **The town telling me it's hot.** "**If the chorus has to tell me
   it's hot, it isn't.**" Any more and reviews say "everyone in this
   town is a comedian about these two and nobody is actually in love."
10. **Fixing 1.2's front third by adding heat to the front third.**
    "The front third's problem is not temperature, it is **format**…
    a longing paragraph on top of a form handoff is still a form
    handoff. Then the review is *it got hornier but it didn't get
    better*, which is the worst review a revision can earn."
11. **Do not tune 1.1 to match.** "Book 1.1 works." If touched at all:
    **ch 2–4 need *presence*, not heat**, and ch 23 and 30 have
    four-word climaxes that could take room. "That's the whole list."

## 8. RANKED GRIPES (by stars cost)

1. **1.2: thirteen chapters before the couple is alone without a job
   between them** — a full star from ~40% of buyers.
2. **1.2: the Friday medical check IS the courtship, eight times** —
   half a star from everyone, a full star from the format-sensitive.
3. **1.1: the drought at ch 2–4, inside the Kindle sample** — a full
   star from a quarter of samplers, who never become buyers at all.
4. **The almost always broken by outside noise** — half a star,
   cumulative.
5. **The highest touch of a chapter arrives in one sentence** — half a
   star, "and it's the half that keeps the fives from being 'I couldn't
   put it down' fives."
6. **1.2: the side couple out-burns the leads** — quarter star, loud.
7. **The secrecy-terms structure repeats book to book** — quarter star
   now, **full star on book three**; costs preorders rather than stars.
8. **1.2's screenshot economy is town-only through ch 13** — quarter
   star. "What gets posted determines what gets bought."
9. **1.1: two chapters of drought immediately after the first kiss** —
   quarter star.
10. **Nobody undone in public except once** — a missed star *gain*.
11. **Every body beat is immediately filed by the mind** — not a star
    cost, a **ceiling**. "elected to blame the smoke," "declined to
    itemize," "declined to examine." Individually charming; fifty times
    it is a thermostat, "and it is the reason the wanting in these
    books is *admired* and not *felt*."

**On the record, in the couple's favour:** the trust contract is
airtight. Whatever changes, none of that should.

## 9. VERDICT

- **Book 1.1: buy-worthy, edging on preorder-worthy.** "A woman who
  quits at chapter 2 never finds out about the wrench on the clean rag,
  and that is a tragedy that happens in the sample, not in the book."
- **Book 1.2 as it stands: borrow-worthy** — an explicit demotion. "I'd
  read it on KU, I'd finish it, I'd love ch 14–18 with my whole chest,
  and I would not have paid money for it, and I would not preorder the
  next one." Not cold — **slow to be a romance**, "and the difference
  is that a cold book I'd quit and a slow book I'd finish and then be a
  little sad about."

> **"The author is not missing the ability. He's missing the length."**
>
> "He can do this. He does it in one sentence, once, and then he goes
> and checks a gutter. **The garment line isn't the problem. The
> garment line is the least of it. Everything I want is above the
> garment line and there's nothing stopping any of it.** I'd read three
> more of these. I want one of them to slow down."
