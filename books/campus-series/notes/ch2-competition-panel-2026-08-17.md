WHAT WE ARE READING: finished first-draft PROSE — three blind candidates
(A, B, C) for chapter two. We judge the pages, not the plan; drafter and
card identities unknown to us beyond the letters on the files. This is a
Cal POV chapter: no Marisol, no heat, and the book's held secret rides in
it. The winner must continue the accepted chapter one so invisibly that a
reader never feels the hand-off.

CALIBRATION (from the book's own docs, not assumed):
- Target reader: 35–45, one chapter in and deciding whether to stay up.
- Register: Gilmore-light — "a laugh lives near every ache"; the FUN
  meter is "the primary gauge of this ruling" (register note).
- Modern-not-2026 texture (register note, modern calibration).
- Cal reads WANTED — Redford-golden, "funnier than he lets on"; hair
  canon light brown, slightest gray (brief §Register). Note: all three
  candidates are inside Cal's head and none describes his own hair —
  correct instinct in his POV; no candidate violates or needed it. His
  wanted-ness must arrive by watching him work, and we scored it there.
- The secret: the Hartwell round "tells the READER what no one in town
  knows... WHY is not revealed, not hinted at in words, not named"
  (brief §The shape). This is the axis the chapter exists for.
- No heat beats; his want is quiet and his Monday is full.

THE PANEL: (P1) buys four books a month, KU, genre-fluent; (P2) reads at
10 p.m., falls asleep mid-scene; (P3) DNFs at 15% without guilt; (P4)

CANDIDATE A — "By Ear" (card D1, sound-forward)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

(a) Wanted, watching him work. The purest competence swoon of the three:
"'Lifter,' he called across the yard. 'Left bank. Quit staring at her.'"
followed by "the particular silence of men discovering he was right,
which was a sound too, and one of his favorites." P1: "That's the beat.
He didn't even leave the doorway. I'd follow this man around a hardware
store." The listening conceit makes his competence a sense, not a
résumé — he diagnoses the new hire by throttle ("He'd cure that by
Friday") and the ice machine by its labor. Redford-golden by behavior:
unhurried, certain, kind to machines.

(b) Fun. Steady and dry rather than loud: "a posture that has never in
recorded history fixed an engine"; "The semester has landed, and now it
itemizes"; "a mini-fridge abandoned in a stairwell like a foundling" /
"the fridge is a rescue, not an orphan"; the meeting attendance record
("you would be carrying somebody the last forty feet"). And crucially A
keeps the Gilmore bill paid INSIDE the secret round — the boiler
"banging away down there in the dark with all the sincerity of stage
thunder" and "'You old fraud. Frightening people in August.'" P2: "I
smiled the whole way through and laughed out loud maybe three times. B
got more laughs out of me. But I never once felt the chapter get heavy,
even in the dark house, which — how?" Editor translation: A's humor
count is a shade under B's and C's; the gap is graft-sized (see GRAFTS).

(c) The secret. Best in show, and it is not close. The discipline is
structural: the House round rhymes with the motor-pool morning —
"because a building reports too, if you give it the chance" — so the
devastation arrives by inference, never narration. The routine tells us
everything without a word of why: "He turned the radio off at the top of
the street. That was the routine." The staircase beat is the cleanest of
the three: "its creak was the one sound in the building he never asked
for, and long habit carried his boot past it clean" — and the climb,
eyes on the landing, "a man who had made this particular climb so many
times he could do it without seeing anything at all." No stating, no
hinting, no narrator's flashlight. And the stomach-drop lands the
outline's signature move verbatim-faithful ("His stomach dropped at the
same line the second time too") and then gets the run's single best
witness beat: "Across the office, Ratchet lifted her head. She could
hear a man go quiet from forty yards." P4: "That's the whole chapter in
two lines. The dog notices what the town doesn't. I put the book down —
in the good way." P3, on the round: "I don't know why he does this and I
would riot if you told me now. That's the first time a book has made me
grateful to be kept out."

(d) Voice-match. Strong — ch. 1's personification and inventory music
continued through a NEW device (sound) rather than reused images: "the
roll door going up with its long complaint," "somebody dropping a socket
and drawing applause for it." One echo debit: the ending re-runs the
ch. 1 closing kit (radio up, drive home through the town's noise) —
milder than B's version of the same echo, but present. The chapter ends
in-chapter, no trailer voice: "He had wanted a quiet one. He would
settle for a running one."

(e) Skim ledger / DNF. P3's one complaint, quoted: "The sprinklers
ticked their way around the quad, on schedule" — "I was twenty-five
lines in before anybody talked. At ten p.m. I'd have trusted you; at a
bookstore sample I might not." Editor translation: the cold open is
ambient machinery for a beat too long; compress, don't cut — the
leaf-blower joke is the wanted-ness thesis and must survive. One brief
process patch: "There was no work order for it, no account code, no line
in any budget" — exposition, but three lines and load-bearing. No DNF
point; confident, because every ambient run in A pays a joke or a
diagnosis within a few lines, and the back half is the strongest back
half on the table.

(f) Compliance. No named students; students exist as forty-one work
orders and a smell "described four different ways, all of them with
feeling" — pass. No years. College words: dorm work orders, parents'
weekend — two, salted, pass. Wrench present, return not staged — pass,
though A's wrench beat is the most inert of the three ("He hadn't gotten
around to it"). Banned moves: none; noted for the record that A twice
uses a "the same as X..." comparison ("a cousin's boat sits on your
trailer hitch"), the legal cousin of the banned scaffold — fine here,
watch the habit. One fact flag: [CHECK: "had thrown a party for a
hundred thousand people Saturday" — the manifest stadium seats ninety
thousand; plausible with tailgaters, but clear or conform it]. The
Hartwell key "lived on Cal's own ring, down at the end" — invented
detail, legal, but see GRAFTS: B's version of this fact is safer for the
secret.

CANDIDATE B — "The Man Who Keeps It Running" (card D3)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

(a) Wanted, watching him work. Delivered, in B's signature register of
economy: "'Seat switch,' he said, and kept walking. ... Revisions were
being made." The rim swap ("They were not out of stock. Cal knew what
was on his own shelves") is quiet-command catnip. P2's favorite exchange
of the whole run: "'We don't have a swing set,' Cal said. / 'They made
one.'" — and his reason for taking the ticket is Cal in one line: "and
besides, he wanted to see it."

(b) Fun. Highest laugh count of the three, and most of it load-bearing:
the pressure washer nobody mentions "in case it heard"; Ratchet's
grading economy ("issued him his grade, which was a B. Cal had made his
peace with the B. The A was reserved."); the air handler "described, in
writing, as haunted"; the swap-and-shop ending, where a man with a stock
trailer and a woman who wanted one find each other on air and Cal turns
it up — "He liked this part." P1: "The radio matchmaking is the romance
promise kept in a no-romance chapter. That's clever in the way I want
this series to be clever." One calibration flag, though: the foil-dish
beat ("The town had decided, a while back, that a man on his own could
not be trusted to eat") is charming AND it is the exact texture the
modern calibration ruled out by name — the author asked for "a DoorDash
that gets delayed instead of a casserole in the oven," and B has both
the delivery-app beat and the casserole. Editor translation: the beat's
warmth is real; its furniture is off-ruling. Fix: keep the sentiment,
modernize the vehicle, or cut — the chapter doesn't lean on it.

(c) The secret. Very good, with two soft spots. The best single
grief-texture beat of the run lives here: "afterward he wiped the water
off the porcelain, and then he wiped it again." The separate key is the
secret's grammar done right: "That key rode by itself in his shirt
pocket, on a plain steel ring with nothing else, and it did not get
explained to anyone, because no one had ever thought to ask." But the
staircase gets one underline too many — "He did not look at them. He was
good at not looking at them. He had had practice." P4: "Two beats where
one would kill me. The third sentence is the author tapping the glass."
It still never states or hints WHY — the wall holds. The bigger miss is
the block beat: the ratified outline specifies "his stomach drops on the
same line both times," and A and C both execute the drop-and-repeat; B
renders it as "The last line said Hartwell House. / He sat still." with
a reread "like a man rechecking a torque he already knew." The shape is
present; the outline's signature repetition is not. P1: "'He sat still'
is good. It's just not the beat I was promised, and the promised beat is
better."

(d) Voice-match. In the comic-inventory mode, B is the most invisible
continuation on the table — "Trash cans filled to legend. One shoe on
the library steps. A folding chair in a tree, and nobody asking how." is
chapter one's exact music with none of its images. Debit: the ending
re-stages ch. 1's closing kit almost piece for piece — windows down,
radio up, the drive home through the warm dark — and the brief says
match the rhythm, not the images. The swap-and-shop content inside it is
new and lovely; the staging is a rerun.

(e) Skim ledger / DNF. B reads longest and its midmorning catalog is
where the tired reader thins out. Quoted: "A loading dock gate was down
again across campus." — P2: "That's the sentence where I noticed I was
reading a list. The app-on-the-fence joke woke me back up." The
casserole paragraph (nine lines, no want in it) and the key-ring
meditation ("It weighed most of a pound and rode his left hip") are the
other two coasting patches. No DNF point. [CHECK: B appears to sit at or
past the 2,300-word ceiling — count before acceptance.]

(f) Compliance. Walls kept: no named students, no years; the ticket
woman and crew are unnamed adults. "He had declined it once already...
on the principle that he had never accepted it" squares with the brief.
College words: the bursar, dorm work orders — pass. Wrench: "once,
passing, he squared it up with the edge of the bench. That was all." —
the subtlest touch-tell of the three, pass. Invented texture ("Third one
this month") is furniture-grade. Canon note for the author: B takes
Ratchet TO Hartwell ("She did that some evenings. She seemed to know
which ones") — sweet, and it directly contradicts C's arrangement;
whichever chapter is accepted sets this canon (see RANKING).

CANDIDATE C — "Deferred Maintenance" (card D4)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

(a) Wanted, watching him work. The best Cal DIALOGUE of the run — he is
funnier than he lets on out loud, which is the canon exactly: "'Living
in them,' Cal said."; "thirty-nine of the forty will be the thermostat,
and the thermostat will be fine"; and the press-box call, the single
most quotable competence beat of the three candidates: "'Door's not
stuck, it's proud,' Cal said. 'Lift on the handle, then push. Don't
shoulder it. You'll bruise yourself and embarrass the door.'" P1:
"Screenshot. That's a man I'd invent a broken door for." The bay-two
trailer set piece is the run's best ROOM — "On a bad day they told God
things about that pillar that God presumably wrote down" — and Cal's
generosity in it is its own wanted-ness: "'Don't teach the others. I
need something to hold over them.'" One flag: the new kid speaks and
takes the spotlight; he is crew, not a student, and the sale-barn-at-
fourteen memory reads him adult, but — [CHECK: make it impossible to
mistake the "new kid" for a student worker; one word ("full-time,"
an age) closes it].

(b) Fun. The biggest individual laughs of the run: the WDSS lost-and-
found ("One set of upper dentures... described on air, with dignity, as
gently used"); the invite that "had accepted itself, and there it sat on
his calendar like it paid rent"; the squirrel "with real estate
ambitions" and its "whole acorn operation." But the meter fails where it
matters most: the final sixth of the chapter is laugh-free and gets
progressively solemn — from "None of it was on a work order" to the end,
there is not one joke, and the secret becomes the closing weather. That
is the Gilmore ruling inverted: grief may be a chord, "never the
chapter's weather," and C ends inside the weather. P2: "I loved this one
hardest in the middle and closed it feeling a little sad, and I don't
think this book wants me going to sleep sad."

(c) The secret. The round itself is disciplined and carries the two most
devastating quiet lines after A's dog beat: "a boiler you only visit in
October is a boiler that quits on you in November, and this one was not
allowed to quit" — and "Ratchet walked him to his truck and did not
offer to come along, which was their arrangement." No why, anywhere; the
wall holds. Two debits. First, the acceptance beat is semaphored: "if
anybody who knew him had been watching, they'd have known on the spot
that something was wrong" — the narrator pointing at the secret with
both hands. It never says why, so it's legal; it's just loud. P4: "A
trusts me to notice. C makes sure I noticed." Second, the secrecy-
mechanics paragraph ("The university did not know he came here. The town
did not know he came here.") explains at length, right after the
staircase, what A implies in three lines — it steps on its own hush.
The stomach-drop, for the record, is verbatim-faithful and well made
("as if the first pass might have been a filing error").

(d) Voice-match. The banter is fully in-voice; the openings are not —
C leads with thesis ("A quiet season, in his experience, was not a
season where nothing went wrong"), and chapter one never explains
itself before it performs. The closer — "one house on Millrow Street
locked a little better than that" — lands in-chapter, so it clears the
trailer ban, but it is button-flavored, and it is the fourth solemn
beat in a row.

(e) Skim ledger / DNF. The bay-two geometry runs eight lines of specs
before the kid appears ("with a hand's width of grace on either side") —
P2 skimmed and was rewarded anyway. The secrecy paragraph, quoted above:
P2 skimmed it outright ("I already understood all of that from the key
in the ashtray"). No DNF point, but P3's closest approach of the whole
run is here: "Somewhere around 'No folder in the facilities office had
ever heard of this address' I checked how many pages were left. First
time all chapter." That is the register finding wearing a reader's
face.

(f) Compliance. Walls kept: no named students (pending the new-kid
[CHECK]), no years, faceless development office, wrench present with the
run's best wanting beat — a note under it "in his own handwriting that
said RETURN. It had said RETURN since Saturday night." — intent on the
page, return unstaged, pass. College words: syllabus week, dorm work
orders — pass. Key in the ashtray, "no list anywhere said what it
opened" — strong and legal.

WANTING (the no-heat chapter's romance thread: the wrench)

The brief bars heat and Marisol, so the reader's wanting has one object
to ride: the confiscated wrench. C carries it best — the RETURN note is
Cal thinking about Saturday without the narrator saying so, denial-on-

RANKING
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

**A > B > C.** Split: P1 and P4 for A outright; P3 for A ("my skims were
in B's catalog and C's ending; A's slow open was my only note"); P2 for
B, conceding to A when the three Hartwell rounds were read side by side
("A's is the only one that made the hair on my arms stand up without a
single sad sentence").

Deciding reasons:

1. **This chapter exists to land the held secret, and A lands it
   cleanest and hardest.** Inference, never narration: the radio off at
   the top of the street, the boot past the third step "clean," and a
   dog who "could hear a man go quiet from forty yards." C narrates its
   secret's significance; B underlines its staircase once too often and
   softens the outline's mandated stomach-drop. A executes the law of
   the chapter to the letter and makes it the best moment on the table.
2. **A stays FUN inside the ache.** The author's primary gauge: B wins
   raw laugh count and C wins loudest single laughs, but A is the only
   candidate that never goes laugh-dark — the boiler gets called "an old
   fraud" in the same cellar where the secret lives. C pays its Gilmore
   bill late and ends in the weather; that is a register finding, not a
   style preference.
3. **A continues chapter one by method, not by images.** The sound
   conceit is a new device doing what ch. 1's object-and-inventory music
   did — invisible continuation. B, the closest voice-twin in its comic
   inventories, spends that credit by re-staging ch. 1's exact ending
   kit and re-seating the casserole the modern calibration removed by
   name.
4. **A's debits are the smallest on the table** — one crowd-size
   [CHECK], a compressible cold open, an inert wrench beat — and every
   one is a trim or a graft. B's debits touch two author rulings and the
   outline's signature beat; C's touch the register itself.

Safety and superconcepts (rule 1: reported, deferred): no safety-wall
violation in any candidate; no heat anywhere, per the brief. [CHECK: the
series SUPERCONCEPTS file was not in this run's permitted reads — the
superconcept audit belongs to the editorial pass, not this panel.] Our
ranking is pleasure only; it authorizes nothing.

Canon decisions this acceptance forces (record in the bible per repo
rule 7, don't leave them to memory): (1) where the Hartwell key lives —
A says on his main ring, B says a bare ring in his shirt pocket, C says
the ashtray; the panel recommends B's version by graft, below; (2)
whether Ratchet ever comes to the House — B says some evenings, C says
never by arrangement, A leaves her minding the gate; the accepted text
sets this; (3) Cal's second-in-command — A's unnamed woman, B's ticket
woman, C's male crew chief.

GRAFTS INTO THE WINNER (A)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

From B — the priority graft, it hardens the secret:
1. **The separate key**, near-verbatim: "That key rode by itself in his
   shirt pocket, on a plain steel ring with nothing else, and it did not
   get explained to anyone, because no one had ever thought to ask." —
   replacing A's "The key to it lived on Cal's own ring, down at the end,
   past all the university brass." A key ring the whole campus knows,
   with one unexplained key on it, is a question waiting to be asked;
   the pocket key is the secret's grammar done right, and it settles
   canon decision (1) in the same stroke.
2. **The swap-and-shop ending** — the stock-trailer man and the woman
   who wanted one, "listening to the two of them find each other" — into
   A's final drive, replacing "the night show still taking calls about
   Saturday" (which repeats A's own morning radio beat AND ch. 1's
   ending staging). It gives the no-romance chapter its romance mirror
   on the last page.

From C — fun and wanting, A's two thin spots:
3. **The RETURN note** under the wrench, replacing "He hadn't gotten
   around to it. It sat there keeping its own counsel" — the chapter's
   only forward-wanting beat, return still unstaged, brief still kept.
4. **The press-box door call** ("Door's not stuck, it's proud... You'll
   bruise yourself and embarrass the door.") — one Cal-voiced laugh into
   A's "The rest of Monday behaved itself" run, which is where A's fun
   meter dips. Zero structural cost.
5. Optional, only if the cousin's-boat line is ever cut: "It had
   accepted itself, and there it sat on his calendar like it paid
   rent." Take one of the two, never both.
