CANDIDATE A — "The Right Number" (declares card D7)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

(a) FUN. The funniest read on the table, and it opens mid-laugh: the jerky
"already out of its wrapper and being refused" before Cal even enters, the
PR man holding it "with the encouraging face people use on toddlers and
juries." The PR man is the run's comic peak — "'These really sing at golden
hour.' / 'It's ten in the morning,' said Cal. / 'It will not always be,'
said the sneakers, undefeated." P2: "I laughed out loud at 'undefeated' and
again at 'possibly about Cal's cortisol.' I was never bored once." Ratchet's
two verdicts bracket the scene perfectly — the opening non-refusal refusal,
and the exit: "Ratchet let him commit fully to the position, held it just
long enough to establish the record, and then looked at the middle distance.
Cal decided she ate free for life." P1: "The dog is the scoreboard and the
scoreboard is hilarious. This is how you sneak a budget meeting past me."
Bonus: the PR man delivers Cal's Redford canon as a joke — "That gray at
the temples. Very trust-building. People pay for that." — description
canon landed without a mirror.

(b) The vise. Lands in one pass, but in one BREATH — Boyd's trust speech
runs fifteen unbroken lines ("here's the thing about trusts... They need
cover, and cover is a professional finding... Unsalvageable"). P3: "I felt
my eyes start to slide around 'They'd be run out of church by Sunday' —
'I've eaten pie on that porch' pulled me back, but that's the one place in
A where I skimmed." Editor translation: content is right, delivery is a
monologue; the reader receives the vise as a speech rather than a squeeze.
Fix: break it across two Cal interjections. What A does better than anyone
is the MENACE floor under it: Boyd says the shortfall figure aloud,
unprompted — "it was the right number... the one that lived in the drawer
that locked, and hearing it out loud in the open air of his own yard was
worse than any threat Boyd could have designed." P4: "That's the scariest
sentence in any candidate and it contains zero numbers. Chef's kiss for
compliance, too."

(c) Boyd. Never stupid, genuinely tempting. "It was, word for word, a thing
Cal believed, and he resented hearing it arrive in someone else's mouth" is
exactly the villain rule executed. "The worst part was the kindness. The
second-worst part was that none of it was a lie." And the exit adds a
second sting past the mandated line: "Don't pay twelve mortgages for a
porch. November." P1: "Great line — but it steps on 'deferred maintenance
with a porch.' The brief's line is the business card; A puts a second card
on top of it." Fix if A advances: end Boyd on the mandated line, move the
mortgages line earlier.

(d) Cal's want and the wrench. Half a miss. The Boyd-off-the-yard want is
dramatized well (the fuel island, "which had sun, no shade, no chairs, and
acoustics that carried nowhere") — but the wrench does not exist in this
chapter until line ~281. P1: "I read an entire chapter of my romance novel
before anyone reminded me it was a romance novel. The button is lovely —
'declined to examine any part of that' — but the brief opens FROM wanting
and A banks all the wanting at the till." Also a logic wobble, quoted: the
fuel island's acoustics "carried nowhere," yet afterward the radio's volume
"meant they'd heard every word." P4: "Did the walk work or not? Pick one."
The heard-it-all version buys A's best ache — the coffee "set at his elbow
on the way past without comment, which in that building was a casserole at
the door. He drank it. It was terrible. It helped." — but it quietly
converts Cal's stated want into a loss without ever noticing it did.

(e) Voice-match. Strong on joke rhythm and object work; the button
gesture — "He turned it over once in his hand" — is a near-verbatim echo of
ch. 1's "turned it over once in his hand like a judge with evidence."
P4 loved it as a deliberate rhyme (his hands repeating the theft in
reverse); the brief says "do not reuse its specific images."
[CHECK: echo vs. reuse — author's call; the panel reports the pleasure and
defers.] Ending temperature: "She stayed where she was, and so, for a
while, did he" is the heaviest close of the three — legal under Gilmore
because "You're a poor judge" sits two lines above it, but it's the
outer edge of the ruling.

(f) Skim / put-down. One skim, named above (the trust monologue). No DNF
point; the panel is confident because no dip in A runs longer than that
single speech, and the dog re-enters within a page of every dip.

(g) Compliance. No Gateway, no Marisol in Boyd's mouth, no named students,
no year numbers, figure unstated, "twelve" as sanctioned. Debits:
"two visiting deans" is off the college-words list [CHECK]; Ratchet is
"she" here and "he" in B and C — the manifest is silent
[TK: Ratchet's sex unratified; whichever candidate is accepted sets it].

CANDIDATE B — "Gravel Is Honest" (declares card D1, sound-forward)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

(a) FUN. High, and every laugh is load-bearing. The eavesdropping crew is
the best-executed of the three because it's played entirely in audio: "the
shop had begun to go quiet in stages... The grinder died mid-note, which no
grinder does naturally. Twelve men can produce an enormous quantity of
listening, and Cal could hear every cubic foot of it." P2: "'It's a great
fence' is the hardest I laughed in any candidate — Cal deadpanning a
security maneuver." The PR man works ("It sounded like a creek full of
chalk"; the exit "finger guns at Ratchet. 'Great dog. Great energy.'
Slosh.") but has no peak laugh — his tablet gets put away before he's
allowed to be fully ridiculous. That's B's one fun deficit, and it's
graft-sized. Ratchet's verdict is the classiest of the three: "rendered his
verdict by making no sound at all," and Cal's cover for him — "He's on the
clock" — is Cal-funnier-than-he-lets-on, executed.

(b) The vise. The clearest FELT delivery of the three: broken into short
turns Cal keeps answering ("I know what my signature's on"), so the reader
assembles it herself — "'Unsalvageable,' in the survey, over the facilities
director's name, is cover. The trust sells with a clean conscience, the
gift lands, the trustees ratify in November, and twelve names never get
typed." All four personas played it back accurately in one read. Then B
loses its nerve, quoted: "And that was the vise, right there, hand-delivered
with a smile." P4: "You built it perfectly and then labeled it. That's the
one clunk I'd quote in a review." Editor translation: the narrator explains
a squeeze the reader already feels — the register note's "felt, never read"
line, violated for four lines. Fix: cut the label, keep at most "the pen,
either way, was his."

(c) Boyd. The cleanest not-quite-a-lie of the run: "Walk it with your own
eyes and your own flashlight, top plate to footing, and then tell me the
word's wrong... If the word's wrong, don't write it." P1: "That's the
temptation. He's not buying Cal's dishonesty, he's renting Cal's honesty."
"He said it kindly, and the kindness was the bad part" and "nobody ever
bought a jersey with a boiler on it" both landed with all four. "Put it
away," said without turning his head, characterizes Boyd's competence in
four words. Never cartoonish; the coasting risk is the opposite — his
shop-admiration inventory ("the lift, the welder, the parts wall sorted to
the bolt") runs a beat long. P2: "I drifted for three lines there."

(d) Cal's want and the wrench. This is where B wins the chapter. The wrench
arrives on the first page, staged as the not-examining made visible: "At
the left end of the bench, on a clean rag with a whole shop's worth of
nothing else on it, sat a crescent wrench that did not belong to him...
and every Monday the wrench stayed on the rag, and the rag stayed clean,
and Cal declined to think about either of those facts, on the grounds that
a man's bench was his own business." P1: "He keeps her wrench ON A CLEAN
RAG. I screenshotted it. In the one chapter she isn't in, that rag is the
whole romance and it's ENOUGH." The want is denied on purpose — his own
purpose, unexamined — which is exactly the brief. The button pays it with
a double meaning nobody else found: "'Not one word,' he told the dog" —
one word to the dog about the wrench, one word Boyd wants on a survey.
P4: "Whether that rhyme was on purpose or not, protect it." The
Boyd-off-the-yard want also lands cleanest here: the crew never gets the
numbers, and the cover story is a one-word chapter thesis — "'Boosters,'
Cal said, which answered everything, and the shop accepted it and sang on."

(e) Voice-match. Closest to invisible. Ch. 1 opens with a town waking as
one organism; B opens with the shop as one organism ("on a Monday morning
it sang. Not prettily. But it sang.") — same music, new key, no reused
image. "Money is quiet... But gravel is honest" and "boots that had heard
about mud and wished it well" are new coinages in the ch. 1 register.
P4: "If you told me the ch. 1 author wrote this the same week, I'd
believe you. That's the assignment."

(f) Skim / put-down. Two coasting patches, both quoted above: the opening
machinery stanza before Cal appears (P2: "at 10 p.m. I'd have drifted for
the first ten lines — the wrench-on-the-rag is what stopped me") and Boyd's
shop inventory. The vise-label paragraph is a skim for P3 ("you told me
what I just read"). No DNF point; the panel is confident because the
chapter's quietest stretch still has the wrench or the dog in frame.

(g) Compliance. No Gateway, no Marisol in Boyd's mouth, no students, no
years, manifest otherwise kept. Debits: "Twelve men" (twice) genders a
crew the manifest leaves as "twelve" [CHECK]; "new roofs on the row barns"
is invented facilities furniture [CHECK: harmless, but not on the
manifest]; Ratchet "he" (see the run-wide [TK] above).

CANDIDATE C — "Practical Men" (declares card D3)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

(a) FUN. Real laughs, drier delivery. The mower-deck argument that "had
developed positions, factions, and a dissenting wing" is a world-class
chorus joke, and closing the chapter by going "out to the bays to lose the
argument about the mower deck" is quietly the warmest ending of the three
— he lets his crew win, and P4 caught it: "That's love, filed as
management." The PR man's "'Grass-fed,' he told the dog. 'Zero sugar. Zero
guilt'" and "'It's a lifestyle,' he explained, to nobody who had asked"
both landed. Ratchet gets the run's best dog LOGIC — "Ratchet had views
about visitors who showed up with the treat already in hand. It meant they
had planned on being liked." — even if A gets the best dog COMEDY. Overall
temperature: P2: "C is the one I respected. B is the one I'd read again
tomorrow night."

(b) The vise. The best-ARGUED of the three: "a trust can't sell the town's
porch... without cover. Serious people need cover to make a hard call,"
paid off with the sharpest line on the mechanism anywhere in the run —
"It lets everybody else stop pretending they're the ones deciding." And C
is the only candidate that makes the vise physical instead of verbal:
"Would you hold a safety meeting on it? Put your whole crew on those
boards, all twelve, and have them jump?" P1: "That's the moment I believed
a good man signs. He's not weighing money against a house, he's weighing
the house against people he loves." One debit: Boyd's budget preamble
("Utilities are up. Enrollment's flat... somebody upstairs patches it with
prayer") is the closest any candidate drifts toward the register note's
banned real-estate-attorney weather — rescued by "nobody upstairs has ever
once paid for the gum," but P3 named it: "I skimmed 'Utilities are up.
Enrollment's flat.' Two lines of a budget book is two lines too many."

(c) Boyd. The most tempting Boyd, and the never-stupid rule gets its
cheapest, best proof in the run: "'Hup — not there,' Boyd said, and steered
him around it without breaking stride. 'That one's reserved.' He had read
the stencil. He had honored it. Cal held that against him immediately."
P4: "A villain who respects the dog's parking spot is a villain this town
could fall for. That beat should survive no matter who wins." Also: "He let
it sit one beat. Not two. He was good."; "The money's sitting there with
its coat on, waiting on one word."; and the closing parry — "'Not one thing
you just said is wrong,' Boyd said, pleasant. 'And none of it moves the
math.'" The brief's "favor between practical men" lands verbatim and
earns its place.

(d) Cal's want and the wrench. Want is the most explicit of the three —
"the goal was Boyd off this gravel before any numbers got airborne" — and
the wrench beat up top is genuinely funny-tender: "Returning it was a
five-minute errand. He had meant to run that errand maybe fourteen times.
He had not run it. There would be a reason, and he kept a strict policy of
not going looking for it." The button adds C's signature object: the pen —
"He looked at it and put it back. It had been just a pen at eight o'clock."
P4: "Best single object beat in the run." The wrench close itself
("he stood there holding it a good deal longer than a man stands holding a
wrench") is good but third-best of the three buttons.

(e) Voice-match. The most deadpan of the three — closer to Cal's speech
than to the book's narration. Ch. 1 narrates warm and quick; C narrates
dry and precise ("He'd opened at seven. Coffee at seven ten."). It reads
like the same characters in a slightly different book. P2: "I kept
noticing the restraint. In chapter one I never noticed anything, I just
grinned."

(f) Skim / put-down. The opening is the run's one soft put-down risk,
quoted: "He'd opened at seven. Coffee at seven ten. By eight he'd walked
the yard once with the cup in his hand" — schedule-as-prose, process with
the want not yet in it. P2: "That's a 10 p.m. paragraph. I'd have set the
book on my face right there and trusted myself to find the page tomorrow."
A reader three chapters in survives it; a sample reader might not, and
this chapter's history says respect that. Second skim: the budget preamble,
named in (b). No hard DNF point.

(g) Compliance. No Gateway, no Marisol in Boyd's mouth, no students, no
year numbers, "twelve" as sanctioned. Debits: "Twenty years of Mondays"
states Cal's tenure — not on the fact manifest [CHECK: invented fact;
clear or cut]; "out past the greenhouses" is invented furniture [CHECK:
minor]; Ratchet "he" (run-wide [TK]).

RANKING
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

**B > A > C.** Split: P1 and P4 for B; P2 and P3 for A on first read; P3
flipped to B on reread (her one skim in A was Boyd's fifteen-line trust
monologue; B delivers the identical content broken into turns she never
skimmed). Final 3–1, P2 holding for A on laugh count and conceding the
rag beat is "the thing I'd still remember next morning."

Deciding reasons:

1. **The wrench is the chapter's romance contract, and only B keeps it
   funded the whole way.** In the one chapter without Marisol, B opens on
   the wrench (the clean rag), keeps the bench in frame, and buttons on a
   double-meaning ("Not one word") that ties the romance thread and the
   vise into a single knot. A banks all the wanting at the ending; C funds
   it at open and close but not between. For readers 35–45 sampling a
   romance, B is the candidate where chapter 4 still feels like the book
   they bought.
2. **B continues ch. 1 most invisibly.** Shop-as-organism is
   town-as-organism in the same music; no seams, no reused images. A's
   button echoes ch. 1 nearly verbatim (flagged [CHECK]); C shifts the
   narration's temperature.
3. **B's flaws are deletions; A's and C's are additions.** B needs the
   vise-label paragraph cut and two coasting patches tightened — surgery
   that cannot hurt a working voice. A needs an early wrench beat written
   and an acoustics contradiction resolved; C needs a warmer opening
   written. On a voice-calibrated series, subtraction is the safer knife.
4. **On the FUN meter — the author's primary gauge — A outscores B by raw
   laugh count, but A's two biggest laughs graft into B in minutes** (see
   GRAFTS), while B's structural pleasures (the eavesdropping shop, the
   rag, "Boosters... and the shop sang on") cannot be grafted anywhere.

Rule-1 note (reported, deferred): no safety-wall violation in any
candidate; all three keep the vise inside the ratified shape; no candidate
cheapens the House — all three let Boyd be half right about it, which the
brief demands. The run-wide [TK]s (Ratchet's sex; crew gendering; A's
"deans"; B's "row barns"; C's "twenty years") are continuity-keeper work,
not panel work. Our ranking is pleasure only; it authorizes nothing.

GRAFTS INTO THE WINNER (B)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

From A — the fun deficit repairs:
1. **The golden-hour exchange**, near-verbatim ("These really sing at
   golden hour." / "It's ten in the morning." / "It will not always be,"
   said the sneakers, undefeated) — insert where B's tablet comes up,

2. **Ratchet's exit refusal** ("let him commit fully to the position,
   held it just long enough to establish the record, and then looked at
   the middle distance") — layered before B's finger-guns beat, which

From C — the vise sharpeners:
3. **The safety-meeting question** ("you've been under that porch on a
   work order. Would you hold a safety meeting on it? Put your whole crew
   on those boards, all twelve, and have them jump?") — folded into B's
   "walk it with your own flashlight" speech. It converts B's argument
