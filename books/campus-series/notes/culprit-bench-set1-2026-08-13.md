## 1. What Jo did

Twenty years ago the state came for a strip of the block, and the
county's own appraisals came in low on every parcel but one. A
twenty-five-year-old stringer at the town paper got hold of the
corridor maps and understood, correctly and before anyone else, that
somebody inside the fight had known where the road was going. She had
three days until the hearing, two sources, and one name.

She ran the name. It belonged to the father of the man she was going
to marry.

She knew whose name it was when she filed it. That is the part that
matters, and the part a later drafter will want to soften. Her second
source had a grudge and she knew that too; the third confirmation was
four or five days out and the hearing was Tuesday. What she told
herself in the newsroom that night was the most defensible sentence in
journalism — *I am not going to be the reporter who kills a true story
because of who it embarrasses* — and that righteousness is precisely
what let her skip the call that would have saved everyone. She did not
tell him it was coming. He found out the way the rest of the town did.

She was right about the corruption and wrong about the man. The
appraisals had been rigged; the person who profited was somebody else
entirely. The correction ran three weeks later, on page eight, and it
said the paper "could not substantiate" the claim — which in a town
that size does not mean *he didn't* but *they couldn't prove it*, and
that is the sentence his family lived inside for twenty years. The
holdout bloc, two families negotiating as one, came apart the week the
story ran: the neighbors read it as *he cut a side deal while telling
us to hold*, settled fast and cheap, and have not been in the same
room since. Both families lost land. One family lost the house the son
grew up in, sold to keep the pit open. Nobody died. Nobody was hurt.
The father is still alive, still at the counter, and has never once
been introduced to a stranger in that town without a half-second pause.

Then she left. She has covered four cities and a war since, she is
good at this, and she has never told the story of her own best early
scoop at any dinner party in twenty years.

**The bill, itemized** (this is the version that stays fixed; nothing
later may reduce it).

1. A false accusation of corruption printed under a real man's name.
2. A correction that cleared nothing, three weeks late, on page eight.
3. Silence beforehand — the man she loved learned it from the town.
4. Two families split, one settlement taken in panic, land lost by
   both, and a house sold to keep the business alive.
5. Twenty years of a small town's pause before a name.
6. The leaving, which is the wound the romance itself has to answer.

**HARM-CAP AUDIT (every clause of the rule).**

| Cap | Status | How it clears |
|---|---|---|
| Survivable | PASS | Financial, reputational, relational. No death, no injury, no illness caused. |
| Never kids | PASS | All victims adults. Ray was ~26; his offstage kid is born after 2006 (freshman in Y1 = born ~Y1−18). No child existed to harm. |
| Never sexual | PASS | Nothing sexual anywhere in the 2006 story. |
| Never animals | PASS | No animal is touched by the harm. Ray's porch dog is present-day texture and must never be written as a memorial to it. |
| Never infidelity-for-sympathy | PASS | Jo and Ray were together; no third party, no affair, in 2006 or since. Ray married after she left; Jo's later divorce is ordinary attrition. See the guardrails in §5. |
| Bad enough to anger a reader who meets the victim first | PASS | The reader meets Ray in 1.1 catering tailgates and learns in 1.2 exactly what was done to his father, from the town's own mouth, two books before she is asked to love the woman who did it. |

**Register check.** 1.4 runs at register 5, warm-communal, with the
sweep's flag that the wound is "largely pre-metabolized, one deep
chord." This design honors that: the harm is twenty years cold, the
book is not a grief book, and the whole wound is played at full volume
exactly once — in §4's ruling scene.

## 2. Why this is the right wrong (double duty, not freight)

The sweep's 1.4 midpoint already requires reopening the 2006
eminent-domain story to confirm the covenant. That means the file Jo
must open to save Ray's land now is the file that ruined his family
then — the corridor maps, the appraisal records, the hearing
transcripts. The plant pays local rent twice over. She cannot do her
job in this book without touching her worst work, and there is no
version of the plot where she gets to leave it alone.

More precisely, the 2006 fight and the 2026 one are the same fight
with the same playbook, which is why her old file is evidence and not
just backstory. STANDARDS 23 satisfied on arrival: this plant is the
A-story's engine, not a deposit for a later set.

## 3. The ladder

Three rungs across the gap, plus one optional pre-plant. Each is
specified as a scene the reader sees. Rung 1 is the charge; rungs 2
and 3 are deeds. **No rung contains an apology, from her or about
her.**

### R0 (OPTIONAL) — Book 1.1, fall. The wound, from the victim's side.

*Jo does not appear.* The petition needs the paper's front page, so
somebody from the paper works the tailgate. A card with the masthead on
it gets handed across the smoker to Ray. He reads the name at the top,
hands it back, and goes on turning meat. Later, asked what that was,
Walt gives the town's one flat sentence about what that paper printed
about that man's father, and that it wasn't true.

- **Deed:** none. This rung is the wound.
- **EARNS ITS PAGES:** 1.1's B-story is a fight for the town's
  attention against Boyd's astroturf; the paper is the institution that
  decides whether a petition is news, and the scene shows the petition
  can't count on it. SC1's second test passes on the paper and the pit
  in one beat.
- **Droppable.** If 1.1's outline is full, cut this and the ladder
  still works. Recommended because it puts the victim's anger in the
  reader before the wrongdoer has a face.

### R1 — Book 1.2, winter. The charge sheet.

*Jo is a byline and a recorder in a corridor, nothing more.* The paper
covers Boyd's engineered review of Aisha's calls. The story that runs
is accurate, fair, sourced, and devastating, because a fair account of
a review is itself a weapon. Then the board thread does what boards do
and relitigates 2006 in twelve posts — three facts wrong, one right,
and the right one is that the correction ran on page eight.

- **Deed:** none. This is where the reader learns the crime, from the
  town, in the book with the sharpest teeth.
- **EARNS ITS PAGES:** the thread is 1.2's existing B-story engine
  (S11, the board second-guessing authority in real time). It raises
  the review's stakes by showing what this town's memory does to a
  person the paper names — which is exactly Aisha's fear, and exactly
  why Dan's on-the-record stand costs what it costs.
- **Superior position (Hauge device):** from this chapter forward the
  reader knows 1.4's heroine's crime and 1.4's hero's grievance, two
  books early. That gap is the arc's whole suspense engine.

### R2 — Book 1.3, spring. The deed, witnessed.

Priya and Marcus bring the astroturf invoices to the newsroom on the
night Boyd fast-tracks the demolition survey. Everyone in the room
wants it published tonight. The reader watches Jo do four things.

1. She refuses to run on the leaked documents alone and spends two
   days getting the same numbers from an independent source, with the
   survey clock running and everyone begging.
2. She calls the foundation for comment and prints the denial in full,
   high in the story, where it hurts her own piece.
3. She tells Priya, before Priya decides, precisely what putting her
   name on it will cost — the CFO job, the references, the industry —
   and does not argue either way.
4. She offers to run it without Priya's name, says plainly that the
   story is weaker that way, and lets Priya choose.

- **Deed:** the two days. That is the whole amends and it is paid in
  the only currency she has. The cost lands on the page — the survey
  is filed inside the window, so the story exposes Boyd instead of
  stopping him, which is why 1.4's covenant fight still has to happen.
- **What is forbidden here:** Jo never mentions 2006. Nobody thanks
  her. Nobody defends her. The most the text may do is one line of
  Marcus's POV noticing that he had expected her to be the other
  thing.
- **EARNS ITS PAGES:** 1.3's resolution needs the leak to land as
  evidence Boyd cannot shake. Her care is why the first public exhibit
  enters the stack at admissible grade instead of as rumor (S05).

### R3 — Book 1.4, summer, first half. The deed in her own book.

She is the lead now, so she does not get to be sympathetic on credit.
Two acts, both before anyone forgives her.

**The FOIA under her own name** (the sweep's PONR, ~50%). In 2006 she
stood behind a source. Here she signs the request, which tells the
county, Boyd, and her own paper's owner precisely who is asking, and
hands all three something to use.

**The full accounting** (after the spike is beaten, before the vote).
Not a correction — a front-page reconstruction of what her paper got
wrong in 2006, what the appraisals actually show, and who actually
profited, with the real beneficiary named. It is the story that made
her career, unmade in public, by her.

- **The discipline that keeps this from buying anything:** she does not
  tell Ray it is coming. He reads it like everybody else, off the
  stack, at his own counter. It is published for the town, on the
  town's timetable, because it is the evidence the vote needs. If it
  is staged as a gift to Ray, the forgiveness beat is purchased and the
  arc is dead.
- **EARNS ITS PAGES:** the 2006 file is the covenant's provenance and
  Boyd's playbook exposed. The correction is an exhibit.

## 4. The forgiveness beat — Ray rules, on the page

**Placement:** after the accounting runs, before the council vote.
1.4's one deep chord.

**The transaction is removed in advance.** At the midpoint Ray gives
her his father's 2006 box out of the pit's storeroom — the papers the
family kept because they never stopped meaning to be believed. He
gives it as a citizen to a reporter, and he says out loud that this is
not that. So when the ruling scene arrives there is nothing left to
trade, which is the only condition under which a ruling means
anything.

**He comes to her.** She does not present herself for judgment; he
walks into the newsroom, or catches her on the square at five in the
morning with the press still running, and he does the talking. That
direction matters. The victim rules, and a victim who has been sought
out is being asked.

**What he says, in order.**

1. **The story.** He ruled on that years ago and tells her so — he
   always knew she believed it. It costs him nothing to say, and that
   is the point: the easy line goes first so the reader knows the hard
   ones are real.
2. **The silence.** This is what actually broke them, and this is what
   he forgives out loud, in words, on the page, unambiguously.
3. **His father's line.** Not his to give. His father is alive and can
   speak for himself, and she will have to live with however that
   lands, and with however the neighbors land, forever.
4. **The leaving.** Not forgiven and not withheld out of spite. It
   isn't finished, because she hasn't finished it. She is here on a
   lease and a hope, and he will not rule on the leaving until she has
   stayed through something.

**Why the ruling is built this way.** A blanket absolution ends the
book at 85% and flatters her; a refusal breaks the HEA promise. This
grants a real, spoken forgiveness for a named wrong, keeps the
un-grantable parts un-granted, and hands the romance back its own
obstacle — staying, which is SC3's whole subject and which the HEA
must then pay for honestly. The redemption arc closes; the love story
does not close early.

## 5. Never retconned softer — the guardrail list

These are the specific softenings a later drafter will reach for.
Each is forbidden by name.

1. **"She didn't know whose father it was."** Banned. She knew when
   she filed. Every version where she finds out at the printer is a
   different, worse arc.
2. **"There was something to it."** Banned. The accusation was wholly
   false as to that man. Partial truth is the softening drift, and it
   would also break the plot — the 2006 file only proves the covenant
   if the real beneficiary is somebody else.
3. **"Her editor made her run it."** The pressure existed and may be
   named exactly once, by someone other than Jo, and Jo declines it.
   It never operates as a defense in narration or dialogue.
4. **Enlarging the correction in hindsight.** Page eight, three weeks
   late, "could not substantiate." Fixed on ratification. Nobody
   later remembers it as bigger or kinder.
5. **Softening 1.2's thread.** The board may get facts wrong in Jo's
   favor; the narration never confirms them. No sympathetic poster
   supplies mitigation. The one true fact the thread lands on stays.
6. **Editorializing 1.3's deed.** Nobody in 1.3 says "she's not what
   they say about her." The deed is witnessed and left alone.
7. **A mellowed Ray in 1.1.** He hands the card back. A victim who has
   mostly let it go by book one makes the ruling in book four worth
   nothing.
8. **Writing the other victims out.** The father stays alive, in town,
   and unreconciled unless the author rules otherwise; the neighbor
   family stays in the story. The debt is not reduced by attrition.
9. **Death-adjacent escalation.** No suicide attempt, no stress-death,
   no "it's what killed his mother." The cap is survivable and it is
   not negotiable upward for drama.
10. **Infidelity for sympathy, in any direction.** Jo's ex is not a
    cheater or an abuser whose betrayal earns her the reader's pity;
    Ray's late wife is a real marriage handled with weight (standard
    14), never a rebound or a placeholder; nobody in 2006 was
    unfaithful.
11. **The dog.** The 2006 harm never touched an animal, and Ray's
    porch dog is not a memorial to it.
12. **Post-hoc revision.** Once 1.2 and 1.3 are accepted, their text is
    not reopened while 1.4 drafts. Any pressure to do so is a finding
    against 1.4, not a fix to 1.2.

## 6. The town's re-acceptance, in a town with a long memory

Re-acceptance is a component of the HEA, so it has to be visible — and
in a town like this it is never a scene where everyone claps. Four
graded, concrete forms, in rising order of what they cost the town.

**The masthead** (RECOMMENDED, and it answers the sweep's open `[TK]`
on the spiked-story escape). The Boyd-adjacent owner spikes the story;
the town buys the paper out from under him. A subscription-and-shares
drive run door to door on the same lists, by the same machinery, as
the petition — the parent board's mailing list, Ray's catering
customers, the neighbors who knock. The town's re-acceptance is
therefore literally measurable: they put their money on her byline
before they knew how the vote would go. *Alternative honestly costed:*
a charter loophole in the paper's ownership documents. It is legally
tidier and emotionally worth nothing, it wins no HEA component, and it
sits one inch from luck. Recommend the masthead.

**The neighbors.** The 2006 bloc's other family ([TK name]) stands at
public comment and says the block should have held in 2006 and will
hold this time. They do not address Jo and do not have to. The town
re-accepting the *fact* is the precondition for re-accepting her.

**The elder.** Ray's father does not forgive her and is not required
to. He lets her buy a plate and he carries it out himself. Deed, not
words, the same rule the culprit lives under, applied back at her.
That is the honest ceiling in a town with a memory, and it satisfies
the one-elder-per-set craft note in SUPERCONCEPTS' dispositions.

**One permanent refusal.** At least one named townsperson never comes
back around, on the page, without a redemption of their own. Majority,
not unanimous. That is what makes it a town.

**Ray's climax act, which is also the re-acceptance's hinge.** The
vote is won at the doors, per the locked set resolution; what Ray does
is stand at public comment and say the 2006 thing out loud — the thing
he has refused to say for twenty years — using the only power he has
ever had in this town, which is that everybody's father knew him. He
is not testifying for Jo. He is testifying that the playbook is the
same playbook, which is what turns her accounting from a reporter's
claim into the town's own memory. His established strength (he
stayed; he feeds them) is what wins the room.

## 9. The fired coach — calibration, and the door closed

Dan Merritt's predecessor `[TK name]` was fired before Book 1.1 opens
for years of rage-culture abuse of **adult staff**. He is backstory. He
is not a bench candidate and never becomes one.

**What he did, and to whom.** Every documented act has an employed
adult as its victim: assistants and coordinators screamed at in front
of their own staff, a director of football operations fired by
voicemail for a scheduling error, an equipment manager kept at work
through a parent's funeral, and — the one that matters structurally —
an athletic trainer fired for declining to change what he had written
in a report. The physical ceiling is property: a thrown headset, and a
hole in a staff-hallway door that was patched and repainted in a
colour that never quite matched.

**Where the line sits, in one sentence a drafter can apply.** *The
victim of every act is an adult employee; the danger to players is the
reason the reform passed, never a thing that happened.* On this side:
humiliation, firings, ruined careers, coerced paperwork, a culture of
fear, damaged property. Across the line, banned outright: any player
berated, benched, injured, played hurt, cleared early, cut, stripped
of aid, or written into an altered record; any implication that an
athlete was harmed and it was covered up; anything sexual; anything
touching a staff member's children. The grey band a drafter will drift
into is "he made the trainer clear a kid who wasn't ready." The ruling
is that he **fired the trainer who wouldn't** — and the university
acted because everyone could see what the next step was going to be.

**How it is referred to on the page** — three registers only, and he
never speaks.

1. **Institutional language.** The conduct clause in every coaching
   contract, the climate review, the anonymous reporting line, and the
   phrase the department uses for the era `[TK]`.
2. **Artifact.** The mismatched paint on the hallway door; the hotline
   poster still taped up beside it.
3. **Survivors' shorthand.** Staff who were there don't use his name;
   they say *before* and *after* ("she's been here since before").

No flashback, no dramatization, no phone call, no letter, no cameo,
in any book of any set. He is employed again somewhere else and doing
fine, which is the honest and un-redemptive answer and costs zero
pages.

**What the firing buys the books — this is why it stays.**

- **Aisha's authority exists because of it.** The review's central
  recommendation was that the head team physician report to the
  university, not to the coach, with unilateral return-to-play
  authority. 1.2's entire load-bearing obstacle is therefore a direct
  structural consequence of the predecessor's abuse, which upgrades
  backstory into SC4 machinery.
- **It is Boyd's lever.** The reform's anonymous reporting line is the
  instrument. Boyd does not trigger a formal review personally; he
  supplies complaints through the same astroturf method 1.1 has
  already established on the boards, plus money and board pressure.
  The machinery built to let the powerless report a bully is being
  worked, anonymously, by the most powerful man in town. That is 1.2's
  dark moment made mechanical rather than atmospheric.
- **Guardrail on that lever.** The reform is good and the book says so
  — Aisha's independence is the reform working, and the escape is
  earned by her charting discipline and Dan's on-the-record testimony,
  which is the same machinery used correctly. The abuse is of the
  tool, never an argument against it.
- **Dan's rigidity** is over-determined and should stay that way: his
  own fraternization scandal is the personal reason, the conduct
  clause he signed is the institutional one.

**Why he is not a bench candidate** — write this into the bible so a
later set cannot reopen it.

1. Set 1's one arc is Jo. A second is a violation on its face.
2. His harm is a pattern over years against many victims, not one
   survivable act; there is no single victim who could rule.
3. The rule requires the victim's on-page ruling, which here would
   mean assembling a dozen people to rule — a different and worse
   book.
4. Redeeming him would retroactively soften the reform that 1.2's
   whole obstacle structure stands on. The arc would attack a
   superconcept to buy a subplot.
5. Standing rule proposed: **the predecessor never appears on the page
   in any book, in any set.**

## 12. Decisions for the author (each its own PR)

1. **Ratify Jo as Set 1's bench with the §1 bill as written** — most
   importantly, that she knew whose father it was when she filed.
2. **Ray's father: alive or dead.** This note recommends alive, in
   town, at the counter, and never granting forgiveness. If dead, the
   never-a-stress-death guardrail (§5.9) must be stated even harder,
   and §6 loses its best re-acceptance beat.
3. **The 2006 date versus the timeline.** The sweep says "2006"; the
   cast doc says "twenty years ago." Together they silently anchor
