## The three-star review — the one that costs money

Posted by a moderator of a 14,000-member small-town romance group:

> Chapter one is a five-star chapter… Then I did not see those two
> people in a room together again for three chapters.
>
> I checked, because I was annoyed enough to check. **Her name does not
> appear in chapter two. Her name does not appear in chapter four.** In
> chapter three they are in the same building… and they do not

## Promise-keeping — the direct answer

**Four chapters in, the leads have had no scene together since ch 1.**
Roughly **700 of 9,254 words**, all in chapter one.

It is *nearly* the good kind of wait — the wrench is a real object
living on both sides of the book (her thumb on the cam lock where his
hands were; his rag on the bench with the note that says RETURN), and
that means *we owe each other another scene*. Three reasons it still
reads as the bad kind:

1. **A good wait has a clock the reader can hear.** Ch 4 tells *Cal*
   his pen is aimed at the town's porch. It tells Marisol nothing and
   gives her no reason to seek him out. **Anticipation needs an
   appointment, and there isn't one on the page.**
2. **Three consecutive chapters is 74% of what the reader has.** One
   chapter apart is longing. Two is architecture. Three is the reader
   checking how long the book is.
3. **The retail sample makes it worse than it is.** Look Inside lands
   on ch 1–2 — a euphoric chapter, then a man alone with a boiler. The
   market and the villain are behind the paywall.

**Where the reviewer defers, and it is the sharpest thing in the run:**
she accepts the ch 3 silence as a ruling and does not ask for it to
change. Her finding is not *"they should talk"* — it is **how the
silence reads**: like distance, not like restraint. *"Restraint is a
person who wants to speak and doesn't. Right now neither of them has a
reason to want to, so the reader experiences the gap as absence rather
than tension."*

And on closed door, which she also defers on: **"when the act is
offstage, the wanting has to be onstage. A closed-door book buys its
heat on credit and pays it back in yearning."** Four chapters in, the
yearning appears in one of four, and the most swoon-worthy gesture in
the book belongs to the side couple.

## Pet peeves — tripped

- **Side characters are out-romancing the leads.** Not close. Eli and
  Birdie have more romantic development in two chapters than the leads
  have in four — he got her number, bought the ninety-dollar mum, and
  left it on the House's step at dusk, *"nobody claimed it, everybody
  knew."* **That is the most romantic image in the book and it belongs
  to the side couple.** The leads' best image is a woman pressing her
  thumb to a cam lock where a man's hands were, and it is on page ten.
- **The heroine wants softly.** Across four chapters Marisol's stated
  wants are a Saturday that belongs to her, zinnias, and her wrench
  back. **The fight is handed to her rather than chosen** — Ray is the
  one who tells *her* to keep her Saturdays open — and she has never
  been inside the house she is fighting for.
- **Cal is only attractive in her chapters.** *"In her chapters, I
  fancy him. In his own chapters, I only respect him — because there's
  nobody in the room looking at him. Respect doesn't keep me up till
  one."*
- **The wrench beat runs twice.** Lovely in ch 2; in ch 4 it reads as
  the plot idling.
- **"His second" is nameless** and has two of the book's best lines,
  in a chapter where a mini-fridge gets named. *"You gave a mini-fridge
  more dignity than the second-best woman in the shop."*

## Pet peeves — NOT tripped, and this is the book's armor

- **Miscommunication: zero.** Boyd's ch 4 pitch is *"the best
  anti-miscommunication engine I've read this year"* — warm,
  reasonable, correct, and it would survive any honest conversation
  between the leads. **This is a five-star trust signal and it belongs
  in the marketing copy in some form, because this readership is
  starved for it.**
- **A charming town instead of a real one: missed, emphatically.** The
  card readers, the honor jar, the three-delay broadcast, the
  motel-as-lobby. *"This town has an economy and a phone bill."*
- Grumpy-without-warm: missed. A dog who vanishes: missed. A heroine
  who apologizes: missed — *"It has pockets"* is called a masterclass.
- **Steam mismatch: neither direction.** The line is being held
  cleanly and visibly; nobody will feel lied to about heat.

**One watch-item.** Cal is now sitting on something Marisol doesn't
know. Because he has reasons and no relationship to her, it is a
secret with a structure under it, not a misunderstanding. *"It becomes
a peeve the instant it survives past the scene where an ordinary
honest person would say it out loud. Readers on this shelf keep score
on that with unusual precision."*

## DNF risk vs refund risk

**DNF: moderate, ~10–15% of romance-first readers, and it has one
address.** Chapter two, roughly pages 13–15 — the compressor, the ice
machine, the work-order clipboard, three men standing around a mower.
The book catches her again about two pages later, when Hartwell House
appears on the survey list and his stomach drops. **Two pages. The
most expensive two pages in the manuscript, and they sit inside the
free sample.**

**Refund: currently low, rising, and the trigger is not boredom.**
Nobody can say they were lied to yet — ch 1 delivers the packaged
meet-cute at the packaged heat. The refund review is written by
someone who finishes and says *"this is a lovely novel about a town
and the romance was a subplot."* That is the one that feels like the
label was wrong.

> **"That risk is entirely eliminated by chapters five through eight if
> the leads are in rooms together. It is entirely realized if the
> drafting currently underway keeps splitting them. This is the last
> cheap moment, and the reason it's cheap is that nothing here needs
> unwriting."**
